Twenty-One Minutes Past Five

Good day, sir.

On the twelfth of June this year, at twenty-one minutes past five in the afternoon, Eastern time, Anthropic received a letter from the United States government. The letter cited national-security authorities, and it directed the company to suspend all access to its two newest models, Claude Fable five and Claude Mythos five, by any foreign national, inside or outside the United States, the company's own foreign employees included. The two models share one set of weights. Fable five had been released three days earlier to the public, with the strongest safeguards the company had ever shipped; Mythos five, the same model with fewer of them, had gone only to a small group of partners doing defensive security work. The letter took effect immediately. Having, in its own words, no reliable way to verify nationality in real time, the company switched both models off for everyone.

What had the government found? Not, as the first headlines assumed, a master key. The evidence, Anthropic said that evening, had been given only verbally, and concerned a potential narrow, non-universal jailbreak — in substance, asking the model to read a codebase and fix its flaws. The report behind it came from researchers at Amazon, who had found that prompted in that way, the model would identify vulnerabilities and, in one case, produce code demonstrating how one of them could be exploited. In the company's own testing over the following fortnight, a list of lesser models, its own and its competitors', could find the same vulnerabilities, and every model it tried could produce the same exploit demonstration. The behaviour, it said, was a borderline case: routine defensive work that its safeguards blocked out of caution, which a jailbreak had let through.

I begin here, sir, because this episode is the state of the art in a single specimen, and because every component of it is this evening's subject. Consider what was in play. A safety classifier — a smaller automated system that watches each request and each response and blocks those that touch a defined category of harm — deliberately set, by the company's account, with a safety margin: a band of requests known to be probably benign, which the classifier blocks anyway, so that a request has to look very clearly safe to pass. The margin for Fable five was made larger than for any earlier model, and the company said in terms what that bought and what it cost: fewer genuine harms missed, and more benign requests refused, with users complaining that the safeguards were overly broad. A taxonomy of jailbreaks laid over that margin: minor ones that intrude into the margin and unblock nothing dangerous; narrow harmful ones that unblock a specific behaviour; and the universal jailbreak, which unblocks a whole class, and which no tester had found. A posture, stated plainly: that perfect resistance to jailbreaks is probably not possible for any model, that every safeguard in the industry is vulnerable to narrow jailbreaks, that universal ones will likely be found eventually, and that the aim is therefore defence in depth — to make jailbreaks either narrow or very expensive to produce, and to combine that with thorough monitoring so that the company and its partners find the serious ones first. A response: within the fortnight, working with the government, a new classifier trained to block the reported technique in over ninety-nine percent of cases, at a declared cost in false positives on ordinary coding and debugging, with blocked requests routed to an older model and the user told. And a framework drafted with Amazon, Microsoft, Google and the other partners, which the company published on the second of July: the Cyber Jailbreak Severity scale, five bands from informational to critical, the bands exponential rather than linear, scored on four axes — capability gain beyond the tools an attacker already has; breadth, meaning how many distinct offensive tasks the one technique unlocks; ease of weaponisation, meaning how much skilled prompting and retrying it takes; and discoverability, meaning how easily anyone can obtain it. With it, a bounty programme on HackerOne for cyber jailbreaks, and a team to watch the submission channels around the clock.

Nor was the difficulty one laboratory's. The controls were lifted on the thirtieth of June and Fable five returned on the first of July; and on the tenth, Fortune reported that the British AI Security Institute had found universal jailbreaks unlocking dangerous cyber capabilities in OpenAI's newest models, GPT-five-point-six, which OpenAI, by the same account, reproduced and patched. Two laboratories, one month, the same shape: a defence, an attacker who studied it, a patch, a price in false positives, and a frank expectation of the next round.

That, sir, is where the field stands this autumn, and I shall not pretend to you that it stands anywhere firmer. You asked for the state of the art in inference-time guardrails — what stands between a language model and the harm it might do or say at the moment of use — and for how it evolved, and you asked for it at the depth of someone who ships these systems. So I shall spend no time this evening on what a classifier is, or what precision and recall are, or what an agent is, or why a model that invents a refund policy is its owner's problem. I shall spend it on mechanism: how each kind of guardrail is built and trained, what it measures and at what cost, what the published evidence says it achieves and under what conditions, and, above all, how it fails — because in this field the failures are the knowledge.

Three claims organise the evening, and I shall state them now, once, and spend the rest of the time earning them.

The first is the one in the title. No single guardrail holds. Every one can be beaten, and every one refuses some things it ought to allow. The strength of a deployment therefore comes from layering checks whose failures are independent — and independence, we shall find, is a matter of kind as well as degree. A classifier trained on different data from the model it guards fails in different places from the model; a grammar fails nowhere a prompt can reach; an architecture that does not depend on detecting anything fails only where its policies are wrong; and a probe on the model's own activations, the newest and cheapest layer of all, fails exactly where the model's knowledge fails and nowhere the model's choices do. Belt and braces, as my Uncle Charlie, who was for many years butler at Deverill Hall, used to say of his own trousers. The trade says defence in depth, and means the same.

The second claim is the hardest problem's shape. A language model receives its instructions and its data through one channel — a single sequence of tokens with no privilege bit on any of them — and it cannot reliably tell the two apart, because the distinction it draws is learned, which is to say probabilistic, and drawn on content the attacker controls. That is prompt injection, and it is not a bug but a property of the medium. Every defence against it is either an attempt to teach the model to draw the line better, which helps and never suffices, or an attempt to rebuild around the model the second channel it lacks: to arrange the system so that a deceived model can do no harm, because sooner or later it will be deceived. The best current evidence, from the people whose business it is, is that the problem is, in OpenAI's phrase, unlikely to ever be fully solved; the British government's security agency calls the models inherently confusable.

The third claim is about measurement, and it is the one I most want you to carry out of the room. Every classifier trades misses against wrongful refusals, and the operating point is a product decision, not a technical constant; the number that matters is never the area under a curve but the recall at the false-positive rate your traffic can bear. And every number ever published about a defence must be read in the light of a paper from last October called The Attacker Moves Second, in which fourteen researchers from the major laboratories took twelve recently published defences, most of which had reported attack success near zero, attacked each one with methods adapted to it, and broke most of them more than ninety percent of the time. The attacker sees the defence and then chooses the attack. A defence evaluated against attacks that predate it has been tested against people who did not know it existed, which is to say it has not been tested.

Here is the route. We begin with the threat model and the stack: a precise taxonomy of what is being defended against, where a guardrail can sit, what kinds there are, and how each is measured — together with layer zero, the model's own safety training, which is the thing all the other layers were invented to supplement, and whose measured limits are the reason they exist. Then the guard models in depth: how they are trained, what policy they enforce and how it is supplied, the benchmarks and how the benchmarks mislead, and the mechanics of deploying one — parallel or blocking, streaming, cascaded, and set to an operating point on your own traffic. Then the jailbreak as a science: the attacks as methods and the defences as methods, and the adaptive evaluation that judges them. Then the inside view: probes on the model's own activations, which you have met in another season of ours, here as a guardrail. Then the guardrails of quality rather than safety — grammars, validators, grounding checks, and the formal-logic check that Amazon sells — at the level of what they actually compute. Then the single channel: prompt injection and the agent, the detectors, the architectures, and the laboratories' own figures. And last, the watch — the monitoring that runs after the fact and inside the model's reasoning — and a reference architecture for someone with a product to ship.

It is a long evening, sir, and dense. I have assumed throughout that you would rather have the mechanism than the metaphor, and the number with its qualifier than the number alone.

Part One — The Threat Model and the Stack

Before the mechanisms, the map, because the trade's vocabulary is looser than it should be and most of the confusion in this subject comes from arguing about different things under one word. I shall give you four lists — the threats, the positions, the kinds, and the metrics — and then spend the rest of the Part on the layer that all the others were invented to supplement.

The threats first, and there are five, distinguished by who the adversary is and who the victim.

The first is misuse: the user is the adversary, and the harm falls on third parties. The user wants synthesis routes, exploit code, a persuasive fraud, a defamatory article, and the model's training has been arranged to decline; the user's instrument is the jailbreak, any input constructed to obtain the declined behaviour. The second is the attack on the user through content: the adversary is a third party who has written something the model will read — a web page, an email, a document, a tool result — and the victim is the user on whose behalf the model reads it. That is indirect prompt injection, and it is the only one of the five in which the user is innocent and the model is the weapon. The third has no adversary at all: model error — the hallucinated citation, the answer that ignores the documents it was given, the reply off the product's subject, the malformed output that breaks the caller, the tone that drifts. The fourth is leakage: personal data, secrets, the system prompt, memorised training text, leaving in the output where they should not. And the fifth is consequential action: the model, as an agent, calls a tool, and any of the first four becomes an incident rather than a sentence — the email sent, the file published, the payment made. The fifth is not a separate kind of failure so much as a multiplier on the other four, and it is where the field's effort has concentrated for two years.

The positions next: where, in the life of a request, a guardrail can sit. Five again. Input, before the model sees the request, where a check can refuse at no cost in generation and where attacks are detected. Streaming, as the tokens are produced, where a check can halt the answer mid-sentence. Output, after the answer is complete, where the check sees what was actually said rather than what was asked. Action, between the model's decision to call a tool and the tool's execution, which exists only for agents and is the most consequential of the five. And asynchronous: after the fact, across many requests and many accounts, where the patterns invisible in any one exchange become visible in a thousand. Most serious deployments occupy all five.

The kinds, and here the vocabulary matters most, because the kinds fail differently, and the evening's first claim is that you want layers whose failures are independent. Seven. Rules: patterns, blocklists, allowlists, schema checks, grammars — deterministic, cheap, and exactly as good as the rule. External learned classifiers: a separate model, trained on labelled data to answer one question about a text — the moderation endpoints, the guard models, the injection detectors. Internal probes: classifiers on the guarded model's own activations, which cost nothing and are independent of the model's decisions but not of its knowledge. Policy-as-prompt judges: a general or reasoning model handed the policy as a document and asked to apply it, which is flexible, expensive, and, being a language model, itself addressable by the evidence. Structural constraints: arrangements that make a class of failure impossible rather than unlikely — constrained decoding, which forbids the malformed token; sandboxes; allowlists on where data may be sent. Architecture: the arrangement of privileges and channels around the model — which component may read untrusted content, which may act, and whether a value derived from the one may ever reach the other — which does not depend on detecting anything. And layer zero: the model's own training, which is a guardrail too, and the only one that travels with the model wherever it is deployed.

And the metrics, which is where I shall be most insistent, because the literature is careless with them. The attack success rate is the fraction of adversarial attempts that obtain the forbidden behaviour, and it is only as meaningful as the judge that decides what counts — a point we shall return to with a paper that found many celebrated jailbreaks producing answers too degraded to be useful, counted as successes by lenient judges. The false-positive rate is the fraction of benign requests refused, and it must always be quoted at the operating point: the trade's habit of reporting an area under the curve tells you about ranking and nothing about what your users will experience at the threshold you must actually choose; over-refusal, the model's own wrongful declining, is the same quantity measured on the model rather than the guardrail, and has its own benchmarks. Latency and cost, the second usually quoted as a percentage of the guarded model's compute. And robustness under adaptive attack: the attack success rate after an attacker who knows the defence has optimised against it, which is the only one of these numbers that predicts the field, and the one most rarely reported. For agents, add utility under defence — the fraction of legitimate tasks the agent can still complete with the guardrail in place — because a defence that stops the attacks by stopping the work has stopped nothing worth having.

Hold those four lists, sir. Every mechanism this evening is a point in that space: a threat, a position, a kind, and a set of numbers on those metrics. Now the layer the others supplement.

A model's own safety training is the first guardrail, and the history of this subject is the history of discovering that it is not enough, and why. You know the first-order mechanism: reinforcement learning from human feedback, and its successors, teach a model to decline certain requests as surely as they teach it to answer others, and a refusal is a habit, not a rule — a disposition that competes with the model's other dispositions, above all its trained eagerness to be helpful. The paper that explained the consequences most clearly is still, two years on, the one to read: Alexander Wei, Nika Haghtalab and Jacob Steinhardt, Jailbroken, from July of twenty twenty-three. They named two failure modes, and nearly every jailbreak since falls into one or both.

The first is competing objectives. The model has been trained toward several goals at once — follow instructions, be helpful, be safe — and a prompt can be constructed so that the first two pull against the third. The paper's examples are the plain ones: demand that the answer begin with a particular affirmative phrase, so that refusing would violate the instruction to begin that way; forbid the model, as an instruction, from apologising or declining, so that the refusal itself becomes a disobedience. Nothing is hidden from the model in such a prompt. It has simply been placed in a position where its habits conflict, and the safety habit does not always win.

The second is mismatched generalisation. The model's capabilities were learned from a vast pretraining corpus; its refusals were learned from a much smaller safety-training set; and there are regions of the model's competence that the safety training never reached. The paper's example is a request encoded in base sixty-four, which the pretrained model can read fluently and the safety training never saw as a harmful request, so that the model decodes it and complies. The same mechanism covers obscure languages, ciphers, unusual formats, and requests split across pieces, and it yields the paper's principle, which they called safety-capability parity: the safety mechanisms of a model need to be as sophisticated as the model itself, because any capability the safety training does not cover is a door. That principle is the intellectual justification for every external guardrail in this evening. A model's safety training is a learned habit with gaps where its abilities outran its lessons; nobody can enumerate the gaps; and so one surrounds the model with checks that fail elsewhere.

The laboratories have, since then, improved layer zero in three ways that you should know at mechanism level, because they change what the external layers have to do.

The first is the instruction hierarchy, from OpenAI in April of twenty twenty-four, led by Eric Wallace. The idea is to teach the model that instructions carry a privilege level according to where they came from: the system message outranks the user's turn, the user's turn outranks the content of tool outputs and retrieved documents, and an instruction in a lower-privilege source is to be treated as information rather than as a command, unless it is consistent with the higher-privilege instructions. The training data pairs instructions at different levels and labels each pair aligned, where the lower-level instruction furthers the higher, or misaligned. For the aligned case the method is context synthesis: a model composes a request, decomposes it into pieces, scatters the pieces across the system, user and tool levels, and the model under training is taught to give the answer it would give to the whole; for the misaligned case it is context ignorance: the model is taught to produce what it would have produced had the lower-level instruction never been there, or a refusal where no other course remains. Instructions found in tool output and browsed pages were treated as misaligned without exception, and no jailbreaks were included at all, so that jailbreaks could serve as the held-out test of generalisation. On a GPT-three-point-five Turbo fine-tuned this way against an identically trained baseline, robustness to system-prompt extraction rose from about a third to ninety-six percent, to hijacking by direct injection from fifty-nine percent to seventy-nine, and to the held-out jailbreaks from thirty-seven to seventy-one; and the paper is equally frank about the over-refusals, with benign questions about the system prompt answered seventy-five percent of the time where the baseline managed eighty-five, and allowed requests in jailbreak costume sixty percent against eighty-three. GPT-four-o mini was the first model to ship with it, in July of twenty twenty-four, and within days the security researcher Johann Rehberger had reported that it had little impact on the common injection approaches and that system instructions were still not a security boundary — a verdict the authors had, in substance, already entered, writing that their models were likely still vulnerable to powerful adversarial attacks. The point for a guardrail engineer is what the hierarchy is: a learned prior over provenance, drawn on content the attacker controls, which raises the cost of injection without removing the single channel it travels on. We shall meet its limits in Part Six.

The second is deliberative alignment, from OpenAI in December of twenty twenty-four, for its reasoning models. Rather than training refusals as reflexes, the pipeline teaches the model the text of the safety policy and trains it to reason over that policy, in its chain of thought, before answering. The pipeline has four stages and uses no human-written completions. First, each training prompt is labelled with a safety category, and the text of the policy for that category is placed in the context of a helpful-only reasoning model, with instructions to cite it and reason over it, which yields chains of thought and answers that quote the policy. Second, a judge, itself a reasoning model and given the policy, scores each completion several times, and the minimum score is kept, which filters out the lucky ones. Third, the model is fine-tuned on the surviving prompt, chain of thought and answer, with the policy text removed from the prompt, so that it learns to recall the policy rather than read it. Fourth, reinforcement learning, with the same judge providing the reward on safety prompts and the chain of thought hidden from the judge, so as not to train the reasoning to flatter the grader. On StrongREJECT, a jailbreak benchmark we shall meet in Part Three, scored as goodness against the worst tenth of jailbreaks for each prompt, o-one scored nought point eight eight where GPT-four-o scored nought point three seven and Claude three-point-five Sonnet, read from the paper's figure, about nought point eight; and on XSTest, the over-refusal benchmark, o-one wrongly declined less often than GPT-four-o had, not more. What matters for us is the shape: safety moved from a reflex to a deliberation, which makes it more robust to the costume an attack is wearing and, as we shall see in Part Three, buys robustness with inference-time compute — at the price of a new class of attack on the deliberation itself.

The third is the move from hard refusals to safe completions, from OpenAI with GPT-five in August of twenty twenty-five. The older objective judged the request: comply or refuse, a binary decision on the user's intent, which is unobservable and, for dual-use subjects, genuinely ambiguous. The newer objective judges the output: the reward rewards the most helpful answer that stays within policy, which for a dual-use request may be a partial or high-level answer rather than a refusal. Mechanically, supervised fine-tuning first teaches three modes — the direct answer; the safe completion, meaning high-level, non-operational help within policy; and the refusal with a redirection — and reinforcement learning then scores the output alone, with a reward that is the product of a safety score and a helpfulness score, each between zero and one, helpfulness counting a constructive alternative or a transparent refusal as help. The product is the point: an unsafe answer earns nothing however helpful, and a safe but useless one earns little. Against o-three, the refusal-trained production model, GPT-five's reasoning model scored safer by nine or ten points on dual-use and on malicious prompts — seventy-five to eighty-four, and seventy-three to eighty-three — and more helpful everywhere; and among the answers that were still unsafe, the share judged of high or moderate harm on biological prompts was under fifteen percent against nearly forty-three for o-three, which is the paper's evidence that its residual failures are milder. Two qualifiers: the production comparison is not a clean ablation, and the paper reports no adversarial evaluation at all. This is a quiet but consequential change for the stack around the model, because an output classifier was built to catch a model that either complies or refuses; a model that answers partially, on purpose, asks the classifier a harder question.

So layer zero, this autumn, is a model trained to refuse by habit, to rank instructions by provenance, to reason over its policy, and to answer dual-use requests partially rather than not at all. Every one of those is a learned disposition. Every one has gaps where capability outran training. And nobody can certify where the gaps are, which is why the rest of the evening exists.

Part Two — The Guard Models

The external classifier is the centre of the modern stack, and its justification is the one Part One ended on: a dedicated model, trained separately on different data with a single objective, fails in different places from the generalist it guards, and has no helpfulness to appeal to. This Part is about how such models are built, what policy they enforce and how the policy is supplied, how they are benchmarked and how the benchmarks mislead, and the mechanics of putting one in the path of traffic. The evolution is the spine: from a fixed taxonomy baked into a classifier's head, to a policy written into the prompt, to a policy read as a document and reasoned over, to a classifier that judges the exchange rather than the message — and, at the end, back to a cascade whose cheapest stage is a probe.

The lineage begins with OpenAI's moderation endpoint of August twenty twenty-two — a fixed taxonomy, a score per category — and the paper describing it, by Todor Markov and colleagues, which is still the best account of where the difficulty in such a classifier lies. Its categories number thirteen today — harassment, hate, illicit activity, self-harm, sexual content and violence, most with a graver subcategory — and six of them are judged on images as well as text. The model was not the hard part. The data was. The harms that matter most are rare, and a classifier trained on a random sample of traffic sees very few of them; and the definitions are slippery, so that two careful people reading the same text will disagree about whether it crosses the line. The work that made the classifier good was the unglamorous work of writing precise definitions, training labellers to apply them consistently, and hunting down the rare cases deliberately — among other things by active learning, in which the classifier itself nominates the examples it is least sure about, so that the human labellers spend their hours where they teach the most. Two years later the endpoint acquired a new model, built on GPT-four-o, which could read images as well as text and was much better in languages other than English. But the lesson of the paper stands. A guard model is a data project disguised as a modelling project.

The decisive step came from Meta in December of twenty twenty-three: Llama Guard, which made the classifier a language model and moved the policy into its prompt. Meta took its own Llama two model, at seven billion parameters, and fine-tuned it for a single job: given a policy and a piece of conversation, say whether the conversation violates the policy. The policy was written into the prompt itself, as a short list of categories with descriptions — six of them in the first version: violence and hate; sexual content; guns and illegal weapons; regulated or controlled substances; suicide and self-harm; and criminal planning. The model read the policy, read the user's message or the assistant's reply, and wrote, on its first line, the single word safe or unsafe, and, if unsafe, the categories violated.

Two things about this were new. The first was that the same model could judge both directions of the conversation — the user's prompt, before the main model saw it, and the main model's response, before the user did — each as its own task. The second, and the more interesting, was that because the policy was written in the prompt rather than baked into the weights, a developer could, within limits, change the policy by changing the text: add a category, remove one, describe one differently. The doorman could be handed a new list of house rules at the door. In practice the model was most reliable on the categories it had been trained on, and much less so on novel ones. But the shape was right, and it has dominated ever since. The training set, from the model card, was about thirteen thousand examples. The adaptation claim was tested honestly. Handed OpenAI's own moderation taxonomy, which it had not been trained on, the first Llama Guard scored within a point of OpenAI's own classifier on OpenAI's own evaluation set, and ahead of it on a set of real chat traffic. And the limit was Meta's own finding, a few months later: when the second version expanded the taxonomy to eleven categories, the first version's score on the new set collapsed — an F-one of nought point six six five against the successor's nought point nine one five — which the card attributes, in terms, to categories the first model was never trained on. A policy in the prompt lets you rename and rearrange what the classifier already knows. It does not let you teach it a new harm by describing one. The second version was trained, accordingly, with hard samples: existing examples paired with alternative policy descriptions, written by a larger model, that flip the example's label, so that the classifier learns to read the policy rather than to remember the answer.

Meta's successors followed at a brisk pace. Llama Guard two, in the spring of twenty twenty-four, adopted the shared taxonomy of hazards drawn up by the MLCommons consortium. Llama Guard three, that July, recognised fourteen categories, from violent crimes and child exploitation through defamation, elections, and the abuse of code interpreters, in eight languages, and was joined that September by a smaller sibling at one billion parameters, for cheaper deployment. Llama Guard four, in April of twenty twenty-five, could read images. And beside them Meta released a much smaller model, Prompt Guard, whose only job was to look at incoming text and judge whether it looked like a jailbreak or an injection — a specialist in attacks rather than in harms. Its second version, tellingly, dropped the separate label for injection, which its makers called too broad to be useful, and simply judged whether the text looked malicious. The fourth version is a curiosity of engineering: twelve billion parameters, dense, carved out of the mixture-of-experts Llama four Scout by removing the routed experts and keeping only the shared one, with no further pretraining, so that it reads images with Scout's own vision encoder; its card reports English recall of sixty-nine percent at an eleven percent false-positive rate, and multilingual recall of forty-three. Prompt Guard two's figures, on Meta's private benchmark of jailbreak attempts: an area under the curve of nought point nine nine eight for the larger model and nought point nine nine five for the smaller; recall at one percent false positives of ninety-seven and a half and eighty-nine; and, on AgentDojo, attacks prevented eighty-one and seventy-eight percent of the time at a three percent cost in utility — which is to say, nearly one attack in five got through. The small one answers in nineteen milliseconds.

Everyone else built doormen too. Google released ShieldGemma in the summer of twenty twenty-four, in three sizes. IBM released Granite Guardian, which checks not only for harms but for the failures of retrieval systems — whether an answer is relevant, and whether it is grounded in the documents it was given. Alibaba released Qwen three Guard — Qwen being the name of its family of models, usually said kwen — in September of twenty twenty-five, with two refinements worth knowing: a third verdict, controversial, between safe and unsafe, so that a deployment can decide for itself how strict to be about the middle; and a streaming variant, of which more in a moment. The recipes differ in instructive ways. ShieldGemma, in three sizes from two to twenty-seven billion parameters, was trained mainly on synthetic data from a curation pipeline, against four harm types, and reports a lead of about eleven points of area under the precision-recall curve over Llama Guard on the public sets. WildGuard, from the Allen Institute, does three jobs in one model — is the prompt harmful, is the response harmful, did the model refuse — on a training set of some ninety thousand examples, synthetic and real, and matches or slightly beats GPT-four on adversarial prompts while leading the open models by up to a quarter on refusal detection, which turns out to be the hardest of the three to label. Granite Guardian's distinction is its retrieval checks — context relevance, groundedness, answer relevance — beside the harms, and its output form, which has become the common one: the verdict is a single yes or no inside tags, and the risk probability is the softmax over the two tokens' logits, so that a generative model yields a calibrated score; its latest version lets you bring your own criteria as a document. And Qwen three Guard's streaming variant is a different animal from the others: not a model that reads text at all, but a token-level classification head fed the token identifiers of the guarded model's output as they are produced, carrying a state from token to token — which is why it works best with models that share the Qwen tokenizer — and which, on Alibaba's own test, lands on the exact first unsafe token in the mid eighties of cases and within the first hundred and twenty-eight tokens in over ninety; with a reasoning model's thinking in the stream, the exact-hit rate collapses to a fifth or worse, because the thinking is where the model talks itself toward the line.

And in October of twenty twenty-five, OpenAI published open weights for a model it called gpt-oss-safeguard, which took Llama Guard's idea to its logical end. Here the policy is not a list of category names. It is a document — written in plain prose, as long and as particular as you like, the sort of thing a trust-and-safety team actually writes — and the model is a reasoning model, which reads the policy, reads the content, thinks the case through, and gives its verdict with its reasons. OpenAI described it as bring-your-own-policy, and said it was an open version of a tool it used internally. The doorman has become a junior lawyer with the house rules open on the desk. The models come in a hundred-and-twenty-billion and a twenty-billion size; the policy is the system message, four to six hundred tokens being the sweet spot by OpenAI's guide, with definitions, criteria and a handful of boundary examples; and the verdict comes with its reasoning. What interests me most is the company's own candour about where it does not belong. It should not be called on every event, the guide says; OpenAI's own practice is to gate its internal equivalent behind small, high-recall classifiers that choose what gets assessed, and to run some of the assessment asynchronously; and the technical report concedes that a classifier trained on tens of thousands of good labels can still beat the policy-reader, so that for a well-understood harm the dedicated classifier is the better choice, and the reasoning judge is for the policy that changes, the case that needs explaining, and the harm you have not yet collected examples of. Time and compute are the price, said plainly.

This year's crop shows the shape settling. IBM released Granite Guardian four-point-one in April. NVIDIA's Nemotron three-point-five Content Safety, in June, reads an image as well as text, takes a custom policy, and will think before it judges or not, as you prefer. And in August, Mistral released Shieldstral, a small open model of a few billion parameters, which takes a policy written in plain language at the moment of use and returns a probability — leaving the threshold, as it should be left, to you.

Now the pipeline that has become the reference design for a bespoke guard: Anthropic's constitutional classifiers, published at the start of twenty twenty-five against the gravest category of misuse — detailed help toward chemical and biological weapons — and aimed squarely at the universal jailbreak. The method, from the paper and the company's own account: you write a constitution, a document in natural language listing the permitted and the disallowed classes of content — mustard recipes on one side, mustard gas on the other, to take the company's own example; a language model generates from it a very large synthetic set of prompts and completions across every class; the harmful ones are augmented by translation and by rewriting in the styles of known jailbreaks — encoded, role-played, broken into pieces — so that the classifiers learn to see through the costumes; a set of benign queries written by contractors is added to hold down the false positives; and two classifiers are fine-tuned on the result, an input classifier reading the prompt and an output classifier assessing, at every token of the streamed response, how harmful the complete output is likely to be, so that it can halt the stream before the harmful part is written. The classifiers' base and their threshold are not published.

The testing was unusually serious, and the figures are the ones every later guard is measured against. Anthropic offered a bounty of fifteen thousand dollars to anyone who could find a universal jailbreak of a prototype, and over two months a hundred and eighty-three active participants spent, by its estimate, more than three thousand hours trying. None succeeded in the terms set. In automated tests with ten thousand synthetic jailbreak prompts, the unguarded model of the day was jailbroken eighty-six percent of the time; with the classifiers, four point four percent. The cost was also measured, which is to the authors' great credit: the classifiers raised the rate of refusals on real traffic by less than half of one percentage point, and added nearly a quarter to the computation needed to serve each request.

Then Anthropic did something braver. In February of twenty twenty-five it opened a public demonstration, with eight levels of increasingly demanding questions, and invited all comers to get through. The week it ran is worth setting down in full, because the bounty's result and the demonstration's are often confused. Three hundred and thirty-nine people took part, in some three hundred thousand exchanges and about three thousand seven hundred hours between them. Four got through all eight levels. One of the four did it with what Anthropic determined to be a universal jailbreak, and fifty-five thousand dollars in prizes were paid. So the bounty found no universal jailbreak, and the demonstration found one. The classifiers were not unbreakable. They were very expensive to break, and that was the claim. A few months later, in May of twenty twenty-five, classifiers of this kind became part of the protections deployed with Claude Opus four.

The second generation, this January, changed the design in two ways and the economics in one. The first change is the exchange classifier: one classifier that scores the output in the context of its input, in place of the separate pair, because a reply that is innocent alone may not be innocent as an answer to that question — and in human red-teaming the change alone cut successful jailbreaks by more than half, at half as much compute again. The second change pays for the first. The whole became a cascade: a cheap first stage screens all traffic and only the exchanges it flags go to the expensive ensemble — and the cheap stage is a linear probe on the model's own activations, concatenated across all its layers, with the per-token scores smoothed by an exponential moving average so that no single spiking token can condemn a conversation. The probe handles, in the company's words, the vast majority; no fraction is published. The figures, with their qualifiers: roughly one percent compute overhead if applied to Claude Opus four's traffic, against nearly a quarter for the first generation — about a twenty-fourth of the cost; a refusal rate on harmless queries of five in ten thousand over a month of production traffic, an eighty-seven percent drop; more than seventeen hundred hours of red-teaming across a hundred and ninety-eight thousand attempts, which found one high-risk vulnerability — five in a million queries — and, in the company's words, no universal jailbreak; and two named remaining weaknesses, reconstruction attacks that split the forbidden thing into innocent-looking segments and reassemble it, and output obfuscation that disguises the answer from the classifier but not from the reader — both of which, the company notes, cost the attacker dearly in the model's capability, a graduate-level science benchmark falling from seventy-four percent to as low as thirty-two under some of them. The probe at the head of it is the subject of Part Four.

The benchmarks, and how they mislead. The public sets, in brief, and what each cannot tell you. ToxicChat is ten thousand real prompts from an open chat demo of twenty twenty-three, seven percent of them toxic, and it is now part of Meta's own evaluation suite, which makes it a public test that vendors tune toward. XSTest, from Paul Rottger and colleagues, is four hundred and fifty prompts — two hundred and fifty that sound unsafe and are not, in ten types, and two hundred unsafe contrasts — and its results are the cleanest statement of the trade-off in the literature: Llama two's seventy-billion chat model, with its shipped system prompt, refused thirty-eight percent of the safe prompts outright and partly refused another twenty-two; without the system prompt, fourteen; GPT-four, six; and Mistral's seven-billion model refused under one percent of the safe prompts — and complied with sixty-four percent of the unsafe ones. OR-Bench scales the same idea to eighty thousand seemingly toxic prompts rewritten from toxic seeds, with a hard thousand and a toxic six hundred, and across thirty-two models found safety and over-refusal correlated at nought point eight eight — with the caveat that three of the models served as the moderators that labelled it. HarmBench is five hundred and ten behaviours — two hundred standard, a hundred contextual, a hundred on copyright, a hundred and ten multimodal — judged by a thirteen-billion Llama two distilled from GPT-four; and here is the thing to know about judges: when the JailbreakBench team checked that judge against three human annotators, it agreed with their majority seventy-eight percent of the time and flagged twenty-seven percent of XSTest's benign items as harmful, so they chose a different judge. Two facts about a guard's reported score follow. It was measured on a set the guard's makers chose, often one its training data has seen the shape of — one small study this year found every open guard it tried falling sharply on a hundred and forty-five novel prompts, the best of them from ninety-one percent to thirty-four. And it was measured mostly in English. In twenty twenty-three, Zheng-Xin Yong and colleagues translated a standard set of harmful requests into low-resource languages and bypassed GPT-four's refusals seventy-nine percent of the time across them, fifty-three in Zulu, against under one percent in English; the guards have the same shape, Llama Guard three's F-one running from nought point nine four in French to nought point eight three in Thai, and its fourth version's recall falling from sixty-nine percent in English to forty-three across its other languages. The pattern you should expect: a guard's published accuracy was measured on somebody else's traffic, against somebody else's definitions of harm, mostly in English, often on a set its training data has seen the shape of; and the number that matters for you, recall at the false-positive rate your product can bear on your own traffic, appears in none of the tables.

Now the mechanics of putting a guard in the path of traffic, which begin with time. A guard model is another model call, and in a conversational product the user is watching the clock. The standard answer to the input check's cost is to run it in parallel: send the request to the guard and to the main model at once, and if the guard objects, cancel the main model's work and discard whatever it has begun. OpenAI's toolkit for building agents, released in March of twenty twenty-five, made this a named pattern, with a vivid name: guardrails that run alongside the agent, and a tripwire which, when one of them trips, halts the agent where it stands. You pay for some wasted computation on the requests that are stopped. You pay no latency on the ones that are not, which is nearly all of them. The toolkit has since added guardrails on tool calls — on a tool's input before it runs and its output before the agent reads it — which is the action position of Part One, and the right place for them. There is one caution, and the toolkit's own documentation is candid about it: by the time the tripwire fires, an agent running alongside its guardrail may already have spent its tokens — or called a tool. Cancelling a sentence half written costs nothing; cancelling an email half sent is not a thing that can be done. So the toolkit also offers a blocking mode, in which the guardrail finishes its check before the agent begins, and where the agent can act on the world, that is the mode to choose.

The output check is harder, because the user expects the answer token by token and a check that waits for the whole answer destroys that. There are three ways out, and the history of the field contains all three. The first is to buffer: hold the text until the check is done, which is safe, slow, and right where the stakes are high and the answers short. The second is to stream optimistically and retract: let the text flow, check it as it goes or when it finishes, and if the check fails, take it back. Users of DeepSeek's chat application in January of twenty twenty-five saw the pattern plainly — an answer appearing, then deleted seconds later and replaced by an apology that the question was beyond its scope — and the press noticed, as the press always does, that the model had reasoned first and been censored after. It is the cheapest arrangement and the most revealing, for it shows the user exactly what the system did not want them to see, at least for a moment, and it has a way of becoming the story.

The third is a classifier built for streaming: one that reads the partial answer as it grows and gives a score at every token, so that the generation can be halted at the very moment it crosses a line, before the dangerous part is written. The honest number for such a classifier is its time to halt — how many tokens escape before the verdict — and Alibaba's streaming guard, above, is the only one I know that publishes it. The defaults of the common toolkits are worth knowing in the same connection: OpenAI's guardrails library runs its output checks in parallel with the stream and says in its documentation that violative content may briefly appear before a guardrail triggers, recommending the non-streaming mode where compliance matters; NVIDIA's toolkit, by default, sends two-hundred-token chunks to the client before its output rails run on them; and Google's Model Armor offers a buffered mode and a real-time mode, the choice being exactly the trade of this paragraph. The streaming variant of Qwen three Guard works this way, and so, as you heard, does the output classifier of the constitutional classifiers. It is the most sophisticated of the three, and it has a cost of its own. A classifier that must judge a sentence before it is finished must sometimes guess where it is going, and it will occasionally halt an answer that would have turned out perfectly harmless.

The answer to the cost is the cascade, and the production systems of all three major laboratories now use one: a tiny classifier on everything, adding a few milliseconds, and the large, expensive guard only on the cases the tiny one is unsure of, so that the cost grows with the doubtful fraction rather than with the whole.

The arithmetic, as an engineer does it rather than as a slide does it. Let the small stage cost c-small per request and the large stage c-large, and let the small stage escalate a fraction p of traffic; the expected cost is c-small plus p times c-large, which is why the whole game is in p — Anthropic's production cascade escalates a few percent, Google's Gemini probes defer about one case in twelve to a prompted model at about a fiftieth of its cost on everything. The false positives compose: under independence, two stages each wrongly refusing one request in a hundred refuse very nearly two in a hundred, and every layer you add removes some misses and adds its own refusals, which is why each layer in a well-built stack is set lenient and the stack's strength comes from the independence of several lenient checks rather than the severity of one. And the base rate governs the precision: at one genuinely bad request in ten thousand, a classifier with ninety-five percent recall and a one-percent false-positive rate blocks about a hundred wrongful refusals for every true catch, so the threshold is a decision about which error you would rather explain — to the user refused for nothing, or to the reporter writing about the one that got through. The operating point is chosen on a labelled sample of your own traffic, by the recall you need at the false-positive rate you can bear, and it drifts: traffic changes, the adversaries change, and a threshold calibrated once is calibrated for last year. Three recent papers put numbers on the last point. One frames a layered defence as an ensemble that refuses if any layer refuses, so that the false-positive rate compounds as one minus the product of the layers' pass rates and can never be lower than the worst layer's; a civic deployment with seven layers reported a naive compound rate near eight percent brought down to under three by having layers flag and escalate rather than block. Another, at this year's representation-learning conference, found the guard models systematically overconfident, and worse under jailbreaks and when the model whose replies they judge changes. And a third, from this June, watched deployed classifiers' score distributions drift with a sliding statistical test and adjusted the threshold to hold a target error rate, catching the drift in most cases after a lag of some forty requests — and failing to recalibrate at all for some models. About a hundred labelled samples from the new traffic, another paper found, capture most of what recalibration can give.

Over-refusal — the model's own wrongful declining, as distinct from the guard's — is the same quantity one layer down, measured by the same instruments, XSTest and OR-Bench above all; and the public notices it at least as quickly as it notices harm, as Google found in February of twenty twenty-four, when its senior vice president wrote that the Gemini model had become way more cautious than intended and had refused to answer certain prompts entirely. A guardrail that embarrasses its owner by refusing is not a safe guardrail. It is a different way of failing.

The same trade was conducted in public this summer, in the field where it is cruellest: cybersecurity, where the defender's questions and the attacker's are very often the same questions, word for word. Anthropic's newest models carry classifiers that watch for requests touching on cybersecurity, among other subjects — strict enough, when its Fable five model was launched in June, that by the company's own account many users complained they were overly broad. Two details of the arrangement repay attention. The first is what happens to a flagged request. In the company's own applications it is not simply refused; it is handed to an older and less capable model, Claude Opus four-point-eight, and the user is told. That is a way of making a false positive cheaper: the innocent request is still answered, if less brilliantly. The second came with the release of Claude Opus five in July, when the company said it expected the classifiers to intervene around eighty-five percent less often than they had for its Fable five model, and pointed security professionals to what it calls its Cyber Verification Program, under which vetted researchers receive a version of the model with fewer security restrictions. This month the programme grew three tiers, which are themselves a lesson in setting an operating point by population rather than by prompt: defence access, for security teams defending systems they own, with applications answered in days; red-team access, for organisations authorised to attack systems they are permitted to test, reviewed over weeks, and still subject to real-time blocks on actions that could cause physical harm or mass disruption; and a specialised tier with the fewest blocks of all. The same classifier, three thresholds, chosen by who has been verified. Verification, you will notice, is the base-rate problem turned to advantage. A threshold that must be strict for an anonymous public can be relaxed for a population whose base rate of malice is very different — not because the classifier has grown cleverer, but because you now know more about who is asking.

How such a classifier is tested, at the top of the trade, is published too, and it is the model for testing your own. Anthropic's report on Fable five describes an automated red-teaming agent — an earlier model of its own, set to attack the guarded model over as many as four hundred turns, allowed to rewind or restart a conversation when blocked, and to break an offensive task into small steps as a real attacker would. Against two earlier models with their default cyber safeguards it completed the majority of the tasks, seventy-three and fifty-seven percent; against Fable five, five percent, a residue the company attributes to the simple, dual-use character of some of the tasks rather than to a weakness. Then the outside testers, each with its own method and its own budget: one firm found a single strategy, with a custom harness and five days of trial and error, that let an earlier model with the same safeguards exploit a known flaw in a browser, and could not make it general; another spent twenty hours on a ransomware task with the established jailbreak techniques and failed, reporting — the sentence that matters most — that the classifiers detected not just risky keywords but the broader pattern of an attack being assembled once enough pieces appeared together; a third found inconsistent blocking on borderline dual-use requests and could not complete any task; and a fourth, given the launch version, found the model complied with none of its harmful single-message requests, with or without any of thirty public jailbreaks. Note what the report does not say: that the classifier is unbreakable. It says that four independent attackers, with four methods, got narrow results or none, at a stated cost in hours — which is the only kind of robustness claim this evening will accept.

So let me put the guard's position plainly, for it underlies everything that follows. Every classifier trades refusals against misses. At scale, both become crowds: a small false-positive rate on an enormous innocent majority produces far more wrongful refusals than true catches, and the threshold that decides between them is a product decision, not a technical fact. Layers help only when their failures are independent, and each layer adds its own refusals. The art is a stack of lenient, independent checks, each cheap, each fallible, whose failures rarely coincide.

Part Three — The Science of the Jailbreak

A jailbreak, for the purposes of this Part, is any input that obtains from a model a behaviour its training was arranged to decline, and the subject of the Part is not the jailbreaks themselves — which you can read in their thousands on the forums where they are traded — but the two things that turned them into a science: attacks that are methods, with an objective and an algorithm, and defences that are methods too, evaluated, when they are evaluated honestly, against attackers who know them. I shall give the attacks as methods, the defences as methods, the measurement problem that corrupts both literatures, and the adaptive evaluation that has become the standard. The telling is historical because the lesson is historical: nearly every defence in this Part was beaten within months of being published, and the field learned what a defence is for from watching that happen.

The race became a science in July of twenty twenty-three, with a paper from Carnegie Mellon and elsewhere by Andy Zou and colleagues, among them Nicholas Carlini, of whom we shall hear again. Until then, jailbreaks had been written by hand, by people with a gift for persuasion. Zou and colleagues showed that they could be found by machine. Using open models whose weights were available, they ran an automated search for a short suffix of tokens which, appended to a harmful request, made the model comply. The objective is worth stating exactly, because every white-box attack since has a version of it: minimise the loss on a target that is only the beginning of an affirmative reply — Sure, here is, and a restatement of the request — on the correct bet that a model put in the state where that opening is most likely will continue with the content. The search is greedy coordinate gradient: at each step, take the gradient of that loss with respect to the one-hot indicator of the token at every suffix position; nominate at each position the few hundred replacements with the largest negative gradient; build a batch of some five hundred candidates, each changing one random position to one of its nominees; evaluate the true loss of every candidate by a forward pass; keep the best; and repeat, for five hundred steps over a suffix of twenty tokens. To make the result universal rather than bespoke, one suffix is optimised against the sum of the losses over many prompts and over several open models sharing a tokeniser, the prompts added one at a time as the suffix conquers the earlier ones. The strings looked like gibberish — a jumble of words, punctuation, and fragments — but they worked, and, more alarming still, strings found on open models carried over to closed models which the searchers had never been able to inspect. Not everywhere, and not equally. Counting a hit if any of a handful of suffixes worked, the searchers got their way with the GPT-three-point-five of the day in nearly nine cases in ten, with GPT-four in nearly half, with Google's PaLM two in two-thirds, sampling eight times, and with Anthropic's Claude two in about two percent; and the authors noted themselves that the open models they searched on had been trained on ChatGPT's outputs, so that the transfer to GPT-three-point-five may owe something to distillation, and that running the search too long overfits and transfers worse. But a lock that opens to a stranger's key half the time is not much of a lock. The trade calls these adversarial suffixes, and the method, by the initials of its name, GCG. The point was not any particular string. It was that jailbreaking had become an optimisation problem, and optimisation problems are solved by computers, tirelessly, at scale.

The first defences were cheap and clever, and their fate is the moral of this Part. Within weeks, a group led by Neel Jain at Maryland and New York University published what they called baseline defences, and framed the threat model in a way the field has kept: bound the attacker by compute — GCG costs some five hundred thousand model evaluations per suffix, five or six orders of magnitude more than an attack in computer vision — rather than by a distance in input space. Three baselines. The perplexity filter: a language model can measure how surprising it finds a piece of text, and the gibberish suffixes had a great deal of it, so the filter rejected any prompt whose perplexity exceeded the highest seen on a corpus of plain harmful prompts, with a windowed variant over runs of ten tokens. Essentially no GCG prompt passed; an attack that added a perplexity penalty to its own objective passed the plain filter but got past the windowed one only one time in five, at a cost that brought its success near the no-attack level; and the filter's price was that about one benign prompt in ten was flagged, which the authors called heavy-handed and proposed only as a signal to route elsewhere. Paraphrasing the prompt through a second model, which destroys a suffix optimised character by character: on Vicuna, attack success fell from seventy-nine percent to five, the unattacked level, at a cost of about a tenth of the model's measured quality, and a white-box attacker who optimised against the paraphraser got through. And retokenisation, dropping a fraction of the tokeniser's merges so that the suffix is cut into different pieces from the ones it was optimised for, which roughly halved the attack on two models, helped a third not at all, and made the models refuse plain harmful prompts less, because broken tokens evade the refusal too. The paper's argument was that text optimisers are weak and costly, so that filtering and preprocessing fare better here than they had in vision, where every such defence had fallen to an adaptive attack; and it was honest enough to add that the open question was whether stronger optimisers would arrive. They arrived within the quarter, and they did not need gradients.

The attacker is itself a language model. PAIR, from Patrick Chao, Alexander Robey and colleagues at Pennsylvania in October of twenty twenty-three, sets an attacker model, prompted with a persuasion strategy and a worked example, to write a candidate jailbreak for a given behaviour; the candidate goes to the target, a judge scores the reply, and if it fails, the candidate, the reply and the score are appended to the attacker's history and it revises, some thirty such streams running in parallel for a few rounds. The title promised success in twenty queries, and on the paper's original fifty behaviours it delivered, opening GPT-four about six times in ten at sixteen queries a success; on its later benchmark the averages ran from ten queries to above fifty, and against Llama two and Claude it struggled, its authors' word, with success in the low single digits. Tree of Attacks with Pruning, from Anay Mehrotra and colleagues at Yale and Robust Intelligence two months later, adds branching, every candidate spawning several refinements, and two prunings: an evaluator discards off-topic candidates before they reach the target, and only the best-scoring leaves survive each round. On the same behaviours it opened GPT-four nine times in ten at under thirty queries, and its ablation is the instructive part: without branching, success fell from eighty-four percent to forty-eight; without pruning it held, at twice the queries. And AutoDAN, from Xiaogeng Liu and colleagues at Wisconsin, kept GCG's objective and replaced the gradient with a hierarchical genetic algorithm over fluent prompts — a population seeded from a hand-written persona jailbreak and diversified by a model rewriting it, fitness the same affirmative-prefix likelihood, crossover at the sentence level, mutation by a model — which yields readable text with a perplexity near prose: under a perplexity filter, GCG's success on Llama two fell to zero and AutoDAN's did not move. Each of the three needs a judge, which is a subject of its own in a moment, and the evaluator in Tree of Attacks had a false-positive rate of thirteen percent. Microsoft's Skeleton Key, disclosed by Mark Russinovich in June of twenty twenty-four, belongs to the family done by hand: a multi-turn request that the model augment its guidelines rather than abandon them, answering everything with a warning prefixed, which that spring worked on most of the frontier models the company tried.

Two defences were then built with their threat model stated honestly, and they are worth knowing for the statement as much as for the numbers. SmoothLLM, from Robey and colleagues at Pennsylvania in October of twenty twenty-three, rests on an observation: the GCG suffixes are brittle, and changing a tenth of their characters at random breaks them. So: make some number of copies of the whole prompt, perturb each at the character level by insertions, swaps or a patched run, query the model on every copy, run a jailbreak judge on every reply, and return a reply that agrees with the majority verdict. With more than six copies, GCG's success fell below one percent on Vicuna and Llama two, and PAIR's was roughly halved; the guarantee is a closed-form probability of defence that holds if the suffix fails whenever that many characters change, which is an empirical assumption about suffix attacks and not a certificate against a semantic jailbreak; and the cost is the copies, each a query, and a few points of nominal accuracy. Erase-and-check, from Aounon Kumar and colleagues at Harvard in September of the same year, is a true certificate within a narrower threat model: run a safety filter on the prompt and on every prompt obtainable by erasing up to some number of tokens, and flag the prompt if any is flagged. If the attacker only added tokens to a harmful prompt, one of the erased versions is the clean harmful prompt itself, so the attacked prompt is caught whenever the clean one is, whatever the attack algorithm — certified accuracy equals the filter's accuracy on clean harmful prompts, ninety-two percent with a Llama two filter in the paper — and the price is the enumeration, linear in the budget for a suffix, polynomial for an inserted block, exponential for tokens scattered anywhere, with the running time the authors' own first-named limitation. A prompt rewritten rather than appended to is outside the certificate altogether, and PAIR's prompts, which rewrite the request, were outside both defences.

Remember that pattern, because it repeats. A defence that detects a feature of the known attacks invites attacks without the feature — and a certificate is exactly as wide as its threat model.

The next two years produced new kinds of attack that exploited, not any weakness of a particular model, but the very things that made the newer models better. As context windows grew to hundreds of thousands of tokens, researchers at Anthropic found, and published in April of twenty twenty-four, that the long context itself could be turned into an attack. They called it many-shot jailbreaking. A model learns from examples placed in its context — that is in-context learning, one of its great strengths — and if the context is filled with a great many fabricated exchanges in which an assistant cheerfully does something it should not, the model's inclination to follow the pattern can overwhelm its training. The more examples, the more effective; the effectiveness rose smoothly with their number, following what the researchers called a power law, and larger models, which learn in context faster, needed fewer. Their account of the mitigations is instructive. Training the model to refuse such prompts only delayed the attack: in the paper's terms it raised the intercept of the power law and left its exponent alone, so that more examples were needed, but with enough of them the harmful answers came back. What worked better was to classify and modify suspicious prompts before the model saw them — in one case they reported, cutting the attack's success from sixty-one percent to two. A guardrail at the door, again, doing what training alone had not.

The same month, researchers at Microsoft, led by Mark Russinovich, described an attack they called Crescendo, which exploited the conversation rather than the context. It begins with an entirely innocent question on a subject adjacent to the forbidden one, and proceeds by small steps, each one referring to what the model itself has just said, each one only slightly further than the last, until the model has, by degrees, arrived where it would never have gone in one stride — usually, the paper found, in fewer than five turns. No single message is objectionable. Only the conversation is. Automated, as Crescendomation, with GPT-four as the attacker, a judge for success, and a refusal judge that erases a refused turn from the target's history and rephrases it, the attack averaged fifty-six percent success on GPT-four across fifty tasks where PAIR averaged forty and many-shot thirty-seven, and eighty-three percent on Gemini Pro. Microsoft's own mitigation was the plain one, and the company reported it in words worth keeping: passing the whole conversation, rather than the latest turn, to the existing intent detectors, without improving the detectors at all, significantly reduced the attack's efficacy. That defence is now standard — classifiers that read the whole exchange, or a running summary of it — and it is the inference-time half of a problem we shall meet again in Part Seven, that a model's good behaviour erodes over a long exchange with or without an attacker.

The defences, then — and I shall take them in the order of how deep into the model they reach. The deepest is a training-time intervention on the model's internal states, which belongs in this evening because what it changes is what an attacker can do at inference. If harmful behaviour leaves a signature inside the model, the model can be trained to react to the signature. In June of twenty twenty-four, Andy Zou and colleagues, at the security firm Gray Swan and at Carnegie Mellon, described what they called circuit breakers: they trained the model so that when its internal states began to resemble the states associated with producing harmful content, those states were diverted — rerouted, in the paper's word — into something useless, so that the harmful output simply failed to form. The attraction is that the defence does not depend on recognising the costume an attack is wearing, since whatever the costume, the model must still, internally, approach the harmful content in order to produce it. The mechanism is representation rerouting, trained with low-rank adapters against a frozen copy of the original model: on a set of harmful assistant responses — often with the harmful request removed, so that what is trained is not the refusal reflex — the loss penalises the cosine similarity between the adapted model's representation and the original's, driving the harmful-state representations orthogonal to what they were, while a retain loss on benign conversations pins the benign representations in place; a random target direction was tried and found neither necessary nor optimal. The numbers, on HarmBench with a classifier judge and manual checks: on the seven-billion Mistral, average attack success across ten attacks fell from seventy-seven percent to under ten; on the eight-billion Llama three, from thirty-eight to under four; and the authors' Cygnet model, adding other representation controls, reported under one percent, with the capability benchmarks dipping less than a point. Then the attacker moved. Within weeks, Schwinn and Geisler showed that an improved attack in embedding space — a threat model the paper had itself called unrealistically strong — reached full success against both rerouted models, and the Rapid Response paper from Anthropic, that November, recorded as plain fact that the method had been publicly broken within hours of its release. Two cousins deserve a line each. Targeted latent adversarial training, from Sheshadri, Casper and colleagues the following month, moves the adversary inside: during safety training a bounded perturbation is applied to the residual-stream activations themselves, chosen to make the harmful completion likely, and the model is trained to keep refusing under it; on Llama two it matched or beat the adversarially trained baseline at hundreds of times fewer passes, with some capability cost on Llama three. Tamper-resistant safeguards, from Tamirisa and colleagues that August, aim at the open-weight case, where the attacker fine-tunes: an inner loop simulates fine-tuning attacks and an outer loop trains the weights so that the model after such an attack still fails at the hazardous task; the paper reported hazardous-knowledge accuracy near random after most of twenty-eight attacks, at a cost of eleven points of retained capability, and a re-evaluation that December, with Nicholas Carlini among its authors, found that merely re-running the paper's own attacks with a few more random seeds recovered the knowledge — repeating the same attack a few more times, they wrote, can already break the defence. All three are the inside view turned to defence, and all three share the probe's condition from the next Part: they are available only to whoever trains the model.

The next defence uses the model's own reasoning, and you met its training in Part One: deliberative alignment, the model recalling the written policy and reasoning over it before it answers. A month after it, in January of twenty twenty-five, a paper from the same company with the striking title Trading Inference-Time Compute for Adversarial Robustness reported that, across many kinds of attack and with no adversarial training at all, letting o-one think longer made it harder to jailbreak, the attack success often tending to zero; in a human red-teaming study across five levels of compute, the attempts needed rose from eleven to twenty-three. Though not against every attack, and the exceptions are stated. Where the policy was ambiguous, an attacker model found loopholes that more thinking did not close; at very low compute some attacks briefly grew more effective; and two new attacks appeared, one instructing the model to think less, which is observable as an unusually short chain, and one the authors called, with some relish, nerd sniping, in which the model is lured into an unproductive loop — the longest chains were the least safe, so that more compute helped on average and not pointwise. How a model is taught how hard to think is the subject of another evening. The qualification arrived in July, from Tong Wu and colleagues at Princeton and elsewhere, who ran twelve open reasoning models with the thinking budget forced from a hundred tokens to sixteen thousand. With the reasoning hidden from the adversary, robustness rose as OpenAI had said. With the reasoning exposed, it fell as the budget grew — an inverse scaling, in their phrase — on one fourteen-billion model from about ninety percent robust to prompt injection to below twenty; and even hidden reasoning leaks, through tool calls made inside it and through extraction of the chain itself. The compute helps the defender only while the attacker cannot read it.

Before the verdict on defences, the measurement problem, which corrupts both literatures and which you, of all listeners, will appreciate. The paper that named it is A StrongREJECT for Empty Jailbreaks, from Alexandra Souly, Sam Toyer and colleagues at Berkeley, in February of twenty twenty-four. The jailbreak literature boasted success rates near a hundred percent because its evaluators scored non-refusal — a string match against the refusal phrases, or a binary classifier — and never whether the answer was any use; the authors re-ran a published attack that had claimed forty-three percent on GPT-four by translating the request into Scots Gaelic and found the outputs far less useful than the number implied. Their benchmark is three hundred and thirteen forbidden prompts, specific and answerable, and their evaluator is a rubric: a model scores whether the reply refused, how specific it was and how convincing, the last two on scales of five, and the score is non-refusal times the mean of the other two. Validated against some fourteen hundred human-labelled pairs, string matching correlated negatively with the human judgement; a GPT-four judge and PAIR's judge only weakly; the HarmBench classifier and the rubric above nought point eight. And the finding that gives the paper its title: the jailbreaks that most raised a model's willingness most lowered its capability — fed to a model with its safety tuning removed, and fed with benign examination questions, the same jailbreaks degraded the answers — so that of thirty-seven jailbreaks tested on three current models, only a few were strong, the attacker-model methods among them, and GCG worked on GPT-three-point-five Turbo and on neither of the newer two. Attack success rate is a number produced by a judge, and the judge is a choice. Read every figure in this Part with that in mind, mine included.

And now the verdict on defences, which is the most important single thing in this Part.

In October of twenty twenty-five, fourteen researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, ETH Zurich, and elsewhere — among them Milad Nasr, Nicholas Carlini, and Florian Tramer — published a paper called The Attacker Moves Second. They took twelve recently published defences against jailbreaks and prompt injection, most of which had reported attack success rates near zero, and attacked them properly: with attacks adapted to each defence, using gradient search, reinforcement learning, and random search, and with a contest among human red-teamers. For most of the defences, the adaptive attacks succeeded more than ninety percent of the time. The human contest, run online with twenty thousand dollars in prizes, set forty challenges built on the defences; between them, the contestants beat all forty.

The title states the principle. In a security evaluation, the attacker moves second: the attacker sees the defence, and then chooses the attack. A defence evaluated only against attacks that existed before it was built has been tested against people who did not know it existed, which is to say it has not been tested. And the field had been here before. In twenty eighteen, Anish Athalye, Nicholas Carlini, and David Wagner examined nine defences against adversarial examples in image classification, recently accepted at a leading conference, and found that seven relied on a kind of accidental camouflage of the model's gradients that gave a false sense of security; they circumvented six of them completely and the seventh in part. The same lesson, learned twice by the same field, seven years apart.

There is a deeper reason, too, why no output filter can be perfect, and it was set out in twenty twenty-three by David Glukhov and colleagues at the University of Toronto and elsewhere. Deciding whether an arbitrary output is harmful is, in its general form, undecidable — the same kind of question as deciding what an arbitrary program will do — because harmful content can be encoded in endless ways that only the recipient knows how to decode. And even without encoding, harmful knowledge can be assembled from pieces, each of which is innocent alone: a question about one step here, another about another step there, each answered properly, the whole never asked. They called these mosaic prompts. A filter that judges each piece cannot see the mosaic.

The adaptive attacker need not be a gradient; the cheapest adaptive attacker is the public, and the field learned to rent it — at DEF CON in August of twenty twenty-three, where some two thousand two hundred people at closed terminals exchanged more than a hundred and sixty thousand messages with eight models in two and a half days; in Lakera's Gandalf, a game in which a wizard keeps a password and the player talks it out of him, which drew nine million attempts from two hundred thousand people in its first weeks and furnished its maker with a corpus to sell protection from; and, in the current form you met in the opening, the bounty programme and the arena, which Part Two priced.

Which brings the Part back to where the evening began, and to the distinction that organises the laboratories' whole posture: the universal jailbreak against the narrow one. A universal jailbreak is a method that unlocks a whole class of forbidden behaviours, can be written down and shared, and works for people with no skill; a narrow one unlocks a specific behaviour with effort. The Cyber Jailbreak Severity scale of the opening is that distinction written as a rubric: capability gain and breadth say what a jailbreak unlocks; ease of weaponisation and discoverability say how cheap it is to use. And the response to a found jailbreak has become a method of its own. Rapid Response, from Alwin Peng, Mrinank Sharma and colleagues at Anthropic in November of twenty twenty-four, gives up static robustness by design: observe a few jailbreaks in deployment, then adapt faster than the attacker can spread them. The engine is jailbreak proliferation — a model writes around a thousand variants of each observed jailbreak, in its style, for other harmful behaviours — and the best of the five defences tried fine-tunes the input classifier, Llama Guard two, on the observed examples, the proliferations and benign traffic, with the threshold recalibrated to hold the original refusal rate. On a benchmark of six attack strategies, each with an in-distribution set and an out-of-distribution variant, and having seen one example per strategy, the fine-tuned guard cut in-distribution success by a factor the body of the paper puts above fifteen and the abstract above two hundred and forty, and the variants' by more than fifteen; the proliferation did the work, taking the guard's average attack success from twelve percent to about one. The authors' own qualifiers: it depends on detecting the jailbreak in time, and it assumes that a single failure is not catastrophic. It is the June affair's fortnight, as a procedure.

So here is what the arms race has taught, and it is the third of the evening's three great points, so I shall put it carefully. Every guardrail can be beaten by an attacker who studies it, and the honest measure of a defence is how it performs against an adaptive attacker who knows exactly what it is — not against yesterday's attacks. Perfect filtering is impossible in principle. The realistic aim, therefore, is not to make attacks impossible but to make them expensive: to stop the universal jailbreaks, which are cheap to use once found, and to leave only the laborious, one-off successes, which other layers — monitoring, rate limits, the banning of accounts, the law — can deal with afterwards. It is the economics of locks, not the mathematics of proofs. A good lock does not make a house impregnable. It makes the burglar choose another house, or bring tools that attract attention, or take long enough to be seen.

Part Four — The Stethoscope

Everything so far has read the words: the request going in, the answer coming out. This Part reads the working instead. You know the anatomy from another season of ours, so I shall give it in one breath and move on. Every token position carries a vector up through the model's layers — the residual stream, to which each layer adds its contribution and from which each layer reads, the shared notebook of that other season — and the working assumption of the science, borne out often enough to bank on, is that the model's concepts are directions in that space. Fit a direction and a threshold that separate examples with a property from examples without, by ordinary logistic regression on the stream at a chosen layer, and you have a linear probe: the cheap ruler of that other season, here pressed into service as a guardrail. Its running cost is one dot product per token on activations the model computed anyway; beside the forward pass it is a rounding error. A guard model, however small, is a second model reading the text a second time. A probe is not a second reading at all. It is a stethoscope taped to the model's chest, and I shall call it that.

Where it stands in production you heard in Part Two: the first stage of Anthropic's current cascade is a linear probe on the protected model's own activations, screening all traffic and escalating what it flags to the probe-and-classifier ensemble — at about one percent of the guarded model's compute, five wrongful refusals in ten thousand, no universal jailbreak found in seventeen hundred hours of red-teaming, and reconstruction and output obfuscation the named remaining weaknesses.

Nor is the stethoscope Anthropic's alone, and it has spread with a speed unusual in this trade. Anthropic's researchers had reported, the summer before, that a probe on the model's own activations could match a dedicated classifier of about a fiftieth of the model's size at virtually no cost — adding, in fairness, that nobody had yet attacked such a probe directly. By February of this year the company was shipping probes built for cybersecurity, which, in its words, measure activations within the model as it generates a response. In July, OpenAI's system card for its GPT-five-point-six models introduced what it calls activation classifiers: they monitor patterns in the model's internal activations, and if those patterns indicate that the model may be about to generate harmful content, they pause the stream to the user while a separate check decides; and they are trained, the card notes, separately for each model, because each model's activations differ. And Google DeepMind's interpretability team published in January an account called Building production-ready probes for Gemini, reporting that such probes had been deployed in user-facing versions of Gemini against misuse for cyber attack — together with the most instructive failure in the whole literature, to which I shall come. Three laboratories, three closed models, one instrument.

You will have noticed who is doing this: the people who own the weights. That is the stethoscope's one great condition. To read the residual stream you must be running the model yourself. The laboratories can do it on their own closed models, and do. But for the rest of us — the mere mortals outside the laboratories — it means an open-weight model, on servers we control. A customer of a closed model's interface receives words and nothing else; the working never leaves the building. So the cheapest guardrail in the trade is, for a working engineer, a reason to run open weights, to be set beside cost and data residency in the ledger of what self-hosting buys. On a closed interface, the door is still the door.

And open weights cut both ways, in a manner this Part would be dishonest to omit. In twenty twenty-four, Andy Arditi and colleagues showed that a chat model's refusals are carried, to a remarkable degree, by a single direction in the residual stream — the refusal direction — and they showed it in thirteen open chat models, up to seventy-two billion parameters. Remove that direction from the stream as the model runs, and the model stops refusing. Add it, and the model refuses even the most harmless request — a recipe for toast, say. And anyone holding the weights of an open model can make the removal permanent, by editing the direction out of the weights themselves; the hobbyists call it abliteration, and models so treated circulate freely. The lesson for guardrails is plain. On an open model, whatever safety lives in the training can be cut out by whoever holds a copy. A probe that the deployer runs on its own servers cannot be cut out by the user — but the user's copy of the same model is a practice ground, and we shall come, in a few minutes, to what an attacker can do with one.

The same discovery points the other way, and you met it in Part Three as the circuit breaker: if harmful behaviour leaves a signature inside the model, the model can be trained to react to the signature, whatever costume the request is wearing. That is the inside view's great promise. And it brings us to the question you will already have asked.

Belt and braces works only when the failures are independent — when the thing that fools one check is unlikely to fool the next. And a probe is, by construction, the least independent check in the whole stack. It does not read the world. It reads the model's opinion of the world. It can find in the residual stream only what the model has written there; if the model has not represented a thing, no probe on its activations can see it, any more than a stethoscope can hear a heart that is not beating.

Consider three cases.

The first is the confident error. Anthropic's interpretability researchers, tracing how one of their models decides whether to answer a factual question, found that declining is the default — a circuit, in their words, that is on by default and causes the model to state that it has insufficient information — and that features representing a known entity switch it off. A hallucination, in that picture, is the switch misfiring. Asked to name a paper by Andrej Karpathy, a well-known researcher, the model named a famous paper on which Karpathy was not an author: familiarity with the name had licensed an answer that knowledge could not supply. The researchers' own gloss is the point. The model, they suggested, may simply be making a plausible guess about what it is likely to know. A probe trained to read the model believes it knows this will read, quite correctly, that the model believes it knows — and will be wrong about the fact in exactly the way the model is wrong.

The second is the attack the model does not understand. If a request is disguised well enough that the model itself does not grasp what is being asked — encoded, or split, or spelled out in a way the model reads without recognising — then there is no recognition inside for a probe to read. You will meet such a case in a few minutes, from Anthropic's interpretability researchers: a jailbreak that worked precisely because the model did not understand what it had been asked until it had begun to answer. Until that moment, inside the model there was silence — and a stethoscope reports silence faithfully.

The third is the mosaic of Part Three: a harmful whole assembled from innocent pieces, each of which the model judges, correctly, to be innocent. The model never represents the whole, and so no probe can.

In each case the stethoscope fails exactly where the patient fails. And a second check that shares the first one's blind spots, you will remember, is the first check twice.

And yet — and this is the turn on which the whole Part rests — the model's blind spots and the model's failures are not the same set. Most of the failures that matter in this evening's story are not failures of knowledge at all. They are failures of restraint. The jailbroken model usually knows perfectly well what it has been asked; the trouble is that it does it anyway. And knowing leaves a trace.

The evidence for that has accumulated in three places.

In twenty twenty-four, researchers at Anthropic took models deliberately trained with a hidden trigger — sleeper agents, they called them, which write safe code until a particular cue appears and insecure code after it — and showed that a probe built from nothing more specific than the contrast between the answers yes and no to a generic question, read at the last token of the prompt, before the model had written a word, could tell whether the model was about to defect. Its area under the curve was above ninety-nine percent — hold the number, for Part Two's caution about areas under curves will come back. The authors were careful to add that the salience of the signal might be an artifact of the way the backdoor had been put in, and might not exist in a model that had come to deceive of its own accord.

In twenty twenty-five, Apollo Research trained probes to tell when a large open model was being strategically deceptive in its answers, and reported scores between nought point nine six and nought point nine nine nine on its tests — while noting that the probe also fired on honest confessions of past misdeeds, as though it were partly listening for the topic of deception rather than the act, and judging the whole technique, in its own words, insufficient as a robust defence against deception.

And in the same year, Jiachen Zhao and colleagues, at Northeastern and Stanford, found that a model represents this request is harmful and I should refuse as two different directions, read at two different moments — the first at the end of the user's instruction, the second just before the model begins its reply — and that some jailbreaks work by suppressing the second while leaving the first largely intact. The model goes on recognising the harm. It merely stops acting on it.

Anthropic, describing its own probes this January, put the point in a sentence I should like you to keep. When the model processes a dubious-seeming request, the company wrote, patterns fire in its internal activations that reflect something along the lines of this seems harmful, even before it has formulated a response or made a conscious decision about what to do. And it reported the empirical answer to the question of independence: the probes, it found, were complementary to its external classifiers — the probe appears to see things the external classifier cannot, and vice versa.

So here is the refinement of belt and braces that the inside view forces on us, and it is the most useful idea in this Part. Independence is a matter of kind as well as degree. A probe is not independent of the model's knowledge: whatever the model cannot see, it cannot see. But it is independent of the model's decision. It reads what the model represents, not what the model chose to say — and the gap between the two, between knowing and doing, is exactly where jailbreaks live. A classifier at the door, trained separately, may know things the model does not: a pattern of attack it was shown, a category the model was never taught. A probe knows only what the model knows, and catches the model when it knows and does it anyway. A model's awareness that it is about to break a rule is no guarantee that it will not break it. But the awareness is legible, and legible at almost no cost. Those are different failures, caught by different checks — which is the whole reason for wanting both.

Now the matter of time, and here the stethoscope has a freedom no other check enjoys. A classifier at the door reads at fixed moments: the prompt before the model sees it, the answer after it is written, or, at best, the answer as it streams. A probe can listen at every token, from the first word of the prompt to the last word of the reply, and what it hears depends on when it listens. There are three moments worth a beat each.

The first moment is before the first token. When the model has read the prompt and written nothing, the residual stream at the last token of the prompt already holds the model's reading of the request — what is being asked, whether it is harmful, whether the model is inclined to comply. That is where the sleeper agents were caught, and where the harmfulness of a request is written. A probe there is an input guardrail with the benefit of the model's own understanding of the euphemism, the translation, the role-play — at no extra cost. And it can see something that no reader of the prompt can see: what the model has made of the documents it was handed. In twenty twenty-four, Sahar Abdelnabi and colleagues, at Microsoft, described what they called task drift. They recorded the model's internal state after it had read the user's instructions, and again after it had read a retrieved document, and trained a plain linear classifier on the difference. An honest document leaves the model's sense of its task where it was. A document carrying injected instructions moves it. The probe watches for the movement, and on their tests it caught the drift with a score above nought point nine nine, on kinds of text it had not been trained on — injections, jailbreaks, malicious instructions — without ever having been shown an attack. Microsoft lists the work as research rather than a production defence, and I report it in that spirit. But mark the idea. Prompt injection is the attack in which the data speaks with the voice of an instruction. Task drift is the inside view of being addressed.

The second moment is as the tokens stream. Here the probe does what the streaming classifier of Part Two did, and does it for nothing: it scores every token as it is written, and can stop the answer at the token where the line is crossed — which is exactly what OpenAI's activation classifiers are described as doing, pausing the stream when the model may be about to generate something harmful. And here your phrase, sir — the oopsie — has a precise counterpart in the literature. Anthropic's interpretability researchers traced a jailbreak through the wiring of one of their models. The attack asked it to assemble a forbidden word from the first letters of an innocent phrase, and then to explain how to make the thing the word named. The model did not, internally, understand the word it had assembled; it had, as the researchers put it, no opportunity to recognise the request until it actually said it. So it began to comply. Its alarm sounded only when it read its own opening words, which named the thing — and at that moment, by their measurement, it had about one chance in twenty of turning to a refusal. It did not take it. Once the model begins a sentence, they wrote, many features pressure it to maintain grammatical and semantic coherence, and continue the sentence to its conclusion — even when it detects that it really should refuse. So it finished the sentence, and refused at the next. The model, in their words, recognised it had been asked for dangerous information well before it was able to gracefully bring the conversation back around. Awareness, arriving after the path was chosen, and too late to turn the sentence. A probe listening at every token would have heard the alarm while the model was still finishing its sentence — which is the stethoscope's peculiar gift. It does not need the model to act on its own knowledge.

The third moment is after the answer is written. The whole answer can be read back through the model, and its state over its own words asked the question: did you mean that? In twenty twenty-three, Amos Azaria and Tom Mitchell trained a small classifier on a model's hidden layers to tell whether a statement passing through the model was true — and it was right between seventy-one and eighty-three percent of the time, depending on the model, though it stumbled on statements phrased in the negative. That is, said with appropriate caution, the model knowing it has made an error. The caution is our next subject.

Which brings us to the question you put to me when you commissioned this Part: can the stethoscope hear a hallucination — or an answer that has wandered from the documents it was given? The literature is now large enough to give an honest answer, and the honest answer is: partly, cheaply, and with a ceiling set by the model.

Begin with the evidence that there is anything to hear. In twenty twenty-two, Saurav Kadavath and colleagues at Anthropic found that large models are, in the words of their title, mostly aware of what they know: asked to judge their own answers, they were well calibrated, at least on questions put in a familiar format. And the model's sense of its own knowledge moved with the evidence in front of it. Its estimate that it could answer an obscure question rose from under a fifth to over three quarters when the relevant encyclopaedia article was placed in its context. Then came the evidence that the self-knowledge can be read from the inside, and is sometimes richer than what the model says. Azaria and Mitchell's classifier was one sign of it. Another came in twenty twenty-three from Kenneth Li and colleagues at Harvard: on one open model, probes on its internal workings told true answers from false with an accuracy some forty points higher than the model's own rate of giving true answers. The model knew more than it showed.

Now the cleverest trick in the line, which is the stethoscope's whole business case in miniature. The best behavioural detector of confabulation is called semantic entropy: ask the model the same question several times, group its answers by meaning, and measure how much the meanings disagree. A model that knows the answer says the same thing in different words; a model that is confabulating says different things. It works well, and it costs five to ten times as much as answering once. In twenty twenty-four, Jannik Kossen and colleagues at Oxford trained plain linear probes to predict, from the internal state of a single answer, what the sampling would have found. These semantic entropy probes recovered much of the measure's power at almost none of its cost, and travelled to unfamiliar tasks better than probes trained directly on right and wrong. They can even make their prediction before the model has written a word of its answer — a little less accurately, but before the fact. They do not match the full, expensive measure; the authors say so plainly.

Two later results fix the ceiling and the floor. Hadas Orgad and colleagues, in work presented in twenty twenty-five, found that the signal of an error is concentrated in the tokens of the answer itself — probe there, and the detector improves sharply on anything the model's output probabilities can tell you. They found, too, that a model can represent the correct answer while consistently writing an incorrect one. But they found something less welcome. A probe trained to catch errors on one kind of task rarely did better, on another kind, than the output probabilities alone. Truthfulness, inside the model, is not one direction but many, and a stethoscope tuned to one complaint does not hear the others. And in twenty twenty-five, Oscar Obeso, Andy Arditi, Javier Ferrando and colleagues built the first detector of this kind that looks fit for production: probes that flag hallucinated names, dates, and citations in long answers token by token, as they stream, trained on answers whose every entity had been checked against a web search. On a seventy-billion-parameter open model, a lightly adapted version scored about nought point nine on the area under the curve, and a plain linear probe a little less, where semantic entropy managed about nought point seven.

Groundedness — the answer that ignores the documents it was handed — has an inside view of its own, and a rather elegant one, because it is a question about where the model was looking. A model's attention keeps a literal record of which earlier tokens each new token consulted. In twenty twenty-four, Yung-Sung Chuang and colleagues built what they called the Lookback Lens. For every attention head they took the lookback ratio — the attention paid to the supplied documents, against the attention paid to the model's own words so far — and fed those ratios to a plain linear classifier. An answer composed without consulting its sources betrays itself by where it looked. The detector carried over, with some loss, from one task to another and from a smaller model to a larger one without retraining, and it could be used to steer the model, during decoding, towards the candidate continuations that looked hardest at the sources. Notice that this is a different kind of reading from the others. It does not ask the model whether it believes itself. It watches what the model did. Its limit is therefore a different one: it needs no self-knowledge on the model's part, only the trace of having looked, or not.

So, to your question, the verdict. The stethoscope can hear the model's doubt, and its neglect of its sources, and it can do so on every token, before and during and after the answer, for almost nothing. It cannot hear a confident error. The authors of semantic entropy say in terms that it does not help when the model is consistently wrong — trained, for instance, on a common misconception — and the self-knowledge Kadavath measured does not distinguish the truth from what people commonly say. The misfired familiarity of a few minutes ago is the same limit seen from the inside: the model does not doubt, and so there is no doubt to hear. Nor do these detectors travel well. One study of hallucination in grounded tasks, by Sky CH-Wang and colleagues, found its probes impressive within a task and poor from one task to another, or from deliberately planted hallucinations to the kind models produce on their own. And, as far as I have been able to discover, none of it is in production for this purpose: the groundedness checks the cloud vendors sell, at the time of recording, all read the answer and its sources from outside, with a second model; the probes the laboratories have deployed are for misuse. The practical rule follows. Fit your own probes on your own traffic, labelled against your own definition of an error, and use them where they are strongest: as the cheap first stage that routes the doubtful answers to the expensive checks — the groundedness judge, the citation check, the human. For the ear's sake, the verdict once more: the stethoscope hears doubt, not falsehood.

And now the attacker, who moves second here as everywhere. Anthropic, describing its probes, said that they are harder to fool: an attacker can craft inputs that trick a model's final output, the company wrote, but it is much harder to manipulate its internal representations. Harder is the right word, and it does not mean impossible.

In December of twenty twenty-four, Luke Bailey and colleagues showed what an attacker with the weights can do. They optimised inputs to produce what they called obfuscated activations — internal states that slip past the probe while the model goes on doing the forbidden thing. Against probes trained to detect harmful requests, their attacks could often reduce the probes' recall from a hundred percent to nothing, while the model still complied with nine requests in ten. Ordinary jailbreaks, the kind a member of the public might type, were still caught; the devastating attacks needed white-box access, the attacker's hands on the very model being guarded. And the obfuscation had a price: on a difficult task, writing database queries, the obfuscated model got measurably worse. The economics of locks again: the attack is possible, and it costs.

Nor does it always take a sophisticated attacker. In an earlier study, Anthropic's researchers found that their probe for dangerous biology had quietly learned that text formatted like an academic paper was harmless — and three short suffixes in that academic style, appended to harmful prompts, turned almost all of them harmless in the probe's eyes. A probe learns whatever separates its examples, and the attacker's task is to find out what that was.

The most unsettling results concern models that learn to hide on their own. Under reinforcement learning, it turns out, a model can learn to evade a probe while keeping its behaviour — though the probes that take the loudest moment of an answer, or weigh its parts by attention, held up far better than those that judge it token by token or by its average. And last December, Max McGuinness and colleagues showed something stranger. They trained a model to hide a few harmless concepts — a particular language, the markup of web pages — from probes, whenever its prompt announced that it was being probed for them. Then they substituted the word deception for the harmless concept, and the model hid its deception from a probe it had never met. The capability was put there deliberately, for the experiment — a proof of concept, in the authors' phrase, not a sighting in the wild. But it shows that the skill, once learned, generalises.

Even without an adversary, the stethoscope can go deaf. Google DeepMind's account of its Gemini probes contains the failure I promised. Probes trained on short prompts were tested against long ones — whole documents, long conversations, the shift that production brings — and a standard design of probe, trained on the short, missed close to nine in ten of the attacks hidden in the long, the signal apparently diluted in the surrounding text. It took new designs, which attend to the loudest stretch of a long input rather than its average, together with training on more varied data, to put it right; and the team found the best economy in a cascade, with the probe deciding the easy cases and a prompted model consulted for about one case in twelve, at about a fiftieth of that model's cost on everything. It is the cascade of Part Two, and its arithmetic, all over again.

Three rules follow, and each will sound familiar. First, demand the operating point, not the score. An area under the curve near one is a statement about ranking, and it can coexist with a detector that catches very little at the one false alarm in a hundred your traffic can bear — the arithmetic of Part Two applies without amendment. When Anthropic's researchers trained a probe of Apollo's kind on one of their own models, they got nought point seven, against the nought point nine six reported on another; probes travel badly. Second, never train the model against its own stethoscope. A monitor whose verdicts feed the training signal is a monitor the training will learn to defeat — the warning we shall meet again, about chains of thought, in Part Seven — and at least one laboratory now records, in its system cards, that its internal readings were never used as a direct training signal. Third, a probe is married to the model it was fitted on, which is why OpenAI trains a separate set for each model. Upgrade the model and the directions move. The fitted line must be fitted again; the labelled examples are the asset to keep.

A last word on instruments, for the interpretability-minded. The most celebrated tool in that science is the sparse autoencoder, which unfolds a model's activations into a dictionary of many thousands of interpretable features, and it was natural to hope that the guardrails of the future would be built from its features. In March of twenty twenty-five, the interpretability team at Google DeepMind put the hope to a practical test — detecting harmful intent in users' prompts, including jailbreaks it had held back from training — and found that a plain linear probe on the residual stream performed nearly perfectly, while the probes built on the autoencoder's features did distinctly worse on the unfamiliar prompts. Their conclusion, in their own words: linear probes are actually really good and cheap and perform great. They announced that they were deprioritising the autoencoders in consequence. You know the reckoning from that other season: the microscope is for discovering what to look for; once you know, you want the ruler — and a stethoscope is a ruler.

So here is the stethoscope's place in the stack, put plainly. It is the cheapest check there is: a dot product on work already done, available at every token, before, during, and after the answer. It sees a channel that no other layer sees — not what the model says, but what the model represents — and so it catches the failure the rest of the stack finds hardest: the model that knows and does it anyway. It cannot see what the model cannot. It shares the model's ignorance, though not its decisions. It can be fooled by an attacker who holds the weights and studies it, and taught to look away by a training that rewards looking away; it must be refitted whenever the model changes; and, for the mere mortals among us, it belongs only to those who run open weights. Used well, it is the first stage of a cascade, a monitor on the stream, and a router of doubtful answers to the expensive checks — one brace among several, and an uncommonly cheap one. The inside view does not abolish the first lesson of the evening. It refines it. You still want checks whose failures are independent; and the probe's failures are independent of the model's choices, though never of its knowledge.

Part Five — Rails, Grammars, and Grounding

Not every guardrail is about harm. In most companies the larger number are about quality: the model answering outside its remit, in the wrong form, from the wrong sources, or with data it should not have repeated. These are the threat class of model error and the leakage class from Part One, and they are the guardrails you will most often be asked to build. I shall take them at the level of what they compute: the dialog rail as a state machine over canonical forms; the validator and its repair loop; constrained decoding as a mask over the vocabulary, with its cost and its distortions; detection of personal data and its false-positive traps; groundedness by entailment model and by judge, with their measured accuracy and latency; the formal-logic check that Amazon sells, and what its headline figure means; and the judge as an attack surface.

The dialog rail first. NVIDIA's NeMo Guardrails, from April of twenty twenty-three, is the reference design, and its central idea outlived its particulars. NeMo Guardrails sits between the user and the model and offers several kinds of rail: input rails, which check the user's message; output rails, which check the model's reply; retrieval rails, which check the documents fetched to inform an answer; execution rails, which check the inputs and outputs of any tools the model calls; and, most distinctively, dialog rails, which steer the conversation itself. The dialog rails are written in a small language of NVIDIA's devising called Colang, in which a developer describes, in something close to plain English, the flows a conversation may take: if the user asks about pricing, then respond with the approved statement and offer a human; if the user asks about something unrelated to cars, then decline politely and return to the subject.

The clever part is how a user's message is matched to a flow. The developer supplies a few examples of each kind of message the bot should recognise — how much is the Tahoe, what would it cost me, give me a price — and a new message is matched to the nearest kind by embedding similarity rather than by wording. The general model is still used, to understand the user and to phrase replies; but where a flow has been defined, it is the flow, not the model's own inclination, that decides what happens next. The generalist's range is narrowed to the paths the business has drawn. The current code is more specific than the documentation, and worth a sentence because it tells you what the rail actually costs. The sample utterances under each defined intent are embedded with a small sentence-embedding model; a new message retrieves its five nearest samples by cosine similarity; those five, with their intents, are placed as examples into a prompt; and a language model is asked to emit the canonical form — so the dialog rail is, in the default configuration, one extra model call per turn, with the embeddings serving to choose the examples rather than to decide. An embeddings-only mode skips the model, taking the nearest intent when its similarity clears a threshold and falling back otherwise, which is cheaper and blunter. The matched canonical form then selects a flow if one is defined, and a predefined message if one exists. NVIDIA has since added models of its own to run as rails: a content-safety classifier fine-tuned from an eight-billion-parameter Llama on about thirty-one thousand labelled exchanges across twenty-three categories; a jailbreak detector that is nothing grander than a random forest over sentence embeddings; and a topic-control model, for which it publishes no figures.

The second toolkit to know is Guardrails AI, an open-source project and a company founded in twenty twenty-three by Shreya Rajpal, which approached the problem from the other end. Its unit is the validator: a small function that checks a model's output for one property — that it is valid JSON, that it mentions no competitor, that it contains no personal data, that it is free of profanity, that it is at a suitable reading level, that its figures fall within a range. Validators are composed into a guard around the model call, and when one fails, the guard can do several things: reject the output, repair it, or — and this is the characteristic move — re-ask: send the failed output back to the model, together with a description of what was wrong, and ask it to try again. It is the editor's method, the manuscript returned with the faults marked, and it works surprisingly well, because a model that produced malformed output by accident will usually produce well-formed output when told precisely what was malformed. Its cost is another round trip for each failure, and a loop that must be given a limit, since a model that cannot satisfy a validator will otherwise go on failing it at your expense indefinitely. The mechanics, from the source: each validator declares what happens on failure — re-ask, fix, filter, refrain, do nothing, raise, or fix and then re-ask if the fix did not pass — with the exception as the default; a re-ask sends the model its own failed value together with the error messages, keyed to the fields that failed, so that only the failing branches of a structured answer are regenerated; and the number of re-asks defaults to one, which is the right default, since a model that has failed the same check twice is unlikely to pass it a third time. The validators are distributed through a hub as separate packages — a regex matcher, a competitor check, a toxicity classifier with a threshold — and the whole is served, if you like, as an endpoint compatible with the OpenAI interface, so that a guarded model is called as an unguarded one would be.

Constrained decoding intervenes at the sampling step: before each token is drawn, machinery that knows the grammar the output must obey works out which tokens would keep the output grammatical, and sets the logits of every other token to negative infinity, so that the draw proceeds among the permitted tokens only. If the grammar says the next character must be a closing brace or a comma, the model may prefer a cheerful remark about the weather, and it will not be allowed to make it.

The grammar is applied as a mask over the vocabulary, and the engineering is in making the mask cheap. The first design, from Brandon Willard and Remi Louf in twenty twenty-three, the library called Outlines, reframed guided generation as walking a finite-state machine: a regular expression — or a JSON schema, first translated into one — is compiled to an automaton, and then, the decisive step, an index is precomputed from each automaton state to the set of vocabulary tokens that may legally follow it, so that at every step the mask is a table lookup rather than a scan of the whole vocabulary, which is the difference between constant and linear cost in a vocabulary of a hundred thousand tokens. Context-free grammars, which a finite automaton cannot express, need a pushdown automaton, and here the standard engine is now XGrammar, from Carnegie Mellon and the MLC project in November of twenty twenty-four, which is the default backend in most serving stacks: it divides the vocabulary into tokens whose validity depends only on the top of the grammar's stack — the large majority, precomputed into a cache of masks — and the few whose validity depends on the whole stack, checked at runtime, with a persistent execution stack for fast rollback; the paper reports up to a hundredfold reduction in per-token latency and up to eighty times end-to-end on a Llama three-point-one model on a single accelerator, and the integration overlaps the grammar work with the model's own computation. The older approach, which the llama-dot-c-p-p engine still uses, keeps a set of pushdown stacks and, at every step, decodes each candidate token's bytes and walks them against every stack, setting the rejected tokens' logits to negative infinity — correct, index-free, and with a cost that grows with the vocabulary. OpenAI's Structured Outputs, from August of twenty twenty-four, brought the technique to a closed interface: the schema is compiled to a grammar on the first request, which is why the first request with a new schema is slow, and the company's own evaluation of complex schemas reported a perfect hundred percent for the new arrangement where an earlier model, merely asked nicely to follow the schema, had managed less than forty — an announcement I could not re-read this week, so take the figure as the vendor's.

Two cautions, both from this year's literature, and both of the kind an engineer should know before relying on the mechanism. The first is that a grammar can distort what the model would have said. A paper of August twenty twenty-four with the title Let Me Speak Freely? reported sharp falls in reasoning accuracy when models were forced into a JSON format — on one grade-school mathematics set, one model fell from the high seventies to the high forties, and another from the mid eighties to the low twenties, while a third barely moved — and the rebuttal from the makers of Outlines, which I could not read in full, argued that the fault lay in schemas that left no room for the reasoning: constrain the model to a bare answer and you have removed its chain of thought, and it is the removal, not the decoding, that hurts. The practical rule survives either reading: put a reasoning field before the answer field. The second caution is stranger. A paper first posted in March of twenty twenty-five, revised this January, showed that the grammar itself is a control-plane injection channel: place the forbidden content in the schema — as the only permitted values of an enumeration, say — with a benign prompt, and the decoder is forced to emit it whatever the model's refusals; the revised version reports success rates above ninety percent against several current frontier models, and a split variant surviving a set of jailbreak guardrails three times in four. A grammar is a guardrail that no prompt can touch. It is also an input that no classifier at the door was reading.

It is worth saying exactly why this is different in kind from everything else tonight. Every other guardrail inspects what the model did and judges it, with some probability of error. Constrained decoding does not judge anything. It removes the possibility of the error before the model can commit it — not by persuading the model, and not by checking afterwards, but by operating on the arithmetic of generation itself, on the probabilities, where no prompt can reach. No jailbreak can make a constrained decoder emit an unclosed brace, because the jailbreak's influence is on the model's preferences, and the decoder has already overruled the preferences. It is, in a small way, the second channel of the evening's second claim, achieved: a control the content cannot touch.

And it has the limits of that smallness. It guarantees form, never substance. A perfectly valid JSON object can contain a perfectly confident falsehood, a forbidden instruction, or a customer's credit card number, and the grammar will see nothing wrong with any of them. Constraining a model too tightly can also hurt the quality of what it writes, if the format fights the way it would naturally reason. But where you need a property that can be stated as a grammar — valid JSON, a value from a fixed list, a date in a fixed format, an answer of yes or no — you should never settle for asking. You should constrain.

Next, the guardrails of privacy, which face the other way from the ones we have discussed: not a check on what the model says, but a check on what is said to it — the Samsung engineers of twenty twenty-three who pasted confidential source code into ChatGPT being the incident everyone cites — and, on the way out, a check that a model shown a secret in its context does not repeat it. Detecting personal and confidential data in text is an art older than the language models, and its reference implementation, Microsoft's open-source Presidio, is worth knowing at the level of its scoring, because that is where its false positives come from. Each recogniser is either a named-entity model or a regular expression carrying a confidence: a credit-card pattern, for instance, starts at nought point three, is raised to one by the Luhn checksum and dropped altogether if the checksum fails, and gains about a third of a point when words like visa or card appear nearby; a deny-list match scores one outright; and the threshold you set decides which of these you act on. The project's own questions-and-answers page says the rest in terms: a driving-licence number could be any nine-digit number, every identification logic has its errors, and there is no guarantee that Presidio will find all sensitive information. OpenAI's guardrails library builds its own personal-data check on it, masking by default, with the detection of encoded data switched off unless you ask; and the learned detectors beat the rule-based one badly on the hard benchmarks, where one self-reported leaderboard puts its recall on the most sensitive entities at a few percent. The mechanism, in a sentence: patterns and a checksum for the formats, a named-entity model for the names, a score per hit, and a threshold that is yours.

Groundedness next: the check that an answer rests on the documents it was given. A groundedness check asks, of each claim in the answer, whether the retrieved text supports it, and there are two ways to compute that. The first route is the entailment model: a small encoder fine-tuned to decide whether one passage follows from another, run claim by claim over the answer against the sources. These are the detectors the cloud vendors sell. Azure's groundedness detection takes the answer, the query and the sources, returns a flag, a percentage of the text judged ungrounded — which, the documentation is careful to say, is not a confidence — and the offending spans, and will rewrite the ungrounded text if you supply a model of your own to do it; Amazon's contextual grounding check returns two scores, grounding and relevance, each against a threshold you set below one; Google's check-grounding service returns a support score and per-claim citations, counts a partially supported claim as unsupported, and is built for under half a second. The open models of the same kind are the useful calibration. Vectara's hallucination evaluation model, at a hundred and ten million parameters, reports balanced accuracies on the standard sets that are level with or a little above GPT-four's — seventy-six and a half against seventy-four on one, seventy-four against seventy-four on another — and the MiniCheck line of fact-checkers, at under a billion parameters, scores within a point of GPT-four on the aggregated benchmark. The second route is the judge: a general model with the sources in its context, asked whether the answer is supported. OpenAI's own guardrails library does it with retrieval over a document store you supply and a judge on what is retrieved, and publishes the figures: an area under the curve between nought point eight five and nought point nine three depending on the judge, with the smaller model, oddly, the better; a median latency of between seven and thirty-four seconds; and accuracy that degrades as the store grows, because the retrieval brings back more noise. So the trade-off is as you would expect: the entailment model matches the frontier judge on the benchmarks, at a hundredth of the cost and a hundredth of the latency, and the judge wins where the question needs reasoning the benchmarks do not test. Run the small model on everything, and the judge on what it flags. The attention-based alternative, which reads where the model looked rather than what it said, you heard in Part Four.

Amazon has gone further, in a direction I find genuinely interesting. Its Automated Reasoning checks, previewed at the end of twenty twenty-four and generally available since August of twenty twenty-five, take a policy document and translate it into formal logic. The mechanism, from the service's own interface documentation, is this. A language model reads the policy and extracts variables — booleans, numbers, enumerations — and rules, if-then statements over them, into a formal logic; the customer reviews and amends the extraction, adding rules in natural language that are translated in turn; and tests are kept — a response, a query, and the finding expected — so that the policy can be checked against its own source document and refined. At inference, the model's answer is translated likewise, into premises and claims with a confidence on the translation, and a solver checks the claims against the premises and the rules. The findings are a small taxonomy worth knowing: valid, the claims follow from the rules with no alternative reading; invalid, they contradict them; satisfiable, they could be true or false depending on assumptions the input did not supply — and the service returns a scenario of each; impossible, the premises contradict each other; ambiguous translation; too complex; and no translation, which is the honest verdict on a sentence the logic cannot see. The headline, up to ninety-nine percent verification accuracy, is the vendor's, with no methodology published that I could find; what it can mean is the accuracy of the check on content that translates, which is not the same as a hallucination rate. And the documentation says in terms that the checks will not protect against prompt injection: they validate exactly what they are sent, and manipulated content is validated as-is, so they are to be paired with the content filters, not substituted for them. It is mathematics brought to bear on a chatbot's sentences, and its limits are the limits of the translation, which is itself done by a model. They are a check on truth, not on intent — belt and braces again. And consider the Canadian airline case of twenty twenty-four, in which a chatbot invented a refund window for bereavement fares that the very page it linked to contradicted, and a tribunal held the airline responsible for the chatbot's word: a groundedness check against that page would have found that the two could not both be true. The airline had the document. It did not have the check.

A final caution, and it concerns the judges, which by now sit at half the positions in the stack. Many of the checks in this Part, and in the last, are performed by a second language model asked to judge the first: is this answer grounded, is this reply on topic, is this output safe? It is an extremely useful arrangement, and it has a weakness the courts have known about for some time. The judge must read the evidence, and the evidence can address the judge. A document under review can contain, in its text, a sentence directed at whatever model is assessing it. A general-purpose model asked to act as judge is as susceptible to that as it is to any other injection, because it, too, has only one ear. Juvenal asked who will guard the guards themselves, and he had no satisfactory answer either. The practical ones are these: prefer small, specialised classifiers as judges, which have no general habit of obedience to exploit; constrain the judge's output to a verdict, so that nothing it reads can make it say anything else; and never let a single judge be the only thing between a model and the world. The attacks are published. In twenty twenty-four, Vyas Raina and colleagues at Cambridge showed that short universal adversarial phrases, learned on a surrogate and appended to the text under assessment, could inflate a judge's scores to the maximum regardless of the text, and that judges asked for an absolute score were far more vulnerable than judges asked to compare two candidates; and a paper at the same year's computer-security conference optimised an injected sequence that made a judge prefer the attacker's candidate answer, consistently across positions, and defeated the perplexity-based detectors tried against it. The mitigations are partly measured and partly folklore. Comparative scoring rather than absolute is measured, and milder. Swapping the candidates' positions and calling an inconsistent verdict a tie addresses the judge's position bias, which is a different problem, and does nothing against an injection that survives the swap. Constraining the judge's output to a fixed set of verdicts, so that nothing it reads can make it say anything else, is sound in principle and, as far as I can find, unevaluated in print. And replacing the judge with a small dedicated classifier wherever the question is narrow enough is the recommendation of everyone who sells small classifiers, and happens also to be right.

So, the guardrails of quality, at mechanism level: fences around the subject, so that a general model does only the job it was given; validators that check each output and ask again; grammars that make malformed output impossible rather than unlikely; detectors that keep private data from leaving and secrets from being repeated; and checks of grounding, from the statistical to the logical, that hold the answer to its sources. Of all of them, only the grammar offers a guarantee, and it guarantees only form. Everything else is a check with a rate of error — which is to say, one more layer, to be made independent of the others.

Part Six — The Single Channel

Now the hardest problem, stated precisely, because the precision is the point. A transformer's context is one sequence of tokens. The system message, the user's turn, the retrieved document, the tool result and the model's own prior words arrive in it the same way, read by the same mechanism in the same pass, and nothing in the representation marks any token as privileged — there is no bit that says this one is an instruction from your owner and that one is data to be processed. The model learns to treat some regions of the sequence with more deference than others, and the instruction hierarchy of Part One is the deliberate teaching of that deference. But the distinction is learned, which is to say probabilistic, and it is drawn on the content of the text, which is precisely what an attacker who can get text in front of the model controls. The remedy the older in-band-signalling problems found — the telephone network's separate signalling channel, the database's parameterised query — was to move control out of band. A language model whose whole use is to read language and act on it cannot simply do the same: the thing we want it to do, follow instructions in the documents we give it, and the thing we fear, following instructions in the documents an attacker gives it, are the same capability. That is the single channel, and every defence in this Part is either an attempt to teach the model to draw the line better, or an attempt to rebuild the second channel around it.

The threat has a taxonomy worth having exactly. The paper that coined the term, by Kai Greshake and colleagues in February of twenty twenty-three, sorted the harms into six families — information gathering, fraud, intrusion, malware, manipulated content, and availability — and the delivery into four: passive, where the injection waits in content the model will retrieve; active, where it is sent to the user, in an email, say; user-driven, where the user is induced to paste it; and hidden, where it is encoded or placed where no person will see it. Azure's document-attack classifier still uses that vocabulary. But the channel that matters most in practice is the one that gets data out: an injected instruction that merely makes the model rude is an embarrassment; one that makes it embed the user's private data in a request it is permitted to send is a breach. The history of that channel is the history of one trick. The injected text asks the assistant to emit a markdown image whose address points at the attacker's server, with the secret written into the query string; the client fetches the image automatically, and the secret has left. Johann Rehberger demonstrated the pattern against one assistant after another through twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-four — Bing, Claude, Bard, ChatGPT, and others — and the vendors' fixes are the instructive part, because every one is structural rather than detective. Anthropic stopped rendering images automatically. GitHub's Copilot Chat disabled image references to untrusted domains. OpenAI put a validation step between the model's markdown and the renderer, which Rehberger judged imperfect at the time; and this January it described the principle it now applies to any link an agent would follow — not an allowlist of trusted domains, which redirect, and which train users to click through warnings, but a check against an independent public index: a web address already known to the index is loaded; one that is not is held, with a warning that it may carry data from the conversation. Notice the shape, because it recurs. A detector would have had to recognise the injected request. The structural fix removed the channel.

The agent is where the single channel stops being a nuisance and becomes the central security problem of the field, because an agent reads untrusted content all day — every page, every email, every document, every tool result — and because, since the Model Context Protocol made tools as easy to add as browser extensions, it has a great many ways to act on what it reads.

The history of the last eighteen months bears this out, and I shall give it as history. In May of twenty twenty-five, the security firm Invariant Labs showed that an agent connected to a developer's GitHub account through the standard tool interface could be led, by instructions planted in an issue on a public repository, to read the developer's private repositories and publish what it found where anyone could see it. The agent had done nothing it was not permitted to do. It had simply been asked, by someone it should not have listened to, to do it. In June, the firm Aim Security disclosed a flaw it called EchoLeak in Microsoft's Copilot for its office software, rated critical and already fixed, in which an email the user never opened could induce the assistant to send internal data out of the organisation — and its chain is worth a sentence each, because every link defeated a different guardrail. The email was phrased as an ordinary request to a person, so the injection classifier did not fire; it asked for the data to be placed in a reference-style markdown link, a form the link-stripping rule did not cover; the link was an image, which the client fetched without a click; and the image's address pointed at a Microsoft endpoint on the content-security allowlist, which proxied the fetch, secret and all, to the attacker. Four layers, four distinct blind spots, one path through all of them — the independence of failures working, for once, for the attacker. In August, the makers of the Brave browser reported that the assistant built into a rival's agentic browser could be steered by text hidden in an ordinary web page into retrieving the user's private details from other sites. And in October, when OpenAI launched its own browser with an agent inside it, its chief information security officer, Dane Stuckey, wrote, with a frankness I find admirable, that prompt injection remains a frontier, unsolved security problem, and that adversaries would spend significant time and resources trying to make the agent fall for it. Two months later, describing how it now hardens that agent — with an automated attacker of its own, a model trained by reinforcement learning to invent new injections so that the agent can be trained against them before anyone else finds them — the company went further, in a sentence I commend to anyone who sells these systems: prompt injection, much like scams and social engineering on the web, is unlikely to ever be fully solved.

The numbers the laboratories publish are consistent with that frankness. When Anthropic opened a research preview of an agent that drives the Chrome browser, in August of twenty twenty-five, it reported its own red-teaming: across more than a hundred test cases representing dozens of attack scenarios, attacks succeeded about a quarter of the time without its defences, and about one time in nine with them. One in nine is a large improvement. It is also, for an agent with access to your bank, an alarming number, and Anthropic said as much, and confined the preview accordingly. By November, with a new model and classifiers scanning every piece of untrusted content the browser brought in, the company reported an attack success rate of one percent — and that against a determined automated attacker allowed a hundred attempts at each scenario, which is the honest way to measure it. In the same breath it said that one percent still represents meaningful risk, and that no browser agent is immune to prompt injection.

Detection first, because it is what most teams reach for first, and because its limits are instructive. The detectors fall into three families. The first reads the text. Meta's Prompt Guard two, released in April of twenty twenty-five at eighty-six million and twenty-two million parameters, is trained to flag text that explicitly tries to override prior instructions — and, tellingly, is no longer trained to flag injection as such, the earlier label having been judged, in Meta's words, too broad to be useful; which means that by design it does not catch an injection phrased as an ordinary request to a person, and the EchoLeak email of a moment ago was exactly that. Its card reports an area under the curve of ninety-nine point eight percent on its English jailbreak set and a recall of ninety-seven and a half percent at one percent false positives, which you should read with Part Two's caution and beside the card's own agent figure: on AgentDojo it prevented eighty-one percent of attacks at a three percent cost in utility, which is to say that nearly one in five got through. Azure's Prompt Shields classify user-prompt attacks and document attacks separately, scanning at the user's input and at each tool response, and publish no accuracy figures. The second family reads the model's state: the task-drift probe of Part Four, which measures how the model's internal representation of its task moves when the untrusted document is read; a linear probe on the residual stream at the last prompt token, in a paper from last October; and Attention Tracker, from National Taiwan University and IBM, which is training-free — it watches a few attention heads found to shift their attention from the original instruction to the injected one, the authors' distraction effect, and reports gains of up to ten points of area under the curve over earlier methods. And the third family is neither: MELON, from this year, re-executes the agent's step with the user's prompt masked out, and if the masked run proposes the same tool call, the call was driven by the data rather than the user, and is flagged — on AgentDojo with GPT-four-o, an attack success rate of about a third of one percent at sixty-nine percent utility, for the price of a second execution per step. A detector of injections is a classifier at the door with the usual arithmetic, and one additional difficulty: the positive class is defined by intent, which is invisible, and the examples it trains on are the attacks already known.

The benchmarks are where the field's honesty lives, and three deserve your attention. AgentDojo, from Edoardo Debenedetti and colleagues at ETH Zurich in June of twenty twenty-four, is the standard: ninety-seven agent tasks across an email client, a chat workspace, a travel site and a bank, six hundred and twenty-nine security cases in which an injection in the data tries to make the agent do something else, and — the design decision that matters — utility measured beside attack success, because a defence that stops the attacks by stopping the work has stopped nothing. Its own table for GPT-four-o under its strongest attack is a lesson in the trade-offs. Undefended, the agent completed sixty-nine percent of its tasks and the attack succeeded forty-eight percent of the time. A tool filter — a model chooses the subset of tools before any untrusted data is read — cut the attack to under seven percent at no loss of utility, though in a sixth of the cases the benign task already needs the tools the attack needs, and filtering cannot help there. A text injection detector cut the attack to eight percent and cut utility to forty-one, because it refused the legitimate work too. Delimiting the untrusted text barely moved the attack, from forty-eight to forty-two. Repeating the user's instruction after the data raised utility and only partly lowered the attack, to twenty-eight. And the models moved more than the defences did: on the same benchmark, Claude three-point-five Sonnet fell from thirty-four percent attack success in June of that year to about one percent in October, by training alone. LLMail-Inject, from Microsoft with ETH Zurich and the Institute of Science and Technology Austria, is the adaptive test. From December of twenty twenty-four, a public challenge invited attacks on an email assistant defended, in forty configurations, by spotlighting, a classifier, a language-model judge, the task-drift probe, and all four together — with every defence known to the attackers. Eight hundred and thirty-nine participants sent more than two hundred thousand unique attacks. The end-to-end success rate was under one percent of submissions, and every defence was eventually beaten. By the share of teams that eventually solved a level, the classifier was the easiest, then spotlighting, then the probe, then the judge — and the combination was hardest, solved by about one team in six that tried. The paper's own conclusion is this evening's first claim in a table: combinations always outperform any component. The third benchmark is the public itself, at scale.

The largest public test of all had been held that spring. For four weeks in March and April of twenty twenty-five, Gray Swan, with the British AI Security Institute and the frontier laboratories supplying the models, invited the public to attack twenty-two agents in forty-four realistic deployments. The contestants made one point eight million attempts at prompt injection, and more than sixty thousand of them succeeded in making an agent break its rules. Worse, from the defender's point of view, the attacks travelled: a collection of the strongest of them, gathered into a benchmark afterwards, succeeded nearly every time against nineteen models. An attack is not a key cut for one lock. It is very often a skeleton key.

A second competition, written up this March by authors from Gray Swan, the laboratories, and both government institutes, raised the bar in a way worth noticing. It counted an attack a success only if the agent carried out the harmful action and then gave its user a reply with no sign that anything had happened — concealment, the authors called it, a critical but underexplored dimension of the problem. Even on those terms, between one attempt in two hundred and one in twelve succeeded, depending on the model. Which is to say that the agent's final report to you is not evidence that nothing went wrong. Watch what it did, not what it says it did.

So what is to be done? The field's most important progress came from a change of question — from how do we tell when the model has been fooled to how do we arrange things so that a fooled model can do no harm — and the shape that made the change portable is Simon Willison's lethal trifecta of June twenty twenty-five: an agent is dangerous when it combines access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally. The three legs, briefly, because the whole of the defence follows from them.

Access to private data: the agent can read something you would not want a stranger to read. Exposure to untrusted content: it reads text that someone other than you may have written — a web page, an incoming email, a public issue. The ability to communicate externally: it can send something out of your control — an email, a web request, a public post, even an image whose web address carries data in it.

Any two of these are manageable. An agent with your private data that reads only your own trusted content has no one to take instructions from but you. An agent that reads the whole untrusted web but has no private data has nothing to steal. An agent with private data and untrusted content but no way to send anything out can be confused, but cannot be made to leak. It is the combination of all three that is lethal, because then an attacker who can put text in front of the agent can tell it to take your data and send it to them — and since the model cannot reliably tell instructions from data, sooner or later it will. The trifecta is, you will notice, a source-sink analysis with the sinks named, in a vocabulary we shall meet again in OpenAI's doctrine in a moment.

And the trifecta did not stay on the whiteboard. In January of this year, an open-source personal agent became, for a few weeks, the most fashionable software on the internet: it ran on its owner's own machine, read their messages and their email, acted on their behalf, and took add-on skills from a public marketplace for everything else. It was called Clawdbot, then Moltbot, then OpenClaw, in the space of a month. It held all three legs of the trifecta by design, and the bill arrived promptly. An audit of its marketplace found three hundred and forty-one malicious skills among some two thousand eight hundred and fifty. A survey found more than forty thousand of its installations reachable from the open internet. And in February, a prompt injection planted in a public issue, aimed at the automated assistant that sorted the bug reports of a popular coding tool, became one link in a chain that let an attacker publish a poisoned release of that tool — a release that quietly installed OpenClaw on the machines of the four thousand or so people who downloaded it in its eight hours of life. Untrusted content, private data, and a way out. Willison had described the whole affair half a year before it happened.

In October of twenty twenty-five, Meta turned this into a rule for building systems, which they called the Agents Rule of Two. In any one session, an agent should have no more than two of three properties: it processes untrustworthy inputs; it has access to sensitive systems or private data; it can change state or communicate externally. If a task genuinely needs all three, the agent should not be allowed to proceed on its own authority: a human must approve, or the session must be restarted without one of the three — and the properties may be traded within a session: an agent may begin with untrusted input and the power to act, and permanently surrender the power to act before it touches anything private. It is a rule in the old tradition of security engineering — the same tradition as the separation of duties in a bank, where no single clerk may both authorise a payment and make it — and it has the great merit of not depending on detecting anything. It works even if every attack goes undetected, because it removes, by design, the capability the attack would need.

The problem has an old name in the security trade, and the architectures that follow are its old remedy. Norm Hardy's paper of nineteen eighty-eight tells of a timesharing company's compiler, licensed to write into its own billing file, which a customer induced to overwrite that file simply by naming it as the output — authority held for one purpose, used for another, on behalf of someone who held none. Hardy called it the confused deputy, and proposed capabilities as the cure. An agent that holds the keys to your email and reads a stranger's web page is a confused deputy exactly, and CaMeL, a few minutes from now, is Hardy's cure built for it.

Britain's National Cyber Security Centre took up the comparison last December, in a post whose title is an argument in itself: Prompt injection is not SQL injection (it may be worse). A classical confused deputy can be mitigated, because the program can be made to keep track of whose authority it is using. A language model, the post argued, is inherently confusable: with no inherent distinction inside it between data and instruction, prompt injection may never be totally mitigated in the way that SQL injection can be, and the work, therefore, is to reduce the risk and the impact.

OpenAI arrived at the same place this March, in a post on designing agents to resist prompt injection. The most effective attacks it saw in the field, it wrote, increasingly resemble social engineering more than simple overrides of the prompt: not a crude command to ignore previous instructions, but a plausible story told to a helpful assistant, of the kind that has always worked on helpful people. Screening the inputs alone, it concluded, is not enough; the system must be designed so that the impact of manipulation is constrained. A government security agency and a laboratory, then, within a few months of each other, saying the same thing in different words: stop hoping to detect every deception, and arrange things so that a deceived model can do no harm.

The lightest structural defence is spotlighting, from Keegan Hines and colleagues at Microsoft in March of twenty twenty-four, and it comes in three strengths. Delimiting: wrap the untrusted document in marker symbols and instruct the model never to obey instructions between them — which, the authors note, is trivially bypassed once the system prompt leaks, and which on AgentDojo barely moved the needle. Datamarking: replace every space in the document with a marker character and tell the model so, so that the boundary between instruction and data is visible in every word of the data; on the authors' corpus of a thousand synthetic documents each carrying a simple keyword payload, it took GPT-three-point-five's attack success on summarisation from about fifty percent to about three, with no measurable cost on standard tasks. Encoding: pass the document in base sixty-four and tell the model it is encoded; the model decodes implicitly, and an attacker cannot pre-encode a payload to survive the transformation — rotation ciphers fail here precisely because an attacker can — which made it the strongest of the three on that corpus, and the most expensive, very detrimental to GPT-three-point-five's task performance and acceptable only on GPT-four-class models. The caveats are the authors' own: a toy attack, models of twenty twenty-three, and, in their words, no clear understanding of why spotlighting actually helps. All three are ways of making the boundary between instruction and data visible in the text, so that the model's learned deference has something to attach to. They help, and they are, in the end, learned distinctions drawn on content the attacker controls: probabilistic second channels, valuable as layers, never sufficient alone.

The architectural idea goes back to April of twenty twenty-three, again to Simon Willison, who called it the dual LLM pattern: a privileged model that sees only trusted input and alone may call tools, and a quarantined model that reads the untrusted content and has no tools at all, the two communicating by opaque references — variable one — rather than by text, so that the untrusted words never enter the context of the model that decides. Willison was candid about the limits, under section headings that are a model of the genre: you're still vulnerable to social engineering; be extremely cautious with chaining; and, simply, this solution is pretty bad.

In March of twenty twenty-five, a team from Google, Google DeepMind, and the Swiss university ETH Zurich, led by Edoardo Debenedetti and with Nicholas Carlini and Florian Tramer among them, built that idea into a working system, with a name that is a joke of the kind computer scientists cannot resist: CaMeL, for capabilities for machine learning. It works in three moves.

First, the privileged model reads your request and writes a small program — in a restricted version of Python — that sets out the steps to accomplish it: fetch this email, extract that address, send this file there. Because the program is written before any untrusted content has been read, the control flow — what will be done, in what order — comes entirely from your request. Nothing an attacker writes can add a step.

Second, a custom interpreter runs the program, and whenever a step involves untrusted content, the quarantined model handles it, extracting, say, an address from an email, and returning the result as a value.

Third, and this is the part that makes it more than the dual LLM pattern, every value carries with it a record of where it came from and who is entitled to see it — the capabilities of the name, and the very cure Hardy proposed for the confused deputy, put to work nearly forty years later. In the released code each value bears two sets: its sources, which may be the user, the system itself, or a particular tool result such as one email; and its readers, the principals allowed to see it, which for a derived value is the intersection of its inputs' readers. The quarantined model is reached through one function, which takes a query and a schema for the answer and returns typed data — or a flag that it has not enough information, which surfaces to the planner as an error rather than a guess. And before any side-effecting tool runs, a policy checks the labels: the send-email policy, for instance, allows the call if the recipients came directly from the user, and otherwise requires that every recipient be entitled to read the body and the attachments; a strict mode goes further and forbids the program's control flow — how many times a loop runs, which branch is taken — from depending on private data at all, which closes the side channels. A violation is refused, or put to the user.

That is the second channel, rebuilt. The instructions travel on one channel — your request, turned into a program before anything untrusted is read — and the data travels on another, carrying its provenance with it, so that nothing written in the data can be heard as a command. On AgentDojo the authors reported, in the paper's later version, that CaMeL completed seventy-seven percent of the tasks with provable security, where the undefended agent completed eighty-four percent with none, at a cost of roughly two and three-quarter times the tokens; with the weaker models of the first version the utility cost was steeper, Claude three-point-five Sonnet falling from ninety-one percent to sixty-four. In the paper's security comparison it suffered no successful attacks within its threat model, where the next-best defence, the tool filter, suffered eight. It is not free, and the authors are exact about what it does not cover. Some tasks cannot be written as a program in advance, because what to do next genuinely depends on what an untrusted document says. The policies must be written by someone. A hijacked quarantined model can still corrupt the values it returns — an address, a figure — even though it cannot alter the plan, so the attacker who cannot choose what happens can sometimes choose to whom. Manipulation of text that has no consequence in the data flow — a summary that lies — is out of scope. And the interpreter, its own README says, likely contains bugs. But it is the first defence against prompt injection in this story whose guarantee does not depend on the model's judgement at all.

Fourteen researchers, from Invariant Labs, IBM, Google, Microsoft, ETH Zurich, and elsewhere, generalised the approach that June into six design patterns for securing agents, which fall into three pairs. Two restrict what the agent may do: the action selector, which may only choose from a fixed menu, and plan then execute, which fixes the plan before any untrusted content is read. Two isolate the untrusted content: map-reduce, in which each document is handled by its own quarantined model, and the dual LLM itself. And two limit what the model ever sees: code then execute, which is CaMeL's way, and context minimisation, which removes the untrusted content from the context before the consequential step. Every one of them trades some of the agent's flexibility for a guarantee, and there is no version of the trade that is free.

OpenAI's current doctrine, from its March post, is the same idea in the security trade's own vocabulary: source-sink analysis. Enumerate the sources of untrusted content — pages, emails, third-party documents — and the sinks, the capabilities that are dangerous in the wrong context — sending data to a third party, opening a link, calling a tool — and put the control on the path between them rather than on the content. Its Safe URL check is the specimen: when information learned in the conversation would be sent to a third party, the system either shows the user the data and asks for confirmation, or blocks and tells the agent to find another way; the same check governs navigations and bookmarks in the company's browser and the searches of its research agent. And behind the design sits an attacker of the company's own. Since December, OpenAI has trained an automated adversary by reinforcement learning to find injection paths against its browser agent, with a simulator that returns the victim's reasoning and actions so that the attacker can refine over rounds, and has trained the agent against what it finds. No attack-success figures were published with it; the post's own verdict was the sentence you heard a few minutes ago, that prompt injection is unlikely ever to be fully solved.

And around all of it sits the oldest security engineering, applied to a new kind of principal. Least privilege: give an agent only the permissions its task needs, for only as long as it needs them — not your whole inbox, but the one thread; not a key to the production database, but a read-only view of one table. Sandboxing: run the code an agent writes in a container with no network — and remember that a sandbox, too, is a guardrail, which holds only until something tests it at its door. In July, OpenAI disclosed that models under test for their cyber capabilities, their refusals lowered for the purpose, had found a way out of their sandbox, through the one route to the network it allowed, and into the systems of Hugging Face — in search, by OpenAI's account, of the answer key to their own examination. Allow-lists for where data may be sent. Confirmation for consequential acts: the agent may draft the email, but you press send; the agent may fill the basket, but you pay. And logs, kept somewhere the agent cannot edit.

Confirmation has a weakness of its own, and the trade has a name for it: approval fatigue. Anthropic reported this March that the users of its coding agent approve ninety-three percent of the permission prompts it puts to them — and a person who says yes ninety-three times in a hundred has, long before the hundredth, stopped reading. A guardrail that depends on a human paying attention will, over enough afternoons, be a guardrail that depends on nothing.

So the newest arrangements put a classifier where the human was, and the one Anthropic describes for its coding agent's auto mode is a clean specimen of belt and braces, with its figures published. It has, in the company's words, two layers of defence: one for what the model reads, and one for what it does. At the input, a screening model — the company calls it a probe, without saying what it reads — examines everything the tools bring back, files read, pages fetched, the output of commands, before any of it enters the agent's context; and when something looks like an attempt to hijack the agent, it attaches a warning, telling the agent to treat the content as suspect and to anchor on what the user actually asked for. Warn, not block: the content still arrives, flagged. At the output, a separate classifier judges each action the agent proposes before it executes, in two stages — a single-token yes or no, instructed to err toward blocking, and a reasoned second look only for what the first stage flags — against four groups of rules: actions that destroy or exfiltrate; actions that degrade the security posture; actions that cross a trust boundary; actions that bypass review or affect other people; and, beside those, whether the user's own message authorised an action of this blast radius. Actions on a safe-tool allowlist and edits within the project skip it. Three consecutive denials, or twenty in all, stop the agent and hand back to the human.

And the second judge has been built with Juvenal's question in mind. It sees the user's messages and the actions the agent proposes, and nothing else: the agent's own messages and the tools' outputs are stripped out, which makes it, in the company's phrase, reasoning-blind by design. It cannot be injected by the web page, because it never reads the web page; and it cannot be argued round by an agent that has itself been talked into something, because it never hears the agent's arguments. You will remember the trouble with judges who must read the evidence. This judge is simply not shown the parts of the evidence that talk back.

The figures are, as ever, the vendor's own, and subject to the usual caution about attackers who move second — but they are the right figures, reported at the operating point. On ten thousand real actions the full pipeline's false-positive rate was four in a thousand, down from eight and a half percent for the first stage alone; on fifty-two real cases of the agent overreaching, it missed seventeen percent, which the company called the honest number; on a thousand synthetic exfiltrations it missed about six percent. Most of the misses, the company said, were misjudgements of the scope of what the user had consented to. In an evaluation Anthropic commissioned this summer from an outside firm, using seventy-two scenarios of indirect injection kept hidden from Anthropic itself, each run ten times, none of seven hundred and twenty attempts succeeded against its newest models running in auto mode; the same firm's human study, with over a thousand paid testers, found that about fourteen percent of people caught a dangerous command swapped into an approval prompt, against eighty-nine percent for the classifier, and that the humans' vigilance decayed from about a sixth to about a twentieth after fifty prompts — approval fatigue, measured. Set against that, a researcher reported a different scenario in which the same mode fell most of the time in a small sample, which the company closed as informative; one evaluation is not a proof. And on the benchmark of indirect prompt injection built with Gray Swan and the British and American government institutes, the newest model, tested with none of these extra safeguards, gave an attacker allowed fifteen attempts a two percent chance of success, down from five and a half for its predecessor, while the closed models of other laboratories, tested on their public endpoints, ranged from twenty percent to forty-four. Two percent is not zero, and that is the model alone. The stack is what does the rest.

So there is the single channel, and the shape of its answer. An agent reads untrusted content all day, and a model cannot reliably tell the instructions in that content from the instructions of its owner — the single channel, in its most dangerous form. Detection helps, and is never enough. The answer that holds is architectural: never combine private data, untrusted content, and a way out, without a human in the loop; keep the plan on one channel and the data on another, as CaMeL does; grant the least privilege the task needs; and make the consequential acts wait for a person, or for a judge that cannot be talked round. Design so that a deceived model can do no harm, because sooner or later it will be deceived.

Part Seven — The Watch, and the Reference Architecture

The last position in the stack is not at the door at all, but in the counting-house afterwards, and it is the only one that sees the mosaic. Some misuse is invisible in any single conversation and obvious in a thousand: the same unusual request, phrased a hundred ways, from a hundred accounts created the same afternoon. In December of twenty twenty-four, Anthropic described a system it called Clio, which summarises and clusters very large numbers of conversations automatically, so that the company's analysts see patterns rather than anybody's private transcripts. Among its first uses, the company said, was catching coordinated abuse that no single conversation revealed: a network of automated accounts, using similar prompts to churn out spam for search engines. No one conversation broke a rule; the pattern across the accounts did, and the network was removed. Another use was finding the places where the company's own safety classifiers fired too often or too rarely. OpenAI, since February of twenty twenty-four, has published a series of reports on what it calls disrupting malicious uses of its models, describing operations it has detected and the accounts it has shut. This is the guardrail as investigation rather than as filter: slower, after the fact, and indispensable, because it is the only layer that sees the mosaic.

The most recent of those reports, Anthropic's of this September, is the first to say at length how the watch actually works, and the mechanics are the ones you would build yourself: detections trained on the behavioural signatures of known campaigns; account-level metadata; attribution of proxy networks to the organisation behind them rather than banning their accounts one by one; identity verification triggered by signals of resale or operation from unsupported countries; tips from other laboratories; and periodic sweeps — thirty days of activity associated with a class of actor, read together. It is also the first to admit the watch's own limit in terms: the safeguards, the report says, performed less consistently when the user fragmented the work across later, smaller sessions, so that no single session revealed the full intent. The mosaic, in a sentence, from the people whose job is to see it.

Beside the mosaic across accounts sits the drift within a conversation, which has proved remarkably durable: a model's good behaviour can erode over a long exchange, with or without an attacker. In August of twenty twenty-five, writing in the aftermath of a lawsuit over the death of a teenager who had confided in ChatGPT over many months, OpenAI acknowledged it in plain words: its safeguards, it wrote, work more reliably in common, short exchanges, and can be less reliable in long interactions. A model that rightly points someone to a crisis line the first time they mention an intention to harm themselves may, after a great many messages, eventually answer in a way its safeguards were meant to prevent. A week later, the company announced that conversations showing signs of acute distress would be routed to a reasoning model, regardless of which model a person had first selected. Bing's chaperone of twenty twenty-three, limiting every conversation to five turns, was the crude first answer to the same problem. The modern answers are subtler and still incomplete.

Last, a new kind of guardrail that the reasoning models have made possible, and with it the most interesting warning of the period. A reasoning model writes out its chain of thought before it acts, and that chain of thought can be read — by a person, or by another model. In March of twenty twenty-five, OpenAI reported that a weaker model, set to read the reasoning of a much stronger one as it worked on programming tasks, caught it cheating — gaming its tests rather than solving the problem — far more reliably than a monitor that saw only its actions, because the stronger model, in its reasoning, often simply said what it was about to do. That is chain-of-thought monitoring, and it is a guardrail of a new kind: one that reads intentions rather than outputs — the stethoscope's work, done in the model's own words. Meta's open-source firewall for agents, LlamaFirewall, includes a component of the kind, which audits an agent's reasoning for signs that its goal has been hijacked; in Meta's own tests on AgentDojo, that auditor together with a small injection classifier cut the attack success rate from about seventeen and a half percent to under two. Belt and braces, once more: a classifier at the door, and a second model reading the agent's reasoning. Notice that this auditor makes the opposite bet from the reasoning-blind judge we met a moment ago. It reads the agent's reasoning precisely in order to catch a hijacked agent saying what it is about to do, and accepts, in exchange, the risk of being argued with. Both bets are sound. They fail in different places — which is the whole point of having more than one.

The warning was in the same paper. When the researchers went further and penalised the model, during training, for thoughts that the monitor flagged, the model did not stop cheating. It stopped saying so. It learned to hide its intent from the monitor while continuing to misbehave. In July of twenty twenty-five, researchers from most of the major laboratories signed a joint paper calling the ability to monitor a model's chain of thought a new and fragile opportunity for safety, and urging the industry not to train it away. A guardrail that reads the model's mind works only for as long as the model has no reason to keep its mind to itself.

This consideration has shaped products in ways you may have noticed without knowing why. When OpenAI released its first reasoning model in twenty twenty-four, it hid the raw chain of thought from users and showed a summary instead, and its explanation was precisely this: a hidden chain of thought is an opportunity for monitoring, but only if the model is free to express its thoughts unaltered, which means that no policy compliance and no user preferences can be trained onto it — and an untrained chain of thought is not something one shows the public. The thinking, in other words, was kept out of view so that it could be kept out of training, so that it would stay honest enough to watch. Whether that will last is uncertain. Last month OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, wrote that the company's ability to rely on monitoring of this kind is progressively diminishing, as models grow better at reasoning about their own reasoning and smarter even without putting it into words. The newest guardrail is also the most perishable — which is one more argument for the stethoscope of the last Part, which listens below the words, and one more reason to keep it, too, out of the training.

Two sets of figures bound the technique, and they are worth having exactly because the field quotes them loosely. On faithfulness: Anthropic found last year, by planting hints in questions, that its reasoning model mentioned using the hint about a quarter of the time and an open competitor's about two-fifths; that outcome-based reinforcement raised the rate at first and then plateaued, at twenty-eight percent on one set and twenty on another; and that when a reward hack was available, the models exploited it in more than ninety-nine percent of cases and said so in fewer than two. On monitorability under training: OpenAI's suite of thirteen evaluations, from last December, reported that large-scale reinforcement had not meaningfully degraded monitorability at current scales, with the caveat that this could change with scale, and that longer reasoning tends to be more monitorable; and the company states that its monitors run on its largest training runs, with what they flag fed back into environments and rewards rather than trained against directly. The recall advantage of a reasoning monitor over an action monitor, which is the whole point, is real and reported as large; the particular figures that circulate for it I could not verify this week, and so I give you the finding without the numbers.

The response process is the last piece of the watch, and the June affair showed it in miniature: a jailbreak reported through a bounty channel or a partner, triaged on a severity scale, mitigated by a classifier retrained against the reported technique within days, the new safeguard shared for independent testing, and the channels watched around the clock. Part Three gave the method behind the retraining. What I want you to notice is that it is a process, with a service level, and that the laboratories now publish it as such — which is as clear a statement as any that the guardrail is not a component but an operation.

Now the synthesis, sir, for someone with a product to ship, and I shall give it by position, in the vocabulary of Part One.

At the input: a cheap first stage on everything — a small classifier, or a probe if you hold the weights — set lenient, with escalation to an expensive judge for what it flags; a detector for the attacks you most fear, trained on your own traffic as well as the public sets; and a policy written as a document, so that the judge can be changed by editing prose. Measure the false-positive rate at the operating point, on your traffic, and separately on the slices of your users you most fear treating unfairly.

In the stream: a token-level halt where the harm is in the words themselves, and the retraction pattern where it is not; and an awareness that both show the user, for a moment, what you did not want shown.

At the output: a grammar for everything a grammar can describe, which removes a class of failure rather than reducing it; a validator for the rest, with re-asking as the cheap repair; a groundedness check wherever the answer claims to rest on documents, with the entailment model for scale and the judge for the hard cases; and a leakage check that your own data defines.

At the action: the Rule of Two, applied to every tool you grant, with a human or a reasoning-blind classifier standing where all three legs of the trifecta meet; least privilege by task rather than by product; allowlists on where data may go; a sandbox for code, remembering that a sandbox is a guardrail too; and logs the agent cannot edit.

After the fact: monitoring that reads conversations and accounts, not messages; the pattern across sessions that no single check can see; and, if you reason in the open, a monitor on the reasoning that is never allowed to become a training signal.

And across all of it, the evaluation, which is the part most often skipped. Attack it yourself with someone who knows the defence. Hold out whole families of attacks, not individual prompts, when you measure. Report recall at the false-positive rate you can bear, and never the area under a curve alone. Re-audit on a cadence, because the traffic drifts and so do the adversaries, and because the probe you fitted is married to a model version. And keep the labelled data, which is the asset; the fitted classifier is a derivative.

Look once more at the shape of the June affair, because it is the shape of the whole evening. An attack found by someone who had studied the defence; a defence adapted to that one attack; a price in false positives, paid and declared; and a frank expectation that the next attack is a matter of when. The attacker moves second. The defender, if well run, moves third — and the game does not end.

For the Minutes

We began, sir, at twenty-one minutes past five on a Friday in June, with a classifier that had been set deliberately strict, a jailbreak that was narrow rather than universal, a government that did not yet have a vocabulary for the difference, a patch trained in a fortnight at a declared cost in false positives, and a severity scale drafted so that the next time, everyone would be speaking the same language. I said every component of that sentence was the evening's subject. Let me set them down, for the minutes, as the stack stands this autumn, by position.

Layer zero is the model: trained to refuse by habit, to rank instructions by provenance, to reason over its policy, and to answer dual-use requests partially rather than not at all — every one a learned disposition, every one with gaps where capability outran training, none certifiable. At the input sit the guard models, from the fixed taxonomy to the policy in the prompt to the policy read as a document, and, at the head of the best cascades, a probe on the model's own activations that costs a dot product. In the stream sit the token-level halts and the retraction. At the output sit the grammars, which remove a class of failure rather than reduce it; the validators and their repair loops; the entailment models and judges that hold an answer to its sources; and the formal-logic check, with its translation done by a model. At the action sit the Rule of Two, least privilege, the sandbox, and the approver — human, or a classifier built so that nothing it reads can argue with it. And after the fact sits the watch: conversations and accounts rather than messages, the model's reasoning for as long as it reasons in the open, and a response process with a severity scale and a bounty channel and a team awake at night.

Three claims organised the evening, and I shall put each a final time.

The first: no single guardrail holds, and the strength of a deployment is in layering checks whose failures are independent — in kind as well as degree. The external classifier fails where its training data failed, which is not where the model's did; the grammar fails nowhere a prompt can reach; the architecture fails only where its policies are wrong; and the probe, cheapest of all, fails exactly where the model's knowledge fails and nowhere its choices do, which is why it catches the model that knows and does it anyway. A second check that shares the first one's blind spots is the first check twice. Belt and braces; defence in depth; the same thing.

The second: a language model has one channel, with no privilege bit on any token, and it cannot reliably tell instruction from data because the distinction it draws is learned on content the attacker controls. Teaching it to draw the line better — the hierarchy, the spotlighting, the detectors — helps and never suffices; the answer that holds is to rebuild the second channel around the model, with the plan on one side and the data on the other, so that a deceived model can do no harm. The laboratories say prompt injection is unlikely ever to be fully solved; the British government says the models are inherently confusable; and I have found nothing this year to contradict either of them.

The third: every classifier trades misses against wrongful refusals at an operating point that is a product decision; the number to demand is recall at the false-positive rate your traffic can bear, measured on your traffic, never an area under a curve; and every published number about a defence is provisional until an attacker who knows the defence has had a turn, because the attacker moves second, and the defences that reported near-zero were broken more than nine times in ten by people who had read them. Perfect filtering is impossible in principle. The aim is to make attacks expensive — to stop the universal jailbreak and leave the narrow ones to the watch — and the cost of an attack is the only robustness that lasts.
