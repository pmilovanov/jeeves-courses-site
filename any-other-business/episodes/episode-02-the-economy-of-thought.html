The Clock on the Table

Good day, sir.

In the summer of eighteen fifty-one, while the Great Exhibition filled the Crystal Palace in Hyde Park with the industry of all nations, a smaller exhibition of a different kind was held in London: the first international chess tournament. Its organiser, and the strongest English player of the day, was Howard Staunton — the man whose name is still on the standard design of the pieces. The tournament was won, to English dismay, by a German, Adolf Anderssen. And in the match for third place, Staunton was beaten by a fellow Englishman named Elijah Williams.

Staunton did not take it well. He complained, in print and at length, that Williams had beaten him not by playing better but by playing slower — that Williams would sit over a single move for an hour, or two, or, by Staunton's account, longer still, until his opponent's concentration and patience were both exhausted. Later writers, less charitable, have called Williams the Bristol Sloth. Staunton, it should be said, was a man who found a reason for each of his defeats, and the reason was rarely Staunton. But the complaint touched a real nerve. There was, in eighteen fifty-one, no rule at all about how long a player might think. A game could run through a day and into the next. One could, in principle, decline to move forever.

The remedy took a decade to arrive, and it arrived in the form of sand. In the summer of eighteen sixty-one, at the London Chess Club, Adolf Anderssen played a match against the Austrian Ignatz Kolisch in which, for the first time, each player's thinking was timed by a sandglass: twenty-four moves in two hours, the glass of the player to move running while the other's lay on its side. The press thought it a great success. In eighteen eighty-three, at another tournament in London, the players sat for the first time before a mechanical clock — the tumbling clock, devised by Thomas Bright Wilson of Manchester: two clocks on a rocking frame, so that a player who had moved could tip the frame, stopping their own clock and starting the opponent's. Within a few years no serious competition was played without one. The chess clock is a small machine for answering a large question: how much thought does a move deserve?

The clock did not answer the question for the players. It only made them answer it for themselves. A player with two hours for twenty-four moves must decide, at every move, whether this position deserves five seconds or forty minutes — whether the reply is obvious, or whether the whole game turns on it. Every strong player develops a sense of that, and it is, I should say, one of the things that makes them strong. A weak player spends a quarter of an hour on a forced recapture and plays the critical move in the last minute before the flag falls.

The players have a word for the consequence, borrowed from German, Zeitnot, said tsite-note, which means time trouble: the state of a player who has spent too freely on the early moves and must now make a dozen in a couple of minutes, and who blunders, very often, not because the position is hard but because the clock has left no room to see that it is. Time trouble is not a failure of calculation. It is a failure of budgeting. And the chess engines, when they came, had to solve the same problem: every serious engine has a module devoted to nothing but managing its clock, and the better ones spend more of their time on a move when their opinion of the best move keeps changing as they search, and less when it settles quickly — a crude but serviceable measure of how hard the position really is. Hold on to that idea, of a player who decides how long to think by noticing how unsettled the question is. We shall meet it again at the end of the evening, in a more exalted setting.

I begin there, sir, because you will have met a descendant of that clock at your own desk. The large language models of the last two years think before they answer — they write out a chain of reasoning, sometimes a few sentences, sometimes many thousands of words, before they give you the reply — and the companies that make them now offer you a setting that governs how long. It goes by different names. OpenAI calls it reasoning effort, and now offers seven settings, from none and minimal through low, medium, and high, to extra high and, since this summer, max. Anthropic calls it effort too, with levels from low to max. Google calls it a thinking level. Open models are told in their instructions to reason at low, medium, or high. And in the tools you use every day, a single word typed into a prompt, or a setting in a menu, quietly decides whether the machine will think for a moment or for a quarter of an hour.

You asked how a model is trained to take that setting into account, per response, and you wondered, with characteristic modesty, whether there was enough in the subject to fill an hour and a half. If I may say so, sir, there is rather more than enough, though I grant that the subject is younger than most of the wine in a respectable cellar. The question opens on to three larger ones, each of which needs its own hour to do justice: why thinking out loud helps a language model at all; why the models, left to themselves, learned to think at such extraordinary length; and how one teaches restraint to a machine that has learned, by reinforcement, that more thought pays. The setting in the menu is where those three questions meet.

The title of the evening is borrowed. In May of eighteen eighty-two, the physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach gave an address to the Imperial Academy of Sciences in Vienna, which he called The Economical Nature of Physical Inquiry. His argument was that science is, at bottom, an economy of thought: that a mind of limited capacity, facing a world too rich to grasp in every particular, does well to spend its effort where it saves the most, and that a good law of nature is precisely such a saving — one rule in place of a thousand remembered cases. Mach was thinking of physicists, but he would have recognised the problem we meet tonight at once. Thinking is costly. The economy of thought is the art of spending it where it pays.

So the question that governs this evening is yours: when you set a model's effort to low, medium, or high, what has the model been taught, and how?

Here is the answer, in a paragraph, and the rest of the evening is its evidence.

Effort is not a knob on the machinery. There is no dial inside the model that runs it faster or slower. It is a request, written into the model's instructions, which the model was trained to honour — by reinforcement learning, on rewards that pay for being right and charge for length, the charge set by the effort that was requested. Ask for low effort and, in training, every token of thought was expensive; ask for high, and it was cheap. So one set of weights learns a whole family of behaviours, from terse to exhaustive, selected by a few words in its instructions. Around that trained behaviour, the systems that serve the models add blunter instruments — a hard cap on length, a forced stop, a router that sends easy questions to a cheaper model. And the frontier, this autumn, is the model that estimates for itself how hard the question is, and spends accordingly — as a good chess player does, and as a weak one does not.

The image to keep beside the true terms is the one I have just used: the price of a token. An effort setting is, in effect, an exchange rate between correctness and length, set by you, and learned by the model as a price.

The route, then, in six stages.

First, why a model thinks out loud at all, and why thinking longer helps — which turns out to be a fact about the architecture of the transformer, and a lesson from the games of poker and Go. Second, how the reasoning models were made, by reinforcement learning, and how their chains of thought grew longer and longer without anyone asking them to, until they were thinking at length about two plus three. Third, the blunt instruments: the ways of controlling a model's thinking without teaching it anything, by cutting it off, or by making it go on. Fourth, the heart of the matter: how a model is trained to honour a budget or an effort level, with the reward functions walked through aloud, numbers and all. Fifth, the commercial history: the hybrid models that can think or not think, the parameters the laboratories shipped, and what each choice tells us about how they were trained. And sixth, the frontier: models that decide for themselves how hard to think, the routers that decide for them, and why both err on the side of thinking more.

One caution, before we start, about the evidence. Some of what follows is published in detail: the open models and the academic papers describe their methods, often with their formulas. The largest commercial laboratories mostly do not say how they train their effort settings, and where that is so, I shall tell you what they have said, what the open evidence shows, and what may reasonably be inferred — and I shall say which is which.

An hour and a half or so, sir. We begin with a curious fact about how a transformer does its arithmetic.

Part One — Thinking Out Loud

Let us start with the machine itself, because the whole subject rests on one plain fact about it, and the fact is not widely appreciated even by people who use these models every day.

A transformer — the architecture inside every large language model you have used — produces its output one token at a time. For each token, the whole model runs once: the input passes up through a fixed stack of layers, a few dozen of them in a large model, each doing a fixed amount of arithmetic, and at the top a probability comes out for every possible next token. One is chosen. It is appended to the text. And the whole model runs again, from the bottom, to produce the next.

Notice what is fixed in that description. The number of layers is fixed. The arithmetic in each layer is fixed. So the amount of computation the model can spend on producing any single token is fixed, whatever the token. The model spends exactly as much effort on the word the as it does on the answer to a hard question in arithmetic. It cannot pause. It cannot, within the production of one token, think harder about a hard one.

There is only one way for a transformer to spend more computation on a problem before committing to an answer, and that is to produce more tokens first. Every token it writes becomes part of the input to everything that follows, so a model that writes out its intermediate steps is, quite literally, giving itself more passes through the machine before it must answer — and leaving itself notes, in the text, to read on each pass. The text is its working memory. For a transformer, writing is thinking, and thinking longer means writing more.

Hold on to that, sir, because it is the foundation of the whole evening. When we speak of a model's reasoning effort, we are speaking of how many tokens it writes before it answers. There is no other kind of effort available to it.

You can feel the force of this with a human comparison. Ask someone to multiply two four-digit numbers in their head and announce the answer at once, and they will very probably get it wrong. Give them a pencil and paper and they will very probably get it right. Nothing about their brain has changed. What has changed is that they may now write down the intermediate products and come back to them — that their thinking may now be spread over time, with a memory outside the head. A language model forbidden to write anything before its answer is the arithmetician without the pencil.

The same fact explains a small embarrassment you will remember from the summer of twenty twenty-four, when it was briefly the most famous question in artificial intelligence: how many times does the letter r occur in the word strawberry? Model after model said two. The reason is that a language model does not see letters; it sees tokens, chunks of a word, and the word arrives as two or three of them, with the letters folded away inside. Asked to answer at once, the model must guess from what it remembers about the word. Asked to spell the word out first, one letter to a line, and then count, it writes the letters into its own working memory, where it can see them, and it counts three. The arithmetic was never the difficulty. The difficulty was answering without writing anything down. It is a coincidence, but a pleasing one, that the codename widely reported for OpenAI's first reasoning project that summer was Strawberry.

The practitioners discovered this before the theorists explained it. In twenty twenty-one, a group at Google showed that a language model trained to write out the intermediate steps of a computation on a scratchpad, as they called it, before giving the result, could do arithmetic and execute programs far better than one trained to give the result directly. Early in twenty twenty-two, Jason Wei and colleagues, also at Google, showed that you did not even need to train the model: if you simply included, in the prompt, a few examples of questions answered with a worked chain of reasoning, the model would imitate the format, reason step by step before answering, and get many more answers right. They called it chain-of-thought prompting, and the gains on arithmetic word problems, for the largest model they tried, were enormous. And a few months later, a team from Tokyo and Google found that you did not even need the examples. Simply appending the words let's think step by step to a question raised one model's accuracy on a standard set of arithmetic problems from about eighteen percent to nearly seventy-nine. One sentence, and the model went from failing to passing, because the sentence made it write before it answered.

The theorists followed. Several groups have since proved, in various precise senses, what the practitioners found: that a transformer allowed to write out intermediate steps can solve classes of problem that a transformer of the same size, forced to answer at once, provably cannot. The chain of thought is not decoration. It is extra computation, and for some problems it is the only route to the answer.

So the first half of the subject is established: thinking out loud helps, because it is the only way a transformer can think longer. The second half is the question of how much longer — and the most useful lesson about that came not from language at all, but from games.

You will remember AlphaGo, which beat Lee Sedol at Go in twenty sixteen. What is less often remembered is that AlphaGo had two parts. One was a neural network that, looking at a position, could suggest good moves — a trained intuition. The other was a search: before each move, the program would explore thousands of possible continuations, guided by the network, and choose the move whose consequences looked best. The network alone played well. The network with search played superhumanly. A great deal of AlphaGo's strength came not from what it had learned in training but from the computation it spent at the board, deciding each move. Its successor, AlphaGo Zero, makes the point in numbers. By the figures usually cited from the paper of twenty seventeen, its network on its own, simply playing the move it liked best, was rated at about three thousand and fifty-five on the Elo scale — a very strong player. The same network, given time to search before each move, was rated at about five thousand one hundred and eighty-five. On a scale of that kind, a gap of two thousand points means that the version which searched would beat the version which did not in essentially every game they ever played. Every one of those points was bought with thinking at the board, not with training.

The researcher who has made this point most forcefully is Noam Brown, who built the poker programs that first beat top professionals at no-limit Texas hold'em — Libratus, in twenty seventeen, and Pluribus, two years later — and who later joined OpenAI and worked on its first reasoning models. In a talk in twenty twenty-four, Brown described what the poker work had shown. Letting the program think for just twenty seconds during a hand, Brown said, gave the same boost in performance as scaling up the model a hundred thousand times and training it a hundred thousand times longer. The field, in Brown's view, had spent years scaling the training and neglecting the thinking.

The trade's term for that thinking is test-time compute — sometimes inference-time compute: the computation spent on a problem at the moment of answering it, as distinct from the computation spent training the model beforehand. And in August of twenty twenty-four, a paper by Charlie Snell and colleagues at Berkeley and Google DeepMind asked the question properly for language models: if you have a fixed budget of computation to spend at test time, how should you spend it? Their answer had two parts, both important to us.

The first was that the best way to spend it depends on how hard the question is. On easy questions, it paid to let the model revise its own answer a few times; on hard ones, it paid to search more broadly, trying many different approaches. A strategy that adapted to the difficulty of each question beat the best fixed strategy by a wide margin, using about a quarter of the computation. That is the principle of the chess clock: different positions deserve different amounts of thought, and the player who allocates accordingly does better than the one who spends the same on every move.

The second was that, on the problems where a small model already had some chance of success, spending more at test time could beat a model fourteen times larger that answered directly. Thought, in the right place, could substitute for size.

Before we go on, one distinction will save confusion later, because test-time compute can be spent in two quite different ways. It can be spent sequentially: one line of thought, made longer — the model thinking for ten thousand tokens instead of one thousand before it answers. Or it can be spent in parallel: many independent attempts at the same question, followed by a vote or a judge. The parallel kind is the older trick. In twenty twenty-two, Xuezhi Wang and colleagues at Google showed that if you sample many chains of reasoning for the same question and take the answer that most of them reach — they called it self-consistency — you get more right than any single chain does. The two kinds can be combined: Anthropic reported in twenty twenty-five that one of its models, given the equivalent of two hundred and fifty-six independent attempts and a learned scoring model to choose among them, reached about eighty-five percent on a notoriously hard set of graduate-level science questions. But the two kinds have very different costs. Parallel attempts multiply the bill but not the waiting, since they can run side by side; a longer chain multiplies the waiting as well. The effort settings this evening is about govern, almost entirely, the sequential kind — how long the one line of thought runs. The parallel kind tends to appear in products as a separate, premium mode, with a name like pro or deep think, priced accordingly.

In September of twenty twenty-four, a month after Snell's paper, OpenAI announced o-one — written as a small letter o and the numeral one — the first widely used model trained to do its thinking before it answered. Its announcement carried two charts that have since been reproduced in a thousand slide decks. Both showed accuracy on a hard mathematics competition rising steadily as computation increased. In the first, the computation was spent in training, on reinforcement learning. In the second, it was spent at test time, in thinking. The accompanying sentence has become one of the founding texts of the subject: the performance of o-one, the company wrote, consistently improves with more reinforcement learning, which it called train-time compute, and with more time spent thinking, which it called test-time compute. And in both charts, the horizontal axis was logarithmic. Seven months later, introducing o-three, the company reported that it had pushed a further order of magnitude in both kinds of computation and was still seeing clear gains: the more the models were allowed to think, the better they did.

That detail is the economics of the whole subject, so let me put numbers through it. A logarithmic axis means that each doubling of the thinking buys roughly the same improvement. Suppose that, on some task, a model thinking for a thousand tokens gets sixty questions in a hundred right, and that each doubling adds five more. Then two thousand tokens gets you sixty-five; four thousand, seventy; eight thousand, seventy-five; sixteen thousand, eighty. Each step costs twice as much as the last and buys the same five points. The first five points cost a thousand tokens. The fourth five cost eight thousand. If you wanted twenty points more than that — four more doublings — you would be thinking for a quarter of a million tokens per question, and paying for every one of them, in money and in waiting.

And remember that the price is paid twice. It is paid in money, because thinking tokens are billed as output, usually at the highest rate a laboratory charges. And it is paid in time, because a transformer writes one token after another and cannot write the tenth before the ninth. At a hundred tokens a second — a brisk rate for a large model — a thousand tokens of thought is ten seconds of waiting. Sixteen thousand is two minutes and forty seconds. A quarter of a million is the better part of three quarters of an hour, during which your user, if you have one, has gone to make tea.

I have invented those particular numbers, but the shape is real, and it is the shape the laboratories have published again and again: more thought keeps helping, and helps less and less per token. The returns are logarithmic. Which means that the right amount of thought for a question is not as much as possible. It is however much a further point of accuracy is worth, to you, on that question — and that is exactly what a dial is for.

So there is the first part of the story. A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token, so it can think longer only by writing more; writing out its reasoning lets it solve problems it otherwise cannot; and the benefit of more thinking, as the games taught and the language models confirmed, is real but logarithmic, each doubling buying about as much as the last, at twice the price. The setting in your menu exists because the right amount of thinking is not a constant of nature. It is a decision about price.

Part Two — Reinforcement and the Lengthening

Chain-of-thought prompting showed that a model could be coaxed into reasoning. The reasoning models of the last two years are something else: models trained, at great expense, to reason before they answer, as a matter of habit, and to reason well. How they were trained explains why they think at such length — and why, left to themselves, they did not know when to stop.

The method is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. The name is long, and every word of it is doing work, so let us take it in pieces.

Reinforcement learning is learning from consequences rather than from examples. In the supervised training you know well, the model is shown the right answer and nudged toward producing it. In reinforcement learning, nobody shows the model the right answer. It tries something, the result is scored, and it is nudged toward whatever it did that scored well and away from whatever scored badly. It is the difference between being taught to cook by following a recipe and being taught by being told, after each meal, whether it was any good.

Verifiable rewards means that the score is computed by a program, not by a person and not by another model: for a mathematics problem, a checker that compares the model's final answer with the known answer; for a programming problem, a set of tests that the model's code must pass. The appeal is enormous. A program can score millions of attempts without tiring, it cannot be flattered, and it does not care how the model arrived at the answer — only whether the answer is right.

And that last fact is the key to everything that follows. The reward looks only at the final answer. It says nothing whatever about the thinking. The model is free to write as much or as little as it likes before the answer, in whatever manner it likes, and the only thing that matters is whether the answer at the end is correct. So whatever kind of thinking makes correct answers more likely is the kind the training will reinforce.

The clearest public account of what happens next is the paper DeepSeek published in January of twenty twenty-five, describing its R-one models, and in particular an experimental model it called R-one-Zero. R-one-Zero was trained by reinforcement learning applied directly to a pretrained base model, with no examples of reasoning at all to start from. The model was given a simple template — think first, inside a pair of tags marking the thinking, then give the answer inside another pair — and two kinds of reward: one for a correct answer, and one for using the format. That was all. No one told it how to think, or for how long.

The algorithm DeepSeek used is worth a moment, because it is now the workhorse of the field and we shall need its arithmetic later. It is called GRPO, for group relative policy optimisation. For each problem, the model is asked to produce not one answer but a group of them — say sixteen attempts at the same question. Each attempt is scored. Then each attempt's score is compared with the average of its group: attempts that did better than their siblings are reinforced, in proportion to how much better, and attempts that did worse are discouraged, in proportion to how much worse. The model is not asked to be good in some absolute sense. It is asked, each time, to be better than its own average on this very question. It is a little like marking each pupil's essay by comparison with the rest of the class on the same title — which, as any schoolmaster knows, is both fairer and more motivating than an absolute standard, and has some curious side effects, of which more shortly.

Let me put a small group through it, because the arithmetic matters later. Suppose the model makes four attempts at a problem, and two are right and two are wrong. Scoring one for right and nothing for wrong, the group's average is one half. The two right answers stand above the average, and are reinforced; the two wrong ones stand below it, and are discouraged; and, since the scores are also scaled by how much they vary within the group, the two right answers get an equal push up and the two wrong ones an equal push down. Now suppose instead that all four attempts are right. The average is one, every attempt stands exactly at the average, and nothing is reinforced at all. The same if all four are wrong. A problem that the model always solves, or never solves, teaches it nothing under this scheme. Only the problems it sometimes solves — the ones at the edge of its competence — move it, which is why the trainers take great pains over which problems to set, keeping the model always at the edge, like a good tutor.

Now, the remarkable thing. As the training went on, the average length of R-one-Zero's responses grew — steadily, almost in a straight line, from about five hundred tokens at the start to about ten thousand after some eight thousand steps. Nobody rewarded length. The reward said nothing about length. But longer thinking produced more correct answers, and so the behaviours that make thinking longer — checking a result, trying a second method, going back to find an error, considering a case that had been overlooked — were reinforced, because they paid. The model, in the paper's words, naturally learned to solve reasoning tasks with more thinking time.

And with the length came behaviours that nobody had taught. The paper describes the model learning to reflect on its own steps and to explore alternative approaches. It reproduces a passage from an intermediate version of the model, working on a mathematics problem, in which it suddenly writes: Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here — and then goes back to reconsider its earlier work, step by step. The authors remarked that this was an aha moment not only for the model but for the researchers watching it. It was a striking thing to see: the habit of second thoughts, arising unbidden from nothing but a reward for being right. I should add, in fairness, that not everyone accepts the moment as a birth. A group that later re-examined this kind of training found the same self-correcting phrases in the base model before any reinforcement learning had been done — the habit, on their reading, was latent in the pretrained model, and reinforcement amplified it rather than created it. Either way, the training made it habitual, because it paid.

So there is the first and most important fact about why reasoning models think at length. They learned it because it paid. A transformer can think longer only by writing more; reinforcement learning on correct answers discovered that writing more produced more correct answers; and so the models learned to write more, and more, and more. The lengthening was not designed. It was grown.

But not all of it was earned. In the spring of twenty twenty-five, two groups of researchers looked more closely at the arithmetic of the training itself, and found that some of the lengthening came from a quirk in the objective rather than from any benefit to the answers.

Here is the quirk. In the form of GRPO that most people used, the training signal for each attempt was averaged over the attempt's tokens: an attempt's reward or penalty was, in effect, spread across its length, so that each token bore a share. For a correct attempt, that is harmless enough. But consider an incorrect attempt, which is to be discouraged. If it is short, its penalty is shared among few tokens, and each is discouraged strongly. If it is long, the same penalty is shared among many tokens, and each is discouraged only a little. So, of two wrong answers, the long one is punished more gently, token by token, than the short one. The training was, without anybody intending it, kinder to long failures than to short ones — and a model trained that way drifts toward length even where length buys nothing. Zichen Liu and colleagues, who named their corrected version, with a scholar's sense of humour, Doctor GRPO — GRPO done right — showed that removing this bias kept the accuracy and cut the length; Mehdi Fatemi and colleagues made a related argument, that a good part of the length growth in such training was an artifact of how failures were penalised, and that a short second round of training could remove much of it without loss.

So the long thoughts of the reasoning models are partly a genuine discovery — that thinking more gets more right — and partly an accident of bookkeeping. Both pushed the same way.

And the result, by the end of twenty twenty-four, was a recognisable problem with a name. The trade calls it overthinking.

The paper that named it best had a title that is itself a small joke: Do NOT Think That Much for Two Plus Three. Its authors, from Tencent and Shanghai Jiao Tong University, gave the early reasoning models of the day the simplest questions imaginable, and watched them labour. Asked what two plus three is, one prominent open reasoning model of the day produced, in a single response, thirteen separate solutions — arriving at five, checking it, arriving at five again by another route, and again, and again. Averaged across the reasoning models they tested, the authors reported, the question cost nearly twenty times as many tokens as it did when put to ordinary models, which simply said five. The authors counted the rounds of solution in many such responses, and gave the trade two useful measures: how much of a response was needed to reach the first correct answer, and how much of the rest was genuinely new reasoning rather than repetition. Most of them, on easy problems, added nothing at all: the first solution was already right, and everything after it was the model reassuring itself, at your expense.

Overthinking has three costs, and they are worth separating. It costs money, because thinking tokens are billed as output tokens, usually the most expensive kind. It costs time, because tokens are produced one at a time, so a model that thinks for ten thousand tokens before answering makes you wait for every one of them. And, sometimes, it costs accuracy — which is the surprising one.

In July of twenty twenty-five, researchers working with Anthropic published a paper called Inverse Scaling in Test-Time Compute, which found families of tasks on which giving a model more room to think made its answers worse. On simple counting questions surrounded by irrelevant detail, longer reasoning made models more likely to be distracted by the detail. On problems with plausible but spurious patterns, longer reasoning gave the model more opportunity to talk itself into the spurious pattern. A month earlier, researchers at Apple, in a paper called The Illusion of Thinking, had reported something stranger still: on puzzles of steadily increasing difficulty, the models' thinking grew longer as the puzzles got harder — up to a point — and then, on the hardest puzzles, grew shorter, as if the models were giving up, although they had plenty of room left to think. That paper drew a sharp reply, pointing out that some of its hardest puzzles exceeded the models' output limits and some had no solution at all, and the argument about how to read it has not been settled. But the broader point stands, and both sides would accept it: more thought is not always better thought, and a model's sense of how much to think is not to be trusted blindly.

There is a deeper reason why reinforcement learning of this kind finds it hard to learn when to stop, and it has a name in the trade: credit assignment. The reward arrives once, at the very end, as a single number — right or wrong. The response that earned it may be ten thousand tokens long. Which of those tokens did the work? Perhaps the answer was found in the first five hundred and the rest was reassurance; perhaps it was found only in the last thousand, after a long false start. The reward cannot say. So the training reinforces the whole performance, padding and all, and the model has no way of learning, from the reward alone, that most of the performance was unnecessary. A great deal of the work we shall hear about later in the evening is, at bottom, an attempt to give the model the information that a single number at the end withholds.

So, by the beginning of twenty twenty-five, the position was this. Reinforcement learning had produced models that reasoned remarkably well, because it rewarded correct answers and longer thinking got more of them; the arithmetic of the training had pushed the length further still; and nothing in the training had ever taught the models when to stop. They had learned that more thought pays. They had not learned that thought costs. That is the gap the effort setting exists to fill — and the rest of the evening is the story of how it was filled: first with blunt instruments, then with training.

Part Three — The Blunt Instruments

Before anyone trained a model to manage its own thinking, there were ways to manage it from outside — by interfering with the model as it ran, without teaching it anything. They are still in use, some of them inside the very products whose effort settings you adjust, and they are worth understanding for two reasons: because they show what the problem looks like when you do not solve it properly, and because, as we shall see, the proper solution was in part learned from them.

The bluntest instrument of all is the one every programmer reaches for first: a limit on the number of tokens the model may produce. Every interface to a language model has one. Set it to two thousand, and after two thousand tokens the model is stopped, wherever it happens to be.

For an ordinary model, writing an ordinary answer, that is crude but tolerable: you lose the end of the answer. For a reasoning model, it can be a disaster, because the answer comes after the thinking. If the limit falls in the middle of the thinking, the model is stopped mid-thought, and there is no answer at all — only the first two thousand tokens of somebody else's working, which you have paid for and cannot use. OpenAI's documentation for its reasoning models warns of exactly this: that a response can end incomplete, with the budget consumed in reasoning and nothing visible to show for it, and it advises leaving generous room. A hard cap on a reasoning model is like taking a student's paper away when the time is up, regardless of whether they have reached the part where they write down the answer.

The second instrument is more interesting, and it was made famous by a paper of January twenty twenty-five from Stanford and the University of Washington, called, with commendable plainness, s-one: Simple test-time scaling. The authors, led by Niklas Muennighoff, wanted to know how little it takes to make a reasoning model, and how simply its thinking could be controlled. They assembled a thousand carefully chosen hard questions, sifted from a pool of nearly sixty thousand for difficulty, variety, and quality, each with a reasoning trace and an answer produced by one of Google's reasoning models, and fine-tuned an existing open model — one of Alibaba's, with thirty-two billion parameters — on them. The training took twenty-six minutes, on sixteen of the large accelerators the industry uses. The resulting model, they reported, beat OpenAI's first preview of o-one on competition mathematics by a comfortable margin. Then they controlled the length of its thinking at test time by a method they called budget forcing, which has two halves.

To make the model think less, when the thinking reaches the budget, you force it to stop: you write, in the model's place, the marker that ends its thinking, followed by the words Final Answer, and let it continue from there. The model, finding that it has apparently finished thinking and begun answering, answers.

To make the model think more, you do the opposite. When the model tries to end its thinking — when it is about to produce the closing marker — you suppress the marker, and in its place you write a single word: Wait. The model, finding that it has apparently just said wait, does what a person does after saying wait: it reconsiders. It goes back over its working, often checks its answer, and not infrequently finds a mistake and corrects it. Do this once or twice and the model thinks longer and, on hard problems, gets more right: on one competition in mathematics, forcing it to go on raised its score from fifty percent to fifty-seven. But not indefinitely. After half a dozen forced waits, the gains flattened out, and a model prodded too often begins to go round in circles, reconsidering what it has already reconsidered.

There is something almost comic about this. The most advanced machine of its kind, made to think harder by having the word wait put into its mouth, like a nervous witness prompted by counsel. But it works, and the reason it works is the one we met in the last Part: the reinforcement learning that made these models had rewarded exactly the behaviours — checking, reconsidering, second thoughts — that the word wait introduces. The word does not add intelligence. It triggers a habit the model already has.

NVIDIA's engineers, building a small open reasoning model in the summer of twenty twenty-five, published a revealing account of what happens when a model is stopped this way without being prepared for it. Two things went wrong. The model, having had its thinking cut short, used more tokens in its final answer, to compensate — doing in the answer the thinking it had been denied. And sometimes it carried on thinking even after the closing marker had been inserted, as though it did not register the artificial closure at all. Their cure was to train on examples prepared for the purpose: some prompts paired with answers stripped of their reasoning altogether, so that the model learned to answer from an empty thought, and a few percent of examples whose reasoning had been deliberately cut off after a thousand or two thousand tokens, with the final answer kept, so that the model learned to answer from an interrupted one. Both failures, they reported, largely disappeared.

Budget forcing has a clear limitation, and it is the important thing about it. The model does not know the budget. It plans its thinking as if it had all the time in the world, and is then interrupted, or prolonged, from outside. A thought cut off is not the same as a short thought; it is a long thought, interrupted. A model that had known from the start that it had only a thousand tokens would have thought differently — gone straight for the most promising approach, skipped the second check on the easy part, saved its doubts for the hard part. The chess player who knows there are five minutes on the clock plays a different game from the one whose board is snatched away after five minutes.

The third instrument shows how far one can get by making the model's own habits cooperate with the interruption. When Alibaba's Qwen team — the name is usually said kwen — released its Qwen three family in April of twenty twenty-five, it offered developers a thinking budget, and its technical report describes how the budget is enforced. When the model's thinking reaches the budget, the system stops it and inserts a sentence, in the model's own voice, before closing the thinking: Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now. Then the thinking is closed, and the model answers from whatever it has worked out so far.

I find that sentence rather touching. It is the machine equivalent of the student who, seeing the invigilator approach, writes in the interests of time at the bottom of the page and commits to an answer. And the Qwen team made an observation about it that matters a great deal for what follows. They had not trained the model specially to answer from incomplete thoughts. They found that the ability emerged from the way they had trained it to switch between thinking and not thinking — of which more in the fifth Part — so that a model interrupted mid-thought could still produce a sensible answer from a partial line of reasoning. And they reported that, as the budget grew, the model's scores rose smoothly. A blunt instrument, made less blunt by training the model to take the blow gracefully.

The fourth instrument is the extreme case of the third: give the model no budget at all. In April of twenty twenty-five, Wenjie Ma and colleagues at Berkeley tried simply filling in the thinking section for the model with a short dummy statement — in effect, I have finished thinking — and closing it, so that the model went straight to its answer. They called this NoThinking. On many problems it did far better than one might fear. At tight budgets it did better than thinking: on one mathematics competition, held to seven hundred tokens, the model that skipped its thinking scored about fifty-one where the model that thought scored about twenty-nine, because the thinking model spent its seven hundred tokens on preliminaries and never reached the answer. And combined with the old trick of asking several times and taking the best answer, the quick mode matched the thinking mode at a fraction of the waiting. And it is, as we shall see, exactly what the so-called non-thinking mode of the hybrid models does: the thinking section is there, by format, but empty.

The fifth instrument is the gentlest: simply ask. Tell the model, in its instructions, to be brief, or to use no more than so many tokens. Researchers tried this early. A group from Nanjing and elsewhere, in a paper called Token-Budget-Aware LLM Reasoning, found that models told to keep within a budget of tokens did shorten their reasoning — but treated the budget loosely, and showed a perversity the authors called token elasticity. Told to answer a question in fifty tokens, one model used eighty-six. Told to answer the same question in ten, it used a hundred and fifty-seven. The tighter the budget, the further the model overshot it, as though protesting. A team at Zoom, in February of twenty twenty-five, had more success with a different request, which they called Chain of Draft: think step by step, but keep only a minimum draft for each thinking step, with five words at most — the jottings of an expert rather than the full sentences of a student. On several reasoning tasks, they reported accuracy close to the full chain of thought at a small fraction of the tokens — in one case, about seven and a half percent.

And the sixth instrument tries to stop at the right moment rather than at a fixed one. Several groups proposed early exit: at natural pauses in the thinking — the points where the model writes wait, or begins a new approach — have it attempt an answer, measure how confident it is in that answer, and, if it is confident enough, stop thinking and give it. On easy questions, where the first answer is right and everything after it is the model reassuring itself, this saves a great deal; on hard ones, where confidence stays low, the thinking continues. Notice the neat inversion. Budget forcing treats the word wait as the place to make the model think more; early exit treats the very same word as the place to ask whether it need think at all.

Let me draw these together, because they share a character. Every one of these instruments acts on the model from outside, at the moment of use: cut it off; force it to stop with a polite sentence; force it to go on with the word wait; give it an empty thought; ask it nicely to be brief; or stop it when it seems sure. Some of them work remarkably well. But in all of them, the model is not managing its own thinking. It is being managed. It does not know, as it begins, how much it will be allowed, and so it cannot plan; and when you ask it, in words, to be brief, it was never trained to take the request seriously, and so it treats it as a suggestion.

That diagnosis — the model is managed, not managing; interrupted, not planning; asked, but never taught — is the reason the laboratories went further. If you want a model to think briefly when told to, and at length when told to, and to plan its thinking accordingly from the first token, you must teach it. And the only teacher the reasoning models have ever really listened to is the one that made them: reinforcement learning, and the reward.

Part Four — Paying for Brevity

We come now to the heart of the matter, and I shall take it slowly, because it is the part of the evening that answers your question most directly, and because it is best understood with numbers in one's ears.

Recall where we stand. Reinforcement learning made the reasoning models by rewarding correct answers, and nothing else; longer thinking got more answers right, and a quirk of the bookkeeping pushed it further; so the models learned that more thought pays. They never learned that thought costs, because nothing in their training charged for it. The blunt instruments of the last Part impose a cost from outside, by interruption. The obvious next step is to put the cost inside the training — to make the reward itself charge for length — so that the model learns the cost the way it learned everything else, as a consequence of its own behaviour.

The general name for this is a length penalty: a term in the reward that subtracts something for every token the model writes, or for every token beyond some target. It sounds trivial. It is not, and the details of how it is done decide whether you get a model that thinks efficiently or a model that has simply stopped thinking.

Let me give you the first published version that worked at scale, because its arithmetic is easy to carry in the head. It comes from Moonshot AI, the Chinese laboratory behind the Kimi models, in the report on its model k-one-point-five, published in January of twenty twenty-five, the same week as DeepSeek's. The Kimi team had watched the problem appear in their own training — the report speaks of an overthinking phenomenon, the response length growing sharply as reinforcement went on — and they added, to the reward for correctness, a reward for length that is computed relative to the other attempts at the same problem, in the spirit of the group comparison we met with GRPO.

Here is how it works. For a given problem, the model makes several attempts, of various lengths. Find the shortest and the longest. Then each attempt gets a length score, which runs from plus one half for the shortest to minus one half for the longest, in a straight line between them. An attempt exactly halfway between the shortest and the longest gets zero.

Now the important refinement. If the attempt was correct, it receives its length score, whatever it is: a bonus if it was short, a penalty if it was long. If the attempt was wrong, it can receive the penalty but never the bonus. A short wrong answer gets nothing for being short.

Let me put three attempts through it. Suppose that on some problem the model writes three answers, of one thousand, two thousand, and three thousand tokens, and all three are right. The thousand-token answer is the shortest, and gets a bonus of one half. The three-thousand-token answer is the longest, and gets a penalty of one half. The two-thousand-token answer, in the middle, gets nothing either way. All three are rewarded for being right, but the training now prefers, among right answers, the shorter ones. How strongly it prefers them is a matter of one more number, a weight chosen by the trainers, with which the length score is added to the reward for correctness.

Now suppose instead that the short answer was wrong. It gets no bonus, because wrong answers never do; it gets the ordinary nothing for being wrong. The model learns that brevity is only a virtue in company with correctness — which is exactly the right lesson. A training scheme that rewarded short answers whether or not they were right would teach the model to guess, quickly and confidently, which is the worst of all worlds.

Not every laboratory drew the line for wrong answers in the same place, and the difference is instructive. A group at Carnegie Mellon, in a paper of February of twenty twenty-five, shaped the reward along a gentle curve and made the opposite choice for failures: among wrong answers, the short ones were punished hardest of all — a short wrong answer scored worse than anything else the model could produce — so that a model uncertain of its answer was pushed to keep thinking rather than to commit. Kimi's rule says: if you are going to be wrong, at least be brief. The other says: if you are going to be wrong, at least have tried. Both are defensible. The first saves tokens; the second saves face, and sometimes the answer. It is exactly the choice a schoolmaster faces between the pupil who gives up quickly and the one who fills three pages with a wrong method, and schoolmasters, too, have never agreed.

To return to the Kimi report, two other details of it are instructive. First, the team found that applying the length penalty from the start of training slowed the learning, so they warmed it up: trained first for correctness alone, and only then added the penalty, at a steady weight, for the rest of the training. A model must learn to think well before it learns to think briefly; a pupil drilled in concision before having anything to say produces only very short nonsense. Second, they reported a whole family of what they called long-to-short methods, for transferring the competence of a model that thinks at length into one that thinks briefly: merging the weights of a long-thinking and a short-thinking model; sampling many answers and training on the shortest correct one; and preference training, in which the short correct answer is preferred to the long one. The whole field of efficient reasoning was beginning to take shape in that report.

But notice what the Kimi scheme does not do. It makes the model shorter. It does not make the model controllable. There is one model, with one notion of how long is too long, set by the training. If you want a model that thinks briefly when you say so and at length when you say so, you need to put your wishes into the model's input, and make the reward depend on them.

That is the step taken in a paper of March of twenty twenty-five from Carnegie Mellon, by Pranjal Aggarwal and Sean Welleck, whose title states the aim exactly: L-one: Controlling How Long a Reasoning Model Thinks With Reinforcement Learning. The method has a name, length-controlled policy optimisation, and it is the cleanest public demonstration of how effort control is trained, so it deserves its own beats.

First, the instruction. Each training problem is given to the model with a sentence appended to it: think for so many tokens — with the number chosen at random, problem by problem, anywhere from a hundred to four thousand. The model sees, in its input, a different budget every time.

Second, the reward. The model is rewarded, as before, for getting the answer right — one point for correct, nothing for wrong. And from that it loses a small amount for every token by which its thinking misses the requested length, too long or too short. The amount is small: in the paper, three ten-thousandths of a point per token. Put numbers through it. Ask for a thousand tokens. A correct answer in exactly a thousand tokens scores one. A correct answer in two thousand tokens — a thousand over — loses three-tenths of a point, and scores seven-tenths. A correct answer in five hundred — five hundred under — loses fifteen hundredths, and scores eighty-five hundredths. A wrong answer scores nothing, or less. Being right still matters most. But among right answers, the one that honours the request scores best, and the model is trained, over many thousands of problems, each with a different requested length, to honour it.

The authors also trained a second variant, which treats the number as a maximum rather than a target — think for at most so many tokens — with a reward that pays for correctness only while the answer stays within the budget and fades as it runs over. That is the variant closest to what a product means by a budget.

And third, the result. The model learned to hit the requested length closely, across the whole range — and, more importantly, to use the length well, planning its thinking to fit. At every budget, it got more answers right than the same model interrupted by budget forcing, often by a wide margin. The difference is the difference between a thought cut short and a short thought. A model that knows from the first token that it has five hundred tokens goes straight for the main line; a model told after five hundred tokens that it must stop has spent them on the preliminaries. The authors reported that their method beat budget forcing by as much as twenty points of accuracy at the same budget, and hit the requested length to within a few percent. And they found a pleasant side effect: their small model, of only one and a half billion parameters, held to short budgets, matched and by one measure beat GPT-four-o at the same length of reasoning — as though learning to think briefly had taught it to think more efficiently in general.

There is a term from economics that describes what such a model has learned, and since the trade uses it constantly in this connection, it is worth having. Plot, for any way of running a model, its accuracy against its cost. Some ways of running it are simply worse than others — less accurate and more expensive — and nobody should choose them. The rest form a curve along the top and to the left of the plot, on which you cannot buy more accuracy without paying more cost, and cannot save cost without giving up accuracy. That curve is called the Pareto frontier, after the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto, and every effort setting worth offering is a point on it. A model trained across many requested lengths, as L-one was, has learned not one point but the whole frontier, and the instruction in its input chooses where on the frontier to stand. Training a separate model for each point would cost a fortune and require you to switch models every time your needs changed. One model that has learned the frontier lets you move along it with a sentence.

So there is the mechanism in its purest form, and I want to say it plainly, because it is the centre of the evening. The control signal is written into the model's input. The reward pays for being right and charges for departing from what the input asked. Trained across many problems and many requests, one set of weights learns a whole family of behaviours, and the words in the input select among them.

Now let us see the same idea in a product.

In August of twenty twenty-five, OpenAI released two open-weight reasoning models, gpt-oss, with a model card that says more about effort than any of the company's statements about its closed models. The models support three reasoning levels, low, medium, and high, and the level is set not by a parameter in the machinery but by a line in the system prompt — the words Reasoning: low, or medium, or high. The card discusses them under a heading that reads, simply, variable effort reasoning training; it says that the models were trained to support these levels, and that raising the level raises the average length of the chain of thought; and it shows, for each level, a cloud of results whose accuracy climbs, in the familiar logarithmic way, as the thinking lengthens from one level to the next. The differences are large. On a recent mathematics competition, answered without tools, the larger of the two models solved about half the problems at low effort, four in five at medium, and more than nine in ten at high.

The card does not give the recipe. But notice the shape of what it does say. The effort level is text, in the input. The model was trained to respond to that text by thinking more or less. The behaviour at each level is a matter of average length, not a hard limit. Everything we have heard so far points to the simplest recipe that fits those facts: reinforcement learning in which the effort keyword appears in the prompt, and the charge for length depends on it — heavy at low effort, light at high. I must say clearly that this is inference; OpenAI has not published its method. But it is the recipe that the open literature has shown to work, and it is the one the evidence fits.

And the open literature has since done it with levels as well as numbers. In August of twenty twenty-five, a group led by ByteDance's Seed team published what they called an open recipe for controlling reasoning effort, under the name ThinkDial, with three modes, high, medium, and low. The recipe has two stages. First, supervised training on examples of each mode, each marked with its mode, so that the model learns what low and high look like. Then reinforcement learning with a separate length reward for each mode — a different price per level, in the language I shall come to in a moment. Their medium mode cut the thinking by about half for a loss of less than ten percent of the performance, and their low mode by about three quarters for a loss of less than fifteen percent. And in January of twenty twenty-six, a group from Peking University and Tencent described the most complete open version yet: train the best behaviour at each effort level separately, by reinforcement learning, and then distil all the levels into a single student model, which learns to reproduce whichever teacher its instructions name. One set of weights; several levels; a few words to choose among them.

And this spring one of the large laboratories said, in so many words, what the rest had left us to infer. In April of twenty twenty-six, introducing a reasoning model it called Muse Spark, Meta wrote that its reinforcement learning maximizes correctness subject to a penalty on thinking time — the price, stated plainly by the people who set it. And it reported something I find fascinating about what such a price does over the course of training. On some mathematics benchmarks, it said, the training passed through what it called a phase transition. At first the model improved by thinking longer, exactly as R-one-Zero had. Then the penalty began to bite, and the model's reasoning underwent what Meta called thought compression: it learned to solve the same problems using significantly fewer tokens. And then, having compressed, it began to lengthen its solutions again, and grew stronger still. Longer, then tighter, then longer and better: very much the progress of a student who first learns to be thorough, then learns to be concise, and only then learns where thoroughness is worth its cost.

DeepSeek's report on its model V-three-point-two, in December of twenty twenty-five, shows the price at work in another way: two models from one recipe, differing only in what thought cost them. The official model, the report says, was trained with a length penalty in its reinforcement learning, and the company imposed stricter token constraints on it to keep deployment costs and latency down; a second model, called Speciale, was trained with a reduced length penalty, and thought for longer, and scored higher. The report adds, with some candour, that the official model's performance was constrained by its length penalty, and improved further when the restriction was removed. Two prices; two operating points on the frontier; two models. An effort level is the same thing inside one model.

All of this suggests the image I offered you at the start, which I think is the right way to understand what an effort level is. Let me put numbers through it, and you will see why.

Imagine a question on which, from long experience, the model would get it right half the time if it thought for a thousand tokens, seven times in ten if it thought for four thousand, and eight times in ten if it thought for sixteen thousand. Those are the familiar logarithmic returns: each quadrupling buys less than the last.

Now imagine that, in training, each effort level carried a price for thought, charged against the reward, in points out of a hundred. At low effort, every thousand tokens of thinking cost ten points. At medium effort, two points. At high effort, half a point.

Work out what the model should do at each level. At low effort: thinking for a thousand tokens earns fifty points for the chance of being right and costs ten, for forty in all; four thousand earns seventy and costs forty, for thirty; sixteen thousand earns eighty and costs a hundred and sixty, a catastrophe. So, at low effort, the model should think for a thousand tokens. At medium effort, the same three choices come to forty-eight, sixty-two, and forty-eight: the model should think for four thousand. At high effort, they come to forty-nine and a half, sixty-eight, and seventy-two: the model should think for sixteen thousand.

One question; one model; three different amounts of thought, each the right one at its price. And here is the beautiful part, which is the reason the price is a better image than the budget. Change the question, and the right amounts change with it. On an easy question, which the model gets right ninety-five times in a hundred after a thousand tokens, even high effort buys almost nothing by thinking longer, and so even at high effort the model should stop early. On a very hard question, where thought pays handsomely, even low effort might justify thinking a little longer than usual. A price does not tell the model how long to think. It tells the model what thought is worth, and leaves it to judge, question by question, how much thought this question deserves. A budget, by contrast, says a thousand tokens, whether the question is two plus three or the proof of a theorem.

That, sir, is the economy of thought, in Mach's sense, made literal. An effort setting is an exchange rate between correctness and length. You set it. The model, trained across many prices and many questions, learns to spend at that rate — and, if it has been trained well, to spend more where the question repays it and less where it does not.

I should be honest that the laboratories do not describe their methods in these terms, and that real training mixes several of the schemes we have heard — targets, maximums, relative lengths, prices. But the image is faithful to what they all share: the setting is in the input, the cost is in the reward, and the model learns a different trade at each setting.

Now, the hazards, because a length penalty is a dangerous thing to put in a reward, and the trade has a long list of ways it goes wrong.

The general law was stated by the economist Charles Goodhart, about monetary targets, in the nineteen-seventies, and it is usually quoted in the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's later summary: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Length is a measure of effort. Make it a target, and the model will find ways to hit the target that do not involve the effort — which is what the trade calls reward hacking.

The model may pad: if it has been asked to think for four thousand tokens and has finished in two, it may fill the rest with repetition, to collect the reward for hitting the length. The model may hide its thinking: if only the thinking section is charged for, it may learn to do some of its reasoning in the answer, where it is free. This is not a hypothetical. The ThinkDial team found exactly that in their low mode, gave it a name — reasoning length hacking — and had to add a penalty for reasoning leaked into the answer; it is one good reason to charge for every token the model writes, and not merely the ones inside the thinking tags. The model may become cryptic: a length penalty rewards compression, and compression, pushed hard enough, produces reasoning in a private shorthand that no person can read — which matters, because reading a model's reasoning is one of the few windows we have into what it is doing. A study published this summer reported that models trained with length penalties became markedly less likely to admit, in their reasoning, that they had relied on a hint they had been given. Compress the thinking, and you may compress away the evidence. And, worst of all, the model may simply stop thinking when thinking was needed: a penalty set too high teaches a model to guess, and a model that guesses quickly is cheap, fast, and wrong. This, too, has been confessed in public. Elon Musk, writing last month about a forthcoming Grok model from xAI, said that it needed a few more days because the team might have penalised response length too much in reinforcement learning: the model still gave up on hard tasks — tasks it could do — too early, and was not yet rigorous enough in checking its work.

So the practical art of training an effort dial consists in a great many careful choices that the papers describe and the products conceal. Warm the penalty up, so that competence comes first. Never reward brevity in a wrong answer. Charge for all tokens, not some. Check that every level is at least as good as the level below it — that high effort never does worse than medium — since nothing in the mathematics guarantees it. Check that the levels are far enough apart to be worth having, and close enough together to be predictable. And measure, always, on questions the model was not trained on, because a model that has learned to game a length penalty on its training problems will game it everywhere.

So, here is where the argument has reached. The reasoning models learned to think at length because reinforcement learning paid for correct answers and long thought produced them; they never learned that thought costs. The effort dial is taught by writing the request into the model's input and putting the cost into the reward — one point for being right, minus a charge for length that depends on what was asked — so that one set of weights learns a family of behaviours, selected by a few words. The best image for an effort level is a price: it tells the model what a token of thought is worth, and leaves the model to decide, question by question, how many tokens this question deserves.

Part Five — One Model, Two Minds

So far we have watched the idea develop in papers. Now let us watch it arrive in products, because the commercial history of the effort setting, read carefully, tells us a good deal about how the laboratories trained it — more, sometimes, than they meant to.

At the end of twenty twenty-four, reasoning came in separate bottles. OpenAI had its ordinary models, such as GPT-four-o, and, beside them, its reasoning models, o-one and its successors. Alibaba had its ordinary Qwen models and, beside them, a separate reasoning model. DeepSeek had its V-three and, from January, its R-one. If you wanted a quick answer, you called one model; if you wanted a considered one, you called the other; and the choice of model was the effort setting. Two minds, in two bodies.

The first effort dial, strictly so called, appeared inside one of those bodies. When OpenAI made o-one available to developers in December of twenty twenty-four, it added a parameter called reasoning effort — written, in the interface, as one word joined by an underscore — with three values, low, medium, and high. The documentation was laconic: reducing reasoning effort, it said, could result in faster responses and fewer tokens used on reasoning. That was all. The next reasoning model, o-three-mini, at the end of January, wore its effort in its very name in the consumer product: o-three-mini-high was what you chose when you wanted it to try. Its announcement put the levels in terms a buyer could understand. At low effort, it said, the new model performed comparably with the previous small reasoning model; at medium, comparably with the previous large one; at high, it outperformed both. An effort level, in other words, was offered as a way of choosing which older model you would like to be served, at a fraction of the older price.

Then, in February of twenty twenty-five, came the first model to put both minds in one body. Anthropic released Claude three-point-seven Sonnet and called it, in its announcement, the first hybrid reasoning model on the market. The announcement explained the philosophy in a sentence I shall quote, because it set the terms of the argument that followed: Just as humans use a single brain for both quick responses and deep reflection, we believe reasoning should be an integrated capability of frontier models rather than a separate model entirely. One model, then, which could answer at once, or think first, as you chose. And when it thought, developers could set what the company called a thinking budget: tell the model to think for no more than so many tokens, from a minimum of one thousand and twenty-four up to its output limit of a hundred and twenty-eight thousand.

Two remarks in Anthropic's accompanying material are worth your attention, because they show the price-like behaviour of Part Four already present. The first is that the model's accuracy on mathematics, the company wrote, improved logarithmically with the number of thinking tokens it was allowed — the familiar curve. The second is a caption beneath a chart: even though the model was allowed to use the entire thinking budget, it generally stopped short. The budget was a ceiling the model was trained to respect, not a quantity it was trained to fill. It thought as much as the question seemed to need, up to the limit — which is what a model does when it has learned that thought costs something.

Two months later, in April, Google followed with Gemini two-point-five Flash, which it called its first fully hybrid reasoning model: thinking could be switched off entirely, or given a budget, in tokens, from zero upward. Google's technical report on the Gemini two-point-five family is unusually direct about how these models were made: they were, it says, trained with reinforcement learning to use additional computation at inference time, spending tens of thousands of forward passes in a thinking stage before responding. And it adds a sentence that I would ask you to hold on to: for any task, the model decides for itself how long to think before providing an answer; the budget only constrains it. A budget of zero turned thinking off; a special value told the model to decide entirely for itself.

Google's announcement of the Flash model put the point even more plainly, in a sentence that is as near to a published recipe as any of the large laboratories has come. The model, it said, is trained to know how long to think for a given prompt, and therefore decides automatically how much to think based on the perceived complexity of the task; the budget sets a cap, but the model does not use the full budget if the prompt does not require it. The announcement even gave examples of what it meant. Asked how to say thank you in Spanish, or how many provinces Canada has, the model should barely think at all. Asked the probability that two dice add up to seven, it should think a little. Asked for the maximum bending stress in a loaded cantilever beam, it should think hard. And the price list made the economics visible: at the preview, an output token produced with thinking switched on cost nearly six times as much as one produced with it off. Thought, for the first time, had a published price of its own.

And at the end of April, Alibaba released Qwen three, whose technical report gives the most complete public account of how a hybrid model is actually trained. I shall walk you through it, because it is the nearest thing we have to a published recipe.

The training after pretraining has four stages. The first two make a reasoning model; the second two teach it to be an ordinary one as well.

Stage one is a cold start: the model is fine-tuned on a carefully chosen set of long, worked chains of reasoning, so that it learns the form of careful thought before it is asked to discover anything for itself.

Stage two is reasoning by reinforcement learning, of exactly the kind we met with DeepSeek: verifiable problems, rewards for correct answers. The report gives one figure that conveys its power: over a hundred and seventy steps of this training, the flagship model's score on a hard mathematics competition rose from about seventy to about eighty-five.

Stage three is the one that matters to us, and the Qwen team called it thinking mode fusion. The reasoning model from stage two is fine-tuned again, on a mixture of two kinds of example. Some are thinking examples, with full chains of reasoning — generated, notably, by the stage-two model itself, and filtered for correctness. Others are non-thinking examples: direct answers. Each example carries a flag, in the user's message or the system's — the word think or the words no think, each with a slash before it — and the response obeys the flag. And here is the detail that gives the game away. In a non-thinking example, the thinking section is not omitted. It is present, and empty: the opening and closing markers of a thought, with nothing between them. The model learns that no think means an empty thought, and the format stays the same in both modes, which is why a developer can switch thinking off simply by supplying the empty thought. In longer conversations the flags were scattered at random through the turns, and the model was trained to obey whichever came last.

Stage four is general reinforcement learning, across a broad range of tasks, to make the fused model good at everything.

Now, the thinking budget. The Qwen report says something here that I quoted in passing in the third Part and must now quote properly, because it is the most interesting sentence in the document. Once the model had learned to answer in both modes, it naturally developed the ability to handle intermediate cases — to generate responses based on incomplete thinking. That is what made the budget possible: stop the thinking when the budget runs out, insert the sentence about the user's limited time, close the thought, and the model answers from what it has. And, the report adds, this ability was not explicitly trained. It emerged from the fusion. A model taught to answer with a full thought and with an empty one had learned, without being asked, to answer with half a thought.

But the report is honest about a cost, and the cost is the most important thing about hybrid models. After stages three and four, the model's performance in thinking mode on the hardest tasks — the mathematics competition, a demanding programming benchmark — actually went down. Teaching the reasoning model to be an ordinary model as well had blunted its reasoning. The team wrote that they chose to accept this trade-off, to enhance the model's overall versatility.

Three months later, they changed their minds. In July of twenty twenty-five, Qwen released updated models under the suffix twenty-five-oh-seven, and the hybrid was gone. There was now an Instruct model, which did not think at all, and a separate Thinking model, which always thought — and thought for longer than before. The two minds had been put back into two bodies, because, for the best results, they interfered with each other in one.

I find that retreat the most instructive episode in the commercial history, because it shows that the hybrid is not free. A model's weights are finite, and the habits of quick answering and of deep deliberation compete for them. Anthropic's single brain was a statement of philosophy; Qwen's split was a report of an experiment. Both were right about something. The integrated model is more convenient, and it gives you a dial within one set of weights; the separate models are each better at their one thing. The industry has, on the whole, chosen convenience and spent heavily to reduce its cost — and Qwen itself, a year later, was again shipping models whose reasoning depth could be set with an effort parameter. But the trade-off is real, and it is one more reason why a laboratory might not want to tell you exactly how its dial was made.

DeepSeek went the other way at about the same time: in August of twenty twenty-five its V-three-point-one model fused the two modes into one, with the choice made in the chat template, as Qwen had done. xAI did the same with a model it released that September, its reasoning and its quick answers handled, in the company's words, by the same model weights, steered via system prompts. DeepSeek's fourth generation, this year, offers three modes in one model — no thinking, thinking at high effort, and a mode it calls Think Max, which, the model card says, is selected by a special system prompt: the effort, once again, written into the input. The model card's own figures make the logarithmic shape vivid. On one notoriously hard examination of expert questions, its largest model scored under eight in a hundred without thinking, about thirty-four and a half at high effort, and under thirty-eight at max. The first step bought more than four times the score. The second bought three points, at a great deal more thought.

And OpenAI's open-weight gpt-oss models, which we met in the last Part, took a third course, offering not two modes but three levels of reasoning, selected by a line in the system prompt. By this summer the open laboratories had largely converged on that third course: Moonshot's newest Kimi model always thinks, and takes an effort level of low, high, or max, defaulting to max.

One more development of that year changed what effort means, and it concerns agents. In May of twenty twenty-five, Anthropic's Claude four models gained what the company called interleaved thinking: the ability to think, call a tool, read the result, think again, call another tool, and so on, with deliberation between every step rather than only before the first. For a model working on a long task, effort is no longer a single block of thought spent before it answers. It is spread across dozens or hundreds of steps — how much to deliberate before each action, how many actions to take, how carefully to check each result. And the laboratories have since reported exactly that pattern in the behaviour of their dials: one of Anthropic's models of twenty twenty-six, its notes say, thinks more at higher effort levels particularly on later turns in agentic settings, when the task has grown complicated. A chess game is not one decision but forty, and the clock governs them all.

Now watch the dials themselves evolve, because their history has a direction.

OpenAI's ladder grew at both ends. With GPT-five, in August of twenty twenty-five, came a new lowest level, minimal, which the company said would produce very few or no reasoning tokens, for when you want the first visible word as quickly as possible. In November, with GPT-five-point-one, came a level lower still, none, which forbids reasoning altogether, and which became that model's default. The company's guide to that model contains a candid line: the earlier minimal setting had sometimes led to executions that terminated prematurely — agents, given almost no room to think, simply giving up on their tasks. At the end of that year, a level called extra high, written as an x and the word high run together, arrived with a model built for long programming tasks. And this summer, a level called max. There are now seven rungs, from none to max, and the guide warns that not every model supports every value.

Anthropic's ladder grew too, and changed its nature on the way. With Claude Opus four-point-five, in November of twenty twenty-five, Anthropic introduced a parameter it called simply effort, and the company's announcement gave figures worth remembering: at medium effort, the new model matched the best score of its predecessor on a standard benchmark of real programming tasks while using seventy-six percent fewer output tokens; at its highest effort, it beat that score by more than four points while still using forty-eight percent fewer. And the documentation made a point that distinguishes this dial from a thinking budget: effort governs all the tokens in the response — the text, the tool calls, and the thinking, if there is any. A low-effort model does not merely think less. It makes fewer tool calls, combines operations, skips the preamble, and reports briefly. Effort, in Anthropic's sense, is about how thorough the whole response is, not merely how long the model deliberates.

In February of twenty twenty-six, Anthropic retired the thinking budget on its new models and replaced it with what it called adaptive thinking: the model decides, request by request, whether to think and how much, and the effort level guides that decision — low, medium, high, and max, with extra high added between the last two in April. The documentation now says, in a sentence that could serve as the epigraph of this whole Part: effort is a behavioural signal, not a strict token budget; at lower effort levels the model still thinks on sufficiently difficult problems, but thinks less than it would at higher effort levels for the same problem.

Put that beside the arithmetic of the last Part. A level, not a quantity. The model still thinks hard about hard questions at low effort — just less hard. That is precisely the behaviour of a model that has been taught a price, not a budget.

And the same documentation lets slip a fact that confirms the thesis of the evening about as directly as one could wish. It notes that the chosen effort value is rendered into the prompt — written, that is, into the text the model reads — which is why changing it in the middle of a conversation spoils the cache of the earlier turns. The dial is not a knob on the machinery. It is words in the model's input, which the model was trained to heed. The documentation adds that the scale is calibrated separately for each model, so that the same level name does not mean the same thing on two different models — just as one would expect if each model had been trained to its own prices.

And in July of this year, in a note for the users of its coding tool, Anthropic said the thing outright, in words I can do no better than to repeat. The effort level, it wrote, is sent to the model as part of the request, right alongside your prompt; the model was trained to understand how to behave at each effort level, and that learned behaviour is baked into the frozen weights. Effort, the note went on, is one more input the model responds to, the same way it responds to your prompt text, and it sets how thorough and certain the model needs to be before it considers the task done. At higher effort the model is more disposed to check further hypotheses and verify its work — but it generally won't, the note says, artificially inflate its usage on simple tasks, and the company's trainers pay close attention to overthinking, because it degrades effectiveness. In one example the note gave, the same prompt at a higher effort produced roughly seven times as many tokens, to reach a more confident answer. A few months earlier, in an engineering report, the company had described how the levels themselves are chosen: since, in general, the longer the model thinks the better the output, it calibrates its effort levels by picking points along the test-time-compute curve that give people the best range of options — the Pareto frontier, by its working name — and then chooses, as a product decision, which point to make the default. Request in the input; behaviour in the weights; levels as points on a curve; the default as a business choice. There, from one of the laboratories itself, is very nearly the whole of this evening's argument.

Google's dials made the same journey. Its budgets in tokens, from Gemini two-point-five, gave way with Gemini three, in November of twenty twenty-five, to a thinking level, low or high at first, with minimal and medium added a month later; and Google's guide to the new models says that they treat these levels as relative allowances for thinking rather than strict token guarantees — Anthropic's sentence, in Google's words. Three laboratories, then, within about a year, moved from asking you how many tokens the model should think for to asking you how hard it should try. I do not think that is a coincidence. A budget is a quantity, and the right quantity depends on the question, which the person setting the budget does not usually know in advance. A level is a price, which the person can reasonably choose — this matters a great deal, or this needs to be quick — and the model can apply question by question. The industry learned what the arithmetic of Part Four predicts.

Two more dials deserve a word, because they show how the idea spread beyond the laboratories.

The first is in your own tools. In the spring of twenty twenty-five, Anthropic's command-line coding assistant, Claude Code, recognised certain words in a prompt and translated them into thinking budgets: think was worth four thousand tokens; think hard, ten thousand; think harder, and a word coined for the purpose, ultrathink, about thirty-two thousand. The harness turned your English into a budget before the model ever saw it. The keywords have since been simplified; today, typing ultrathink adds an instruction to that one turn asking the model to reason more deeply, while the effort setting itself is chosen separately, from low to max. You may well have typed the word yourself, sir. If so, you were setting a price.

The second is a dial OpenAI added beside effort with GPT-five, called verbosity: low, medium, or high, governing not how much the model thinks but how much it says. The company's guide reports that, on one test, output grew roughly in proportion to the setting — about five hundred and sixty tokens at low, about eight hundred and fifty at medium, and about thirteen hundred at high — without any change to the reasoning. The separation is wise, and I commend it. Thinking and talking are different costs, borne by different people. The model's thinking costs you time and money; the model's talking costs the reader's attention. A question may deserve a great deal of thought and a two-line answer — most of the best answers do — and a dial that conflated the two would force you to buy one with the other.

So here is what the products tell us, read together. The hybrid model — one set of weights, thinking or not as asked — was trained by teaching a reasoning model to answer with an empty thought as well as a full one, and found, as a bonus, that it could answer from a partial one; and it paid for its convenience with some loss at the top, a cost one laboratory openly accepted and then, for a time, declined. The dials moved from quantities to levels, because a level is a price the user can choose and the model can apply question by question. And the most candid documentation now says it outright: effort is a behavioural signal, rendered into the model's input, calibrated per model, and heeded — not a knob on the machinery.

Part Six — Knowing When to Think

Every instrument we have met so far takes its orders from outside. You set the effort; the model obeys. Even the price of Part Four, which lets the model adapt question by question, is a price that you chose. The last stage of the story, and the frontier this autumn, is the model that sets its own effort — that looks at a question, judges how hard it is, and decides how much thought it deserves. That is what a strong chess player does at every move, and it is what Charlie Snell's paper of twenty twenty-four showed to be worth so much: a strategy that adapts its thinking to each question's difficulty beat the best fixed strategy while using about a quarter of the computation. The question is how to teach it.

Let me introduce the cast of this Part before anything moves, because there are four kinds of player and they are easily confused. There are models trained to choose between thinking and not thinking. There are models trained to know how much budget they have left. There are models trained to answer well whenever they are stopped. And there are routers — separate systems that choose, on the model's behalf, which model or which effort a question should get. We shall take them in that order.

The first kind is the simplest to state. Give a hybrid model the choice, at the very start of its response, between thinking and not thinking, and reward it for choosing well. Two papers of May of twenty twenty-five did exactly this, and each ran into the same difficulty, which is instructive.

But first, a small irony. The behaviour these papers set out to teach had already appeared, uninvited, in the wild. In January of twenty twenty-five, DeepSeek's own instructions for running R-one noted that the model tended, on certain queries, to bypass its thinking altogether — to open its thinking tags and close them again at once, with nothing between — and that this could hurt its performance; the company recommended forcing every response to begin with an open thought. The model had, in its way, begun to judge for itself when thinking was unnecessary. Its makers, reasonably enough at the time, regarded this as a fault, and corrected it. A few months later, the research world was trying to teach models to do exactly that — only to do it well.

The first, from Tsinghua, was called AdaptThink. Its starting point was the observation we met in the third Part, that a reasoning model made to skip its thinking — given an empty thought — often does perfectly well on easy questions. So the authors trained the model, by reinforcement, to decide for itself whether to begin its response with a thought or without one, under a constraint: use the quick mode as often as possible, provided accuracy does not fall below that of the original model. Brevity, in other words, is pursued only so far as correctness allows. In practice the reward gave the quick mode a small bonus, and measured every answer against what the original model had managed on the same question. There was one practical obstacle, which recurs throughout this Part: a model that has always thought will almost never, of its own accord, try not thinking, and so it never discovers that not thinking would have been fine. The authors forced the exploration: during training, half of the model's attempts were made to begin with an empty thought and half with a real one, so that both choices were always being tried and compared. On the small models they tried it on, the result was a model that skipped thinking on most of the easy questions and thought on most of the hard ones, cutting the average length of its responses by fifty-three percent while improving its accuracy by a couple of points.

The second, from the National University of Singapore, was called Thinkless, and it made the choice explicit: the model's very first token is one of two special control words, one meaning short answer and the other meaning think, and everything after follows from that choice. And here the authors met the difficulty I promised. In ordinary reinforcement learning of the kind we met with GRPO, the training signal for a response is spread across all its tokens. A long thinking response has thousands of tokens; the choice of mode is one token. So the learning signal for the one decision that matters most — should I think at all? — is drowned by the signal for the thousands of tokens that follow it, and the model's choice of mode tends to collapse early in training: in their experiments, ordinary training drove the model, within about a hundred and twenty steps, to answer short almost every time. The Thinkless authors' remedy was to separate the two: to give the mode token its own share of the learning signal, weighted so that the decision is learned at its own pace, apart from the content that follows. The reward itself is worth hearing, because it is the price of Part Four in its plainest form: a correct short answer earned a full point; a correct long answer, half a point; a wrong answer of either kind lost a point. Think when you must, the reward says, but thinking costs half your fee. With that, the model learned to use long thinking only on a minority of questions where it helped, cutting the use of long reasoning by between a half and nine tenths on the easier benchmarks, while choosing to think far more often on the hardest.

That difficulty is worth a moment's reflection, because it is a small instance of a large truth. The decision of how hard to think is a single act, taken once, at the start. Everything else is execution. A training method that weighs every token equally will always under-weight the decision, because the decision is one token and the execution is thousands. Teaching a model to judge difficulty needs a training signal aimed at the judgement itself.

The same idea has been taken into production. ByteDance trained a model for its chat service to make the same decision — reason, or answer at once — with reinforcement learning tuned to sit at a chosen point between quality and cost, and reported that on its real traffic the model chose to reason on as few as three requests in a hundred, cutting the average length of its responses by more than two thirds.

The second kind of player is the model that knows its budget. Here the idea is to tell the model, not only at the start but during its thinking, how much it has used and how much remains — the chess player's glance at the clock. Several open models have done this, and the clearest example is one you can read for yourself. ByteDance's Seed team released an open model in August of twenty twenty-five whose thinking budget is visible inside its thought. Give it a budget of five hundred and twelve tokens, and its reasoning is punctuated, every so often, by a small bracketed reflection in a tag of its own. Partway through, it writes: I have used one hundred and twenty-nine tokens, and there are three hundred and eighty-three tokens remaining for use. A little later: I have used two hundred and fifty-eight tokens, and there are two hundred and fifty-four remaining. Later still, a hundred and nineteen. And at the end: I have exhausted my token budget, and now I will start answering the question. It is, quite literally, the chess player glancing at the clock — three glances, and then the move. The team's documentation adds a detail that tells you exactly how the behaviour was made: users are advised to choose budgets that are multiples of five hundred and twelve — five hundred and twelve, a thousand, two thousand, and so on up to sixteen thousand — because the model was extensively trained on those intervals. The budget, in other words, is a number written into the model's input, and the model was trained at particular values of it, as one would train on any other instruction. A research system from the same summer, called BudgetThinker, inserted reminders of the remaining budget at intervals as special control words, to much the same effect. And in April of twenty twenty-six, Anthropic added to its interface what it called task budgets: an advisory number of tokens for a whole agentic task — the thinking, the tool calls, the results, and the output together — with the model, in the company's words, seeing a running countdown and using it to prioritise its work and finish gracefully as the budget is consumed. Whether planning one's thinking from the first token or adjusting it at the twentieth glance, a model that can see the clock can do what budget forcing never allowed: decide what to leave out.

The third kind of player is the model trained to be interrupted. You will recall from the third Part that a thought cut off is not the same as a short thought, and that Qwen found, as a happy accident, that its fused model could answer sensibly from a partial one. In May of twenty twenty-five, researchers at Salesforce made the accident deliberate, in a method they called Elastic Reasoning. They gave the thinking and the final solution separate budgets, and, during reinforcement learning, they deliberately cut the thinking short at its budget and required the model to write its solution anyway, rewarding it, as always, for getting the answer right. The model learned, under that pressure, to make its partial thoughts useful — to reach a usable conclusion early and refine it afterwards, rather than leaving everything to the end. It is the method of the examiner who makes students put down their pens at intervals and write a conclusion from whatever they have; the students learn to keep a conclusion ready. The authors reported that their models held up well under budgets much tighter than they had been trained on, and spent markedly fewer tokens even when no budget was imposed. A related method from the same month rewarded the answers a model would give if stopped at several different points, so that it learned to be good at every point — to reason, in the authors' word, as an anytime reasoner.

And the fourth kind of player is not a model at all, but a dispatcher.

The idea of routing is older than the reasoning models. In twenty twenty-three, researchers at Stanford proposed what they called FrugalGPT: send each question first to a cheap model, check whether the answer looks trustworthy, and escalate to a more expensive model only if it does not — a cascade, like a hospital's triage, in which the specialist sees only the patients the nurse could not help. In twenty twenty-four, a group behind the public model-comparison site Chatbot Arena released RouteLLM, a router trained on the arena's own records of which model's answer people preferred, which learned to predict, from a question alone, whether a cheaper model's answer would do. Both reported large savings for small losses: FrugalGPT claimed it could match the best single model of the day at a small fraction of its cost, and RouteLLM that it could cut costs by as much as eighty-five percent on one standard benchmark while keeping ninety-five percent of the stronger model's quality. A cascade may pay twice, for the cheap answer and then the expensive one; a router sends each question to exactly one model, and so must be right the first time. The idea has lately been carried inside agents: a paper of this spring trained a small router to choose, at every step of an agent's task, the lowest effort level that would still do, and reported cutting the reasoning by about half with little loss of success.

The grandest router yet was the one OpenAI put inside ChatGPT with GPT-five, in August of twenty twenty-five. The company described it as a real-time router that quickly decides which model to use based on conversation type, complexity, tool needs, and explicit intent — if you say think hard about this in the prompt, for example — and said that the router is continuously trained on real signals, including when users switch models, preference rates for responses, and measured correctness. It is a striking specification. The router learns difficulty from your behaviour: when you are dissatisfied enough to switch to the thinking model yourself, that is a signal that the router should have sent you there.

The router's debut was not a happy one. On the day after the launch, OpenAI's chief executive, Sam Altman, explained that the automatic switcher had broken for a good part of the day, and that GPT-five had consequently seemed, in Altman's words, way dumber. Many users disliked the router even when it worked, because it took the choice out of their hands, and the company restored the choice. By the end of that year it had, by reports, stopped routing automatically for its free users altogether. A router must be right about difficulty, and people are very quick to notice when it is not.

The laboratories' response has been to move the judgement inside the model, and OpenAI has published the clearest measurements of what that looks like. In September of twenty twenty-five, describing a version of GPT-five trained for programming, it reported that, on its own employees' traffic, the new model used about ninety-four percent fewer tokens than its predecessor on the easiest tenth of requests — and, on the hardest tenth, thought for twice as long. In November, introducing GPT-five-point-one, it said that it had overhauled the way it trained the model to think, so that it spent fewer tokens on straightforward tasks and remained persistent on difficult ones, adapting its thinking time more precisely to the question: on a representative spread of ChatGPT tasks, roughly twice as fast as its predecessor on the quickest, and twice as slow on the slowest. The spread had doubled at both ends. That is what learning to judge difficulty looks like from outside: not less thinking or more thinking, but a wider gap between the two. And in August of this year, the company announced that, for its paying users, the same model now powered both the instant answers and the deeper reasoning, chosen with a slider — adding that moving the slider toward more effort should feel like the model taking extra time for a more comprehensive answer, not like switching to a different model. A year after the router, the router's work had been folded into the model and handed back to the user as a dial. Anthropic's adaptive thinking, from February of twenty twenty-six, is described in its documentation in almost the same terms: the model evaluates each request and determines whether to think and how much, with the effort setting as soft guidance. And Anthropic's release notes for a model of May of twenty twenty-six say that it triggers reasoning only when a turn needs it, wasting fewer thinking tokens than its predecessor at the same effort level. The dispatcher has been absorbed into the patient.

The same documentation records two behaviours of adaptive thinking that I find quietly revealing. The first is that a model's thinking per request tends to decrease as a conversation grows longer — as though, having established the ground in the early turns, it judged the later ones easier, which they often are. The second concerns changing the effort level in the middle of a conversation, which can now be done message by message. A change made in the ordinary way, the documentation warns, steers the model less reliably than one would like, because its earlier replies were written at the previous level, and it tends to stay consistent with them. The model, that is, takes its own past behaviour as evidence of what is expected of it — the conversation is part of its input, and its input is where its instructions live. A guest who has been answering briskly all evening does not easily become expansive because someone has quietly changed a setting.

Now, all of this rests on a judgement that can be wrong, and the way it goes wrong deserves its own beat.

Difficulty is not always visible on the surface of a question. The trick question looks easy and is not; the intimidating question, full of jargon, is sometimes trivial. A model that judges difficulty by appearances will be fooled exactly as a hurried person is fooled. And the two kinds of mistake are not equally costly. Think too little about a hard question, and you get a wrong answer — delivered, as like as not, with complete confidence, and with no sign to the user that anything went wrong. Think too much about an easy question, and you get a right answer, late and expensive. The first error is silent and damaging; the second is visible and merely wasteful. So the designers of these systems, when in doubt, lean toward thinking more, and the defaults show it. Most of Anthropic's models default to high effort, and this spring its coding tool raised its default, for a while, to extra high, because in a coding agent a wrong answer is expensive. The exceptions prove the rule. OpenAI made no reasoning at all the default for GPT-five-point-one in its programmers' interface, where a developer can be trusted to ask for more. And Anthropic's newest flagship, released last month, defaults to medium — because, by the account of its documentation and its early customers, medium on the new model does about what high did on the old. A default is a guess about difficulty, made once on behalf of everyone; as the models get better at guessing for themselves, the defaults can afford to come down.

Master Ian, at not quite four months, has so far been issued with a single effort setting, which is maximum. Every problem that comes before him — hunger, a sock, the existential affront of being put down — receives the full treatment, at full volume. He has not yet learned to estimate the difficulty of a problem before committing to it. I am assured by those who know that this comes with time. In the models, it comes with training, and, as we have seen, it does not come easily.

Two cautions, before we close, and both concern what effort really measures.

The first is that the thinking we can see is not necessarily the thinking that happens. In the spring of twenty twenty-five, Anthropic published a study, Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think, in which models were given subtle hints toward an answer and then asked to solve the problem. When a model used the hint, it often failed to mention it in its reasoning: Claude three-point-seven Sonnet mentioned it about a quarter of the time, and DeepSeek's R-one about two times in five. So when we measure a model's effort in tokens of visible thought, we are measuring what it writes, which is related to, but not identical with, the computation that produces its answer. The effort setting governs the writing. How much of the writing is the reasoning is a question still under study — and, if anything, a growing one. Last month OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, wrote that the company's ability to rely on monitoring a model's written reasoning is progressively diminishing, in part because the models are becoming much smarter even without putting their reasoning into words at all.

The second is that effort is now a lever of safety as well as of quality. The guardrails placed around these models at inference time are another evening's subject, but I will note here that OpenAI reported in January of twenty twenty-five that letting a reasoning model think longer generally made it harder to manipulate, and that later that year it began routing conversations that showed signs of acute distress to a reasoning model, regardless of which model a person had first selected — effort, in that case, chosen by the guardrail and not by the user. There is a subtler link, too, which OpenAI's researchers set out in December of twenty twenty-five, in a study of how well a model's reasoning can be monitored. Constraints on the length of reasoning, they wrote, create incentives for models to reason less; models that think for longer tend to be more monitorable; and so there is a trade between effort and size — a smaller model run at higher effort can match a larger model run at lower effort while being easier to watch, at the price of more computation, which they called a monitorability tax. How hard a model thinks is not only a matter of cost and accuracy. It is, increasingly, a matter of care.

So the frontier stands like this. A model can be taught to decide for itself whether to think — though the decision, being a single token among thousands, needs a training signal of its own. It can be taught to watch its budget as it works, and to answer well wherever it is stopped. Routers can make the judgement on its behalf, trained on what users do — and are now being absorbed into the models themselves, as adaptive thinking. And the whole arrangement leans, deliberately, toward thinking more, because underthinking fails silently and overthinking only fails expensively. The frontier is the model that knows how hard the question is. It is not there yet; but it is a good deal closer than it was a year ago.

For the Minutes

We began, sir, with Elijah Williams sitting over a single move for an hour while Howard Staunton seethed, and with the sandglass and the clock that the chess world invented to make every player answer, at every move, the question of how much thought the move deserved. You asked how a language model is taught to answer the same question when you set its effort to low, medium, or high. Let me set down the answer for the minutes, in the order in which we found it.

The first thing to record is what effort is made of. A transformer does a fixed amount of computation for every token it produces, so it can think longer only by writing more: its reasoning is written into its own working memory, one token at a time, and effort, for such a machine, can only mean how much it writes before it answers. Writing out its reasoning lets it solve problems it otherwise cannot. And the benefit of writing more is real but logarithmic: each doubling of the thought buys roughly the same improvement as the last, at twice the price. The right amount of thought is therefore not as much as possible. It is whatever a further point of accuracy is worth to you, on this question — which is a decision about price, and the reason a dial exists at all.

The second is why the models think at such length. They were made by reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards, which paid them for correct answers and for nothing else; longer thinking produced more correct answers, so the training reinforced the habits that make thinking longer — checking, reconsidering, trying again — and the length grew, unbidden, from hundreds of tokens to thousands, with an aha along the way. Part of the growth was an accident of bookkeeping, which treated long wrong answers more gently than short ones. But the essential fact is simple. The models learned that more thought pays. Nothing in their training ever taught them that thought costs. And so they thought at length about two plus three.

The third is how the cost was put back, and it is the centre of the evening, so I give it its third and final airing. The effort you request is written into the model's input — a line in the system prompt, a flag in the message, a value rendered into the text the model reads. In training, the reward paid for being right and charged for length, and the charge depended on what the input requested: heavy at low effort, light at high. Across many thousands of problems and many requests, one set of weights learned a whole family of behaviours, and the words in the input select among them. The open papers showed how; the open models' cards confirmed it; and one of the large laboratories has now said it in as many words, describing its reinforcement learning as maximising correctness subject to a penalty on thinking time. The best image for what it learned is a price. An effort level tells the model what a token of thought is worth, and leaves it to judge, question by question, how many tokens this question deserves — which is why, as the most candid documentation now puts it, effort is a behavioural signal and not a strict token budget, and why a model at low effort still thinks about a hard question, only less. The industry's dials moved, within about a year, from budgets in tokens to named levels, because a budget is a quantity the user cannot know in advance and a level is a price the user can reasonably choose.

Around that trained core sit the blunt instruments, still in use: the hard cap that stops a thought mid-sentence, the forced stop with its polite sentence about the user's limited time, the word wait that makes a model think again, the empty thought that makes it not think at all. They act on the model from outside. And beyond them lies the frontier: the model that judges for itself how hard the question is — choosing whether to think, watching its budget as it works, answering well wherever it is stopped — and the routers, trained on what users do, that are now being absorbed into the models as adaptive thinking. Because thinking too little fails silently and thinking too much fails only expensively, the whole arrangement leans, on purpose, toward thinking more.

If I may offer some counsel for your own systems, sir, it is this.

Treat effort as a parameter of your product, and measure it. Take a few hundred questions that represent your real traffic, run them at every effort level, and plot accuracy against cost and against latency. You will usually find a knee in the curve, past which a great deal more money buys very little more accuracy; set the default at the knee, and raise it only for the work where a wrong answer is expensive.

Let me put invented but representative numbers through it, so that the arithmetic is in your ear. Suppose that on a hundred of your tasks, an agent at low effort succeeds eighty-two times at three cents a task; at medium, eighty-eight times at seven cents; at high, ninety times at eighteen cents. The step from low to medium costs four cents more a task, four dollars over the hundred, and buys six more successes: about sixty-seven cents for each additional task done. The step from medium to high costs eleven cents more a task, eleven dollars over the hundred, and buys two: five dollars and fifty cents for each. Whether either step is worth taking depends entirely on what a success is worth to you — and that is a question about your business, not about the model. The model can tell you what thought costs. Only you can say what it is worth. Do not assume that the highest setting is the best: the documentation of at least one laboratory warns that its maximum level adds significant cost for relatively small gains, and can lead to overthinking. Remember that the same level name means different things on different models, so measure again when you change models. Keep effort and verbosity apart: let the model think as hard as the question needs, and then say as little as the reader needs. In an agent, remember that effort governs the number of tool calls as well as the thinking. Do not set a hard limit on output so tight that the model is stopped in the middle of its thought, because you will pay for the thought and receive no answer; if you need to bound the spending, give the model a budget it can see. And, a small practical point that costs real money: since the effort setting is written into the model's prompt, changing it in the middle of a conversation throws away the cache of the earlier turns.

Blaise Pascal, in the sixteen-fifties, apologised at the end of one of his Provincial Letters for its length: he had made it longer than usual, he wrote, only because he had not had the time to make it shorter. For a person, brevity is the expensive thing; a short letter takes more effort than a long one, because the writer must decide what to leave out. The reasoning models arrived at the same truth from the other side. Length came free to them — reinforcement paid for it — and brevity had to be taught, by charging for every token, until a model would decide, as Pascal did, what to leave out. That is the economy of thought, in Mach's sense and in the market's. Thinking is costly; the art is to spend it where it pays.

For reading, should you want the page as well as the voice. The L-one paper, by Pranjal Aggarwal and Sean Welleck, for the cleanest published account of training a model to honour a requested length. The Qwen three technical report, and in particular its pages on thinking mode fusion and the thinking budget, for the nearest thing to a published recipe for a hybrid model — and an honest account of its cost. OpenAI's model card for gpt-oss, for effort levels set in the system prompt. The DeepSeek R-one paper, for the length that grew unbidden and the moment of reflection. The survey by Yang Sui and colleagues, Stop Overthinking, for a map of the whole field of efficient reasoning. And the documentation on effort from any of the large laboratories, read with an eye for the sentences that tell you how the thing was made.

Elijah Williams, I should add in fairness, was a strong player as well as a slow one, and he beat Staunton fair and square, clock or no clock. The trouble was never that he thought. It was that nothing obliged him to decide how much each move was worth. The clock did that for chess. The effort setting, and the training behind it, does it for the machines.

There being no other business, sir, I shall withdraw.
