# Episode Four -- Who Goes There?

*Best Effort -- A Remedial Course in Networking*

## Safes Within Safes

Good day, sir.

Four times a year, in one of two quite unremarkable buildings in the United States, a small group of people meets for a ceremony. One of the buildings is in Culpeper, a small town in Virginia, not far from Washington. The other is in El Segundo, in California, near the airport in Los Angeles. The ceremonies alternate between them, and they are, as ceremonies go, rather peculiar.

They are not secret. Quite the reverse. Every ceremony is filmed, and streamed live, for anybody in the world who cares to watch. Every ceremony follows a script, written and published in advance, and the script is read aloud, one step at a time, by a person whose title is the *Ceremony Administrator*. Before each step is performed, it is read out. After it is performed, it is ticked off, and the time is written down. There are witnesses, whose only job is to watch. There are auditors from an outside firm, whose only job is to watch more carefully. Nothing happens in that room that is not announced beforehand and recorded afterwards.

The participants reach the room through a series of locked doors, each needing its own credential, and no single person holds the credentials for all of them. And at the heart of the building there are two safes.

The first safe holds a machine. It is called a **hardware security module**, and it is a box built to do one thing: to hold a cryptographic key, and to use that key when properly asked, while never, under any circumstances, letting the key itself out. Not to its owners, not to its makers, not to anyone. The key is made inside it, and it never leaves in any form that a person could use.

The second safe holds the means of waking the machine. Inside it is a row of small locked boxes -- safe-deposit boxes, like a bank's -- and each box belongs to one person, who alone holds its key. Inside each box is a smart card. The people who hold those boxes are called **Crypto Officers**, and there are seven of them for each building. They are not employees of the organisation that runs the ceremony. They are volunteers, drawn from the Internet's technical community in countries around the world, who travel in for the occasion; the trade calls them *Trusted Community Representatives*. And the machine will do nothing at all until at least three of the seven have opened their own boxes, with their own keys, and handed over their own cards.

So: the machine, in one safe. The cards that wake it, in the other. And no fewer than three people, from different parts of the world, needed to bring the two together. Safes within safes, and people within people.

When the machine is awake, it does one thing. It signs. The company that publishes the very top of the Internet's directory -- it is Verisign, in Virginia -- has sent ahead a short list of the keys it intends to use for the coming three months. The list is carried into the room, fed to the machine, and signed with the key the machine holds. The signed list is carried out again. Then the cards go back into their boxes, the boxes into their safe, and the machine into its own safe; each is sealed, and every seal is numbered and written down. Everyone goes home. The whole affair takes a few hours, and it is very, very dull, which is precisely the point.

There is one further precaution, for the case nobody likes to name: the loss of both buildings at once. A copy of the key exists, locked by encryption, so that it is useless as it stands. The means of unlocking that copy has been divided among seven more people, around the world, called the *Recovery Key Share Holders*; only if enough of them came together, each bringing their own share, could the key be restored. One of those seven, for some years, was a man whose name you will hear in the third Part of this evening. How he came to hold that share is one of the better ironies the Internet has played on itself, and I shall save it until then.

The key in that machine is the root **key-signing key** of the Domain Name System -- the trade says the *root K-S-K*. It sits at the very top of the system that checks that the answers given by the Internet's directory are genuine. Every resolver in the world that checks those answers carries a copy of the public half of that key, built in, as the one fixed point from which all its checking begins. The trade calls a built-in starting point of that kind a **trust anchor**. Everything else is checked against it; it is checked against nothing. It is simply believed. The trust anchor is the place where the questioning stops.

And on Sunday, the eleventh of October, two thousand and twenty-six -- six days after these words were written, so that by the time you hear them it may well have happened -- that key is being replaced. The key that has signed the top of the directory since October two thousand and eighteen is being retired. Its successor was made at one of these ceremonies in two thousand and twenty-four, and has sat quietly in the directory since January of last year, so that the world's resolvers could learn it in good time. On the eleventh, it begins its work.

It is only the second time this has ever been done. The first replacement was scheduled for October two thousand and seventeen, and was postponed, shortly before the day, for a full year -- because measurements showed that too many of the world's resolvers had not yet learned the new key, and would, on the day, have stopped believing anything the directory told them. When a single key sits at the top of everything, changing it is not a matter of an afternoon.

Why does any of this matter to a working engineer, who has never seen the inside of a safe in Culpeper? Because tonight's question is the hardest of the five, and the ceremony is what it looks like when people take that question seriously.

The question is this. *Who* is speaking, and who is listening?

Every conversation we discussed last week was between two addresses. But you do not type addresses. You type names. So the first half of the question is: when you type a name, who tells you which address it means, and why should you believe them? And the second half is harder. When the answer comes back, and your laptop opens a conversation with that address, how do you know that the machine answering is the one you meant -- the bank, and not somebody pretending to be the bank? And how do you know that nobody between you is listening?

The Internet's answers to those two halves are two great systems. The first is the Domain Name System, DNS, the directory that turns names into addresses. The second is the system of certificates and ciphers that stands behind the padlock in your browser, whose protocol is called TLS. They were built by different people, at different times, for different purposes. And yet, when you look at them closely, they turn out to have exactly the same shape. Each is a *tree of delegated authority*. At the top, each rests on a handful of keys that everybody simply agrees to believe. Below the top, each level vouches for the level beneath it. And in each, whoever controls the top controls, in principle, everything below.

That is the evening's thesis, and I shall say it again before we are done, because it is the most important thing I have to tell you tonight: the Internet's names, and the Internet's trust, both hang from a very small number of keys at the top of two trees.

We shall begin with names: with the directory, how it came to be, how a question is asked of it and answered, and why the answers are sometimes stale and occasionally wrong. Then we shall return to the locked room, and see what the key in that machine is for. Then trust: how two strangers can agree on a secret while a third listens to every word, which I promised you in the first evening is the most beautiful idea in this course; then the papers that strangers show one another to prove who they are; and finally the single round trip in which your browser does all of it, every time you visit a web site. We begin, as so many things in this course have begun, at a research institute in California, with a single file.

## Part One -- The Phone Book That Could Not Be One Book

In the beginning, the Internet's directory was a file.

It was kept at SRI -- the Stanford Research Institute, in Menlo Park, the second node of the ARPANET, where the first evening's *LO* was received. A group there called the Network Information Center, directed from nineteen seventy-two by a remarkable woman named Elizabeth Feinler -- everybody called her Jake -- kept the official list of every computer on the network: its name, and its address. The list lived in a single file, called HOSTS dot T-X-T. When a new machine joined the network, somebody sent its name and address to Jake Feinler's group, and the group added a line to the file.

And every computer on the network kept a copy. Let me walk it, because the procedure is the whole story. When a program on your machine wished to reach a machine by name -- the center's own machine, say, which was called S-R-I dash N-I-C -- it did not ask anybody. It looked the name up in its own copy of the file, found the address beside it, and used that. Every so often, the people who ran each machine fetched a fresh copy of the whole file from SRI, to pick up the new arrivals. The procedure was written down, with a charming informality, in the document that specified the file: you connected to the machine at SRI by the file-transfer protocol, logging in as user *ANONYMOUS*, with the password *GUEST*, and took the file away with you.

So: one file, kept in one place, by one group of people. Every machine holds a copy. Every machine fetches the whole thing again from time to time. That is how names worked on the ARPANET, and for a network of a few hundred machines it worked perfectly well.

It could not last, and the reason is a small piece of arithmetic that you will meet again in your own working life. Suppose the network doubles in size. Then the file doubles in length, because it has a line for every machine. And the number of machines fetching the file also doubles, because every machine fetches it. Twice as many copies of a file twice as long: the traffic needed to keep everybody up to date goes up four times. Double the network, and the cost of the directory quadruples. Grow it tenfold, and the cost goes up a hundredfold. The document that eventually replaced the file put it with mathematical sobriety: the bandwidth consumed in distributing a new version, it said, is proportional to the *square* of the number of hosts in the network. By the early nineteen-eighties the file's size and the rate at which it changed were, in the words of another document, *near the limit of manageability*. Every new machine meant more work at SRI, and more traffic everywhere.

And there was a second problem, less arithmetical and more human. The names in the file were flat: one list, with no structure, so that every machine on the network needed a name that no other machine anywhere had. Two universities could not both call a machine *Venus*. Every new name had to be checked against every old one, by the same small group of people, in one office in California. A single list of names for the whole world is not a directory. It is a queue.

So in nineteen eighty-three Jon Postel -- the editor of the RFCs, whose sentence closes these evenings -- asked a colleague at his institute in Marina del Rey, Paul Mockapetris, to design something better. Mockapetris's design was published in November nineteen eighty-three, as RFCs eight eighty-two and eight eighty-three, and revised four years later, in November nineteen eighty-seven, as RFCs ten thirty-four and ten thirty-five. Those two documents of nineteen eighty-seven are, with a great many later additions, still the definition of the system your laptop used this morning. It is called the **Domain Name System**, and everybody calls it DNS.

I should add one historical detail, which I mention purely in the interests of accuracy. The first program ever written to answer questions in the Domain Name System -- written by Mockapetris in nineteen eighty-three and eighty-four, for the computers at his institute and at SRI -- was called *Jeeves*. I make no comment, sir, beyond observing that the name was well chosen: it answered questions promptly, kept its own counsel, and was in due course replaced by something from Berkeley. The something from Berkeley is called BIND -- the Berkeley Internet Name Domain -- and it still answers a great many of the world's questions today.

Mockapetris's idea was to replace the single list with a *tree*, and to hand out the branches.

Look at a name like *www dot example dot com*. The trade reads it from the right. At the far right, invisible because everybody leaves it out, is the top of the tree, called the **root**; written out in full, the name would end with one more dot, standing for the root, after the *com*. Beneath the root are the **top-level domains**: *com*, and *org*, and *net*, and *edu*, and *gov*, and a two-letter domain for nearly every country -- *ca* for Canada, *uk* for the United Kingdom, *jp* for Japan -- and, since a great expansion in the twenty-tens, hundreds of others, from *london* to *pizza*. At the time of writing there are about fourteen hundred and forty of them. Beneath *com* is *example*. Beneath *example dot com*, whatever its owner pleases: *www*, *mail*, *api*, and anything else.

A name written out in full, all the way to the root, with that final dot for the root included, is called a **fully qualified domain name**. In daily life nobody types the final dot, and the software quietly assumes it. But hold on to it, because next Part we shall meet a machine that does *not* assume it, and the consequences are expensive.

Now the essential move. The organisation that runs the root does not keep a list of every name in the world. It keeps only a list of the top-level domains, and for each one, the names of the servers that are responsible for it. The organisation that runs *com* -- Verisign again, as it happens -- does not keep a list of every machine in every company. It keeps a list of the names registered under *com*, and for each one, the names of the servers responsible for it. And the owner of *example dot com* keeps the list of the names under *example dot com*, and answers for them. Each level knows only the level below it, and only enough to say: *not mine; ask over there*.

That handing-down of responsibility is called **delegation**, and it is the whole genius of the design. The root delegates *com* to Verisign's servers. *Com* delegates *example dot com* to whatever servers the owner of *example dot com* has named. The owner may delegate further -- *europe dot example dot com* to a different team's servers, say -- or not. Each portion of the tree that is managed as one unit, by one set of hands, is called a **zone**, and a server that holds a zone's records and answers questions about it with authority is called an **authoritative server** for that zone. The pointer that a parent keeps for each of its children -- *the servers for example dot com are these* -- is a record of a kind called *NS*, for name server, and it is the stitch that holds the tree together.

Let me say it once more, because everything tonight hangs from it. The directory is a tree. The root knows the top-level domains. Each top-level domain knows the names beneath it. Each owner knows its own names. No one holds the whole directory, and no one needs to. A new company can register a name under *com* this afternoon, set up its own servers, and add a thousand names beneath its own without asking anybody's permission for any of them. The work of keeping the directory is divided among millions of hands, and the queue in California is gone.

Let me walk that afternoon, because you have very likely lived it without seeing the machinery. There are three parties, and the trade has a name for each. You, who want the name, are the *registrant*. The organisation that keeps the zone for *com* -- Verisign -- is the **registry**. And the company you actually pay, through a web page with a shopping basket, is a **registrar**: one of many retailers licensed to sell names under *com* and to pass the orders to the registry. First move: you buy *example dot com* from a registrar. Second: the registrar tells the registry to add *example dot com* to the *com* zone, together with the names of the servers that will answer for it -- your own, or, far more likely, your DNS provider's. Third: within minutes, the servers for *com* begin handing out that pointer to anybody who asks about your name. Fourth: on your provider's servers, you create the records that say where *www dot example dot com* and *mail dot example dot com* actually are. The registry knows only that your name exists and who answers for it. Everything else is yours. That is delegation, bought for the price of a modest lunch.

There is one small knot in the stitching, and it is worth untying, because you will see its name in the configuration of any domain you register. Suppose the servers for *example dot com* are themselves named *ns one dot example dot com* and *ns two dot example dot com*. Then the parent's pointer says: to find anything under *example dot com*, ask *ns one dot example dot com*. But to find the address of *ns one dot example dot com*, you would have to ask the servers for *example dot com* -- which are the very servers whose address you are trying to find. A perfect circle. The parent breaks it by handing over, along with the names of the child's servers, their addresses as well. Those extra addresses are called **glue** records, and they are exactly what the name suggests: the little dab of extra information that keeps the delegation from falling apart.

The names themselves came quickly. The first name ever registered under *com* was *symbolics dot com*, belonging to a maker of computers for artificial-intelligence research, on the fifteenth of March, nineteen eighty-five. Only five other companies bothered that year, among them the network's builders, BBN, and the Digital Equipment Corporation. Today there are well over a hundred and fifty million names under *com* alone.

And now let us go up to the top of the tree, because the top is where tonight began. The root zone is small: a list of about fourteen hundred and forty top-level domains and their servers. But every question that cannot be answered from somebody's memory starts there, so it must be answered fast, and from everywhere, and it must never, ever be unavailable. The servers that answer for the root are called, naturally, the **root servers**, and there are, by one count, thirteen of them, and by another count, more than two thousand.

Thirteen is the count of their *names*. They are called *a dot root-servers dot net*, *b dot root-servers dot net*, and so on through the alphabet to *m* -- thirteen letters, run by twelve independent organisations: universities and companies, NASA and two arms of the American military, a Japanese research project, a Swedish operator, non-profit bodies in Europe and America, and ICANN, of which more in a moment -- with Verisign running two of the letters. Why thirteen, and not twelve, or twenty? Because of a size limit in the original design. A DNS answer was meant to fit in a single small packet -- five hundred and twelve bytes at most -- and the list of the root servers' names and addresses, which every resolver needs before it can ask anything at all, had to fit inside that limit. In the early nineteen-nineties the list was approaching it, and two engineers, Bill Manning and Paul Vixie, proposed giving all the root servers names under one common suffix, *root-servers dot net*, so that the suffix could be written once and the names abbreviated against it. That squeezed in room for thirteen, and thirteen it has been ever since.

But more than two thousand is the count of *machines*. You met the trick last week: anycast. Each of the thirteen letters is not one computer but a whole constellation of them, scattered around the world, all announcing the same address into the Internet's routing, so that a question sent to *k dot root-servers dot net* is answered by whichever copy of *k* happens to be nearest you. At the time of writing, the root server operators count two thousand and forty-five of these copies, in cities on every continent but Antarctica, and there is very probably one in Toronto. Thirteen names; two thousand machines; one zone, the same everywhere.

Who decides what goes into that zone? Here the story takes a turn that I find instructive, because it shows that the top of a technical tree is, in the end, a social arrangement. In January nineteen ninety-eight the root zone was compiled by a company, Network Solutions, under contract to the United States government, and published from its server, the *A* root, in Virginia. The other root servers copied the zone from there. That winter there was a fierce argument about who should control the root in future -- a company, a government, or some new body answering to the Internet's own community -- and the American government was days away from publishing its proposals. Jon Postel ran the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority from his office at the institute, and had in many ways been the directory's guardian since its birth. And on the twenty-eighth of January, nineteen ninety-eight, he sent an email to the operators of eight of the root servers -- those run neither by the American government nor by Network Solutions itself -- asking them to copy the zone, from now on, from a server at his institute instead.

Most of them, perhaps all, complied. The data did not change by a single character: Postel's server took the zone from Network Solutions and passed it on unaltered. He called it a test. But for about a week, the master copy of the Internet's directory was, in effect, being distributed by a man in an office in Marina del Rey on his own authority. The United States government did not see it as a test. A senior adviser to the President, Ira Magaziner, who was leading the government's work on the future of the directory, made his displeasure known -- by his own later account, he warned Postel that the redirection could bring criminal charges -- and Postel asked the operators to switch back. Within the year a new body had been created to take on the work: a non-profit called the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers, ICANN, said *eye-can*, incorporated at the end of September nineteen ninety-eight. Postel died a few weeks later. It was another eighteen years before the American government gave up its last formal oversight of the root, in two thousand and sixteen; and it is ICANN, through its IANA staff, that runs the ceremonies in Culpeper and El Segundo today.

I tell you the story not for its drama, though it has some, but for its lesson, which belongs to the evening's thesis. The top of the tree is not a technical fact. It is an agreement among a few dozen organisations and a few governments about whose list to copy. Everything beneath it -- every name you have ever typed -- rests on that agreement.

So: the directory began as one file, copied everywhere, whose cost grew as the square of the network; and it was replaced by a tree. The root knows the top-level domains; each top-level domain knows the names beneath it; each owner knows its own. Each level hands responsibility to the next by delegation, and the servers that answer for each zone are its authoritative servers. At the very top sit the root servers: thirteen names, two thousand machines, by anycast. And the top of the tree is, in the end, an agreement about whose list to believe.

## Part Two -- Asking the Way

Now let us ask the directory a question, and watch it answered. There are five parties to the conversation, so let me introduce them all before anybody moves.

First, the **stub resolver**: a small piece of your laptop's operating system whose job is to ask questions about names on behalf of your programs. It never searches the tree itself. It only asks, and waits.

Second, the **recursive resolver**: a server that does the searching on your behalf. It may belong to your Internet provider, whose address your laptop was given by the box in the hall when it joined the network; or it may be one of the large public resolvers -- one dot one dot one dot one, or eight dot eight dot eight dot eight -- which you met last week as examples of anycast. In everyday speech the trade simply calls it *the resolver*.

Third, a root server. Fourth, a server for *com*. And fifth, an authoritative server for *example dot com*.

The question is the one you ask a thousand times a day without knowing it: *what is the address of www dot example dot com?*

First move. Your browser asks the stub resolver. The stub resolver looks in its own small memory of recent answers, finds nothing, and sends the question to the recursive resolver: one small packet, by UDP, to port fifty-three -- the door at which DNS has always listened.

Second move. The recursive resolver looks in its own memory, which is much larger. Suppose, for the sake of the walk, that it has only just been switched on, and knows nothing at all -- nothing except a short list, built into it, of the names and addresses of the thirteen root servers. So it sends the same question to one of them: *what is the address of www dot example dot com?*

Third move. The root server does not know. It never knows anything so specific; it knows only the top-level domains. But it does not answer *I don't know*. It answers with a pointer: *not mine. Names under com are answered by these servers -- here are their names, and here are their addresses.* An answer of that kind, which does not answer the question but says whom to ask instead, is called a **referral**.

Fourth move. The resolver asks one of the *com* servers the same question, word for word. And the *com* server, too, replies with a referral: *not mine. Names under example dot com are answered by these servers, and here are their addresses.*

Fifth move. The resolver asks one of the servers for *example dot com*. And this server knows, because it is the authority for that zone. It answers: *www dot example dot com is at two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot ten* -- and attaches to the answer a number, which we shall come to in a moment.

Sixth move. The resolver hands the answer back to your stub resolver, which hands it to your browser, which can now, at last, begin the handshake of last week.

Let me give you the whole walk again, in a single breath. The stub asks the resolver. The resolver asks the root, and is pointed to *com*. It asks *com*, and is pointed to *example dot com*. It asks *example dot com*, and gets the answer. It hands the answer back. The trade has a pair of words for the two different kinds of asking you just heard. Your laptop's question to the resolver is a **recursive** query: it means *find out for me, however long it takes, and bring me the answer*. The resolver's questions to the root and to *com* and to *example dot com* are **iterative** queries: each server answers only what it knows, and points onward, and the resolver does the walking itself.

Now, that was the slow way, and almost no question is ever answered that way, because of the number attached to the answer. Every answer the directory gives carries a **time to live** -- TTL, the same three letters as the counter in every IP packet that you met last week, though here they mean something quite different. Here, the time to live is a number of seconds, chosen by the owner of the zone, for which the answer may be remembered and re-used. The resolver remembers everything it learned on its walk, each item for as long as its time to live allows. It remembers the servers for *com*, which the root told it, typically for two days. It remembers the servers for *example dot com*, which *com* told it. And it remembers the address of *www dot example dot com*, for as long as the owner of *example dot com* said it might -- five minutes, say, or an hour, or a day.

So the next time anybody using that resolver asks about *www dot example dot com*, the answer comes straight from memory, without a single further question, in the time it takes a packet to reach the resolver and come back -- a millisecond or two, if the resolver is near. And the next time anybody asks about any other name under *com*, the resolver skips the root entirely and goes straight to the *com* servers, whose addresses it remembers. In practice, a busy resolver consults the root servers only rarely, and answers most questions from memory. That memory is called the **cache**, and caching is what makes a system that could have been very slow into one that is nearly always fast.

And caching is also, as we shall see in a moment, the source of nearly every misunderstanding about DNS that you have ever heard repeated in a meeting. Hold on to the idea: *every answer is remembered by everyone who received it, for its time to live, and nobody can make them forget sooner.*

Before the misunderstandings, the vocabulary. The pieces of information the directory holds are called **resource records**, or just *records*, and each has a type. Let me take the types you will meet in three small groups.

The first group gives addresses. A record of type *A* gives an address in IP version four. A record of type *quad-A* -- written as four capital As -- gives an address in IP version six. When your browser asks for the address of a name, it usually asks for both, at the same moment.

The second group points elsewhere. A record of type *NS*, for name server, you have already met: it says which servers are the authority for a zone, and it is how a parent delegates to a child. A record of type *MX*, for mail exchanger, says where to deliver e-mail addressed to the domain. And a record of type **CNAME**, for *canonical name*, says that one name is simply an alias for another: *www dot example dot com is really example dot cdn-provider dot net; go and ask about that instead.* CNAMEs are how you point your own names at a content delivery network, or at a cloud load balancer, whose addresses are not yours to know and change without notice.

The third group holds notes. A record of type *TXT* holds a short piece of free text. It began as a curiosity and has become indispensable, because when a service wants you to prove that you control a domain -- before it will issue you a certificate, or send e-mail on your behalf -- it asks you to put a particular string of text into a TXT record, and then it looks. If the text appears, you control the zone. There are others -- *SOA*, the zone's own particulars; *SRV*, for locating services; and, newest, *HTTPS* records, which tell a browser in advance how a site would like to be connected to -- but those three groups are the ones you will live with: addresses; pointers; notes.

Now, the first of your rituals. You have very likely been told, by a DNS provider's documentation or a colleague's sorrowful face, that you cannot put a CNAME on the bare name of your domain -- on *example dot com* itself, as opposed to *www dot example dot com* -- and that if you want the bare name to point at your load balancer, you must use some special record of your provider's own invention, called something like *ALIAS*, or a feature called *CNAME flattening*. Here is why. The original specification laid down a rule: *if a CNAME record is present at a node, no other data should be present* -- because an alias that also carried data of its own could say one thing while the name it pointed to said another. But the top of every zone -- the bare name, which the trade calls the zone's **apex** -- must carry other data: the NS records that make the delegation work, and the SOA record that describes the zone. So a CNAME at the apex would break the rule, and the rule wins. Providers worked around it by looking up the target themselves and handing out its addresses as if they were the apex's own. And in two thousand and twenty-three a proper remedy was finally standardised: the HTTPS record I mentioned, which, among other things, can alias the apex without breaking anything. The ritual, then, is the scar of a rule written in nineteen eighty-seven to keep aliases honest.

The second ritual concerns the answer *no*. When you ask about a name that does not exist, the authoritative server says so, with a reply the trade calls *N-X-domain* -- written in the tools as one word in capitals, N, X, DOMAIN, for *non-existent domain*. And that answer, too, is cached -- for a time set by the zone's own SOA record. That is called **negative caching**, and it was standardised in nineteen ninety-eight by a document that observed, with commendable candour, that the protocol allows an answer to be cached for up to sixty-eight years, and recommended, for the negative kind, something nearer one to three hours. Now picture the trap. A deployment script checks whether a new name exists, before anybody has created it. The resolver asks, receives *no such name*, and remembers the *no*. A minute later, the script creates the record. And for the next hour, everybody using that resolver is told, firmly and incorrectly, that the name does not exist. If you have ever created a record, tested it at once, been told it did not exist, and then watched it mysteriously begin to work an hour later, you have been bitten by a cached *no*.

The third ritual is the largest, and it is a myth that has cost the trade a great deal of needless waiting. You will have heard it said that *DNS changes take forty-eight hours to propagate*. They do not, because nothing in DNS propagates at all. Nothing is pushed anywhere. When you change a record, the change takes effect at your authoritative server at once, and anybody who asks the authoritative server from that moment gets the new answer. What lingers is the *old* answer, in the caches of every resolver that asked before you made the change -- and each of them will keep it only for the time to live it was given when it asked. If the time to live was a day, some of the world will see the old answer for up to a day. If it was five minutes, for up to five minutes. The forty-eight hours is folklore from an age of long times to live and badly behaved caches. The truth is simpler: *the old answer lives exactly as long as you told the world it might.*

And so the ritual that you have performed before a migration -- *lower the TTL a day or two beforehand* -- now explains itself. Let me walk it. Suppose your record has a time to live of one day, and you intend to move your site to a new address on Thursday. On Tuesday, you change the time to live to five minutes, and change nothing else. Then you wait for one full day -- the *old* time to live -- so that every resolver in the world that cached your record with its day-long lifetime has let it expire, and fetched it again with the new, short one. On Thursday, you change the address. Within five minutes, every well-behaved resolver in the world has the new one. And afterwards, once you are satisfied, you raise the time to live again, so that the world is not asking your servers every five minutes forever. The day of waiting is not superstition. It is the time it takes for the world to forget your old promise.

I said *well-behaved* resolvers, and the qualification matters, because some caches are not under your control at all -- including some inside your own programs. The most famous is Java's. For many years, a Java program running with the platform's security manager switched on cached every successful lookup *forever*: it asked once, at startup, and never asked again. A service that had been running for a month would cheerfully go on connecting to an address that had been retired weeks earlier. Without the security manager, the default was thirty seconds -- and since the security manager has been permanently disabled in recent versions of the platform, thirty seconds is now the rule. But the folk memory of the forever-cache is the reason so many Java services carry a setting with a long dotted name, *networkaddress dot cache dot ttl*, set by somebody who once spent a very long night discovering that their program had never heard of the migration.

And there is a resolver inside every one of your Kubernetes pods that deserves its own warning, because it turns one question into eight. Kubernetes gives each pod a resolver configuration with a *search list* -- a list of suffixes to try appending to short names, so that a pod can say *database* and mean *database* in its own corner of the cluster. Alongside the search list goes an option called **ndots** -- the letter N and the word *dots* -- which Kubernetes sets to five. It means: *if a name has fewer than five dots in it, try it with each of the search suffixes first, and only then as it stands.* Now suppose your pod asks for *api dot example dot com*, which has two dots. Two is fewer than five. So the resolver first tries *api dot example dot com* with the pod's namespace suffix appended, and is told *no such name*. Then with the next suffix: *no such name*. Then the third: *no such name*. And only then does it ask about *api dot example dot com* itself, and get an answer. And since it asks for both the IP version four and the version six address each time, that is up to eight questions where one would have done, six of them answered *no*. Every call your service makes to an outside name may be paying that tax. The cures are two. Either write the name fully qualified, with the final dot for the root that I asked you to hold on to in the last Part -- *api dot example dot com dot* -- which tells the resolver that the name is complete and must not be extended. Or set the pod's *ndots* lower. The trailing dot looks like a typing error, and is in fact the most precise thing in the configuration.

One last point about how the questions travel, because it explains a fourth ritual, and a firewall rule you may one day write. DNS was designed to ask and answer in single small UDP packets, and the original specification limited an answer carried that way to five hundred and twelve bytes. An answer too large for that was cut short, with a flag set to say *truncated*, and the resolver was expected to ask again over TCP, which has no such limit. Five hundred and twelve bytes was ample in nineteen eighty-seven. It is not ample for answers with many addresses, or with the signatures we shall meet in the next Part. So in nineteen ninety-nine Paul Vixie published a set of extensions -- the trade calls them E-D-N-S -- by which a resolver can announce that it will accept larger answers over UDP. Larger, but not too large: a UDP answer bigger than the path's packet size must be broken into fragments, and you will remember from last week how much the Internet loves fragments. So in October two thousand and twenty the makers of the world's principal DNS software, with several large operators, agreed together on a sensible limit -- twelve hundred and thirty-two bytes, chosen to fit inside the smallest packet that IP version six guarantees -- and agreed that anything larger should go by TCP. Which brings us to the ritual. If you ever write a firewall rule that allows DNS through on UDP port fifty-three and forgets TCP port fifty-three, everything will work perfectly -- until the day an answer is too large, and then that name, and only that name, will mysteriously fail to resolve. DNS needs TCP too. The standards have said so, in capitals, since two thousand and sixteen.

So: a question about a name is asked by the stub resolver in your laptop of a recursive resolver, which walks the tree from the root, by referrals -- root to *com*, *com* to *example dot com* -- until an authoritative server answers. Every answer carries a time to live, and is cached by everyone who receives it for that long, so that most questions are answered from memory in a millisecond or two. Nothing propagates; old answers expire. The rituals follow: no CNAME at the apex, because the apex must carry other records; a cached *no* after a premature check; lowering the time to live a day before a migration; the Java cache that never forgot; the trailing dot that saves a Kubernetes pod six needless questions; and TCP port fifty-three, open beside UDP.

## Part Three -- The Keys to the Root

Here is a question that the directory's designers did not, at first, think very hard about. When the answer comes back -- *www dot example dot com is at two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot ten* -- how does the resolver know that it is true?

For most of the directory's history, the honest answer was: by circumstantial evidence. Every DNS question carries a number chosen by the asker -- an identifier, sixteen bits long -- and the answer must carry the same number, so that a resolver with many questions outstanding can tell which answer belongs to which. The answer must also come back from the address the question was sent to, and arrive at the port it was sent from. If all of that matched, the answer was believed. Nothing in it was signed. Nothing in it was proved. An answer that looked like a reply to your question was taken to be one, and remembered, and handed out to everybody else who asked, for as long as its time to live allowed.

You will see at once why that matters. A resolver serves thousands of people. If a false answer were ever planted in its cache, everybody who asked it about that name would be sent to the wrong place, and would have no way of knowing. The trade calls that **cache poisoning**, and for many years it was regarded as a real but difficult thing -- a matter of extraordinary luck.

In early two thousand and eight a security researcher named Dan Kaminsky, working on something else entirely, noticed that it was nothing like as difficult as everybody believed: that the resolvers of the world could be made to accept false answers far more easily than anyone had thought, and not merely for a single name, but for whole domains at a stroke. What he did next is the part of the story I want you to remember. He did not publish. He told the people who wrote the world's resolvers, privately. On the thirty-first of March, two thousand and eight, sixteen people from the companies and projects that made the world's DNS software met, in secret, on Microsoft's campus near Seattle -- most of them, it is said, not knowing why they had been summoned until he explained. They agreed on a remedy. And on Tuesday, the eighth of July, more than eighty vendors released their fixes on the same day -- billed at the time as the largest synchronised security update in the Internet's history. The details were to be kept quiet for a month, to give the world time to patch. They leaked in a fortnight, which is about what the trade expected. Kaminsky told the full story on the sixth of August, at a conference in Las Vegas, to a room in which there was standing room only.

The remedy was more randomness. From then on, a resolver chose not only a random identifier for each question, but also a random port to send it from, so that a genuine answer had to match both, and anybody hoping to impersonate one faced odds many thousands of times longer. That made the problem hard again. But everybody involved said plainly that it was a mitigation, not a cure. Randomness makes a forgery improbable. It does not make the answer *true*. The cure, if there was to be one, was to have the answers signed.

And there was one waiting, already standardised, and almost entirely unused. It is called **DNSSEC** -- said *D-N-S-sec*, for DNS Security Extensions -- and it had been published in its modern form in March two thousand and five. One of the engineers who later held a key at the ceremonies wrote that it took Kaminsky's discovery to get it over the line. Two years later, the root was signed.

Let me show you how it works, because it is the evening's thesis made into a machine.

The idea is a signature on every answer. The owner of a zone has a pair of keys -- a private key, which it keeps secret, and a public key, which it publishes in the zone itself, for anybody to fetch. It signs every set of records in its zone with the private key, and publishes each signature alongside the records. A resolver that receives an answer also receives its signature, fetches the zone's public key, and checks: was this answer signed by the holder of that key? If anything in the answer has been altered by so much as a letter, the check fails. How such signatures work -- how a public key can check what only a private key can make -- is the subject of the next Part, and I shall ask you to take it on trust for a few minutes.

But you will see the difficulty at once. The resolver fetched the zone's public key *from the zone*. How does it know that the key is genuine, and not a forger's key, with forged signatures made by it? It needs somebody to vouch for the key. And the somebody is the parent.

So here is the second half of the design. Each parent zone publishes, alongside its pointer to each child, a fingerprint of the child's key -- a short digest from which the key can be recognised -- and the parent signs that fingerprint with its *own* key. That record is called a **delegation signer** record, DS, and it is the cryptographic twin of the NS record from Part One. The NS record says *the servers for example dot com are these*. The DS record says *and the key for example dot com is this one*.

Now walk it, with the cast announced. The resolver; the answer for *www dot example dot com*; the zone *example dot com* and its key; the zone *com* and its key; the root and its key; and, built into the resolver, the trust anchor.

First. The answer arrives, signed with the key of *example dot com*. The resolver checks the signature against that key. It is good -- *if* the key is genuine.

Second. To trust the key of *example dot com*, the resolver looks in *com* for the delegation signer record for *example dot com*: the fingerprint of its key, signed by the key of *com*. The fingerprint matches. That is good -- *if* the key of *com* is genuine.

Third. To trust the key of *com*, the resolver looks in the root for the delegation signer record for *com*: the fingerprint of the key of *com*, signed by the root's key. It matches. That is good -- *if* the root's key is genuine.

Fourth. And the root's key, the resolver does not check against anything at all. It compares it with the trust anchor built into it: the copy of the root's public key it was given when it was installed. They match. The chain is complete, from the trust anchor down to the answer, every link signed by the link above.

And if any link fails -- a signature that does not check, a fingerprint that does not match -- a validating resolver will not hand you the answer at all. It will tell you that the server failed. Better no answer than a false one. That is the whole design: every zone signs its own records; every parent vouches for its children's keys; and the root is vouched for by nobody, but believed by everybody.

There is one refinement you must have, because it explains the ceremony. Each zone in fact keeps *two* pairs of keys. The first, the **zone-signing key**, does the daily work: it signs the zone's records, and it can be changed whenever the owner likes, as often as it likes. The second, the key-signing key, does only one thing: it signs the zone's set of keys. It is the key-signing key whose fingerprint is lodged with the parent. The point of the division is convenience. The workhorse key can be changed every few months without troubling the parent at all, because the key-signing key vouches for whatever workhorse is currently in harness; and the key-signing key, which the parent knows about, need change only rarely.

And now you can see what happens in Culpeper and El Segundo. The root's workhorse -- its zone-signing key -- belongs to Verisign, which signs the root zone with it every day. The root's key-signing key belongs to nobody but the machine in the safe. Four times a year, Verisign brings in the list of workhorse keys it intends to use over the next three months, and the ceremony signs that list with the root's key-signing key. That is the *short list of keys* from the beginning of the evening. That signature, made in a locked room in front of witnesses, is what every validating resolver in the world checks, in the end, every time it believes an answer.

And you can see, too, why replacing the root's key-signing key is such a business. For an ordinary zone, the parent's fingerprint is simply updated. But the root has no parent. Its fingerprint is held by *everyone*: by every validating resolver on Earth, as its trust anchor. So to change the key, every one of them must learn the new one. The root was first signed on the fifteenth of July, two thousand and ten, with a key made at the first ceremony, in Culpeper, a month before. That key's successor was published years in advance of its use; resolvers that follow the standard procedure learn a new root key automatically, by seeing it vouched for by the old one, steadily, for a month. And yet in two thousand and seventeen the measurements showed that too many had not learned it, and the change was put off for a year, to the eleventh of October, two thousand and eighteen. The key that took over that day is the one that stops signing on Sunday, the eleventh of October, two thousand and twenty-six, in favour of the key made in two thousand and twenty-four. The trust anchor at the top of the tree is the one thing in the system that cannot be vouched for by anything above it; and so it is the one thing that cannot be changed without asking the whole world to change with it.

I promised you an irony in the opening, and here it is. Among the seven people around the world who between them could restore the root key if both buildings were lost, the American representative, for many years, was Dan Kaminsky. The man who showed the world that its directory could not be trusted held, for the rest of his life, a share of the key that made it trustworthy. He died in April two thousand and twenty-one, at the age of forty-two. The New York Times called him a digital Paul Revere, which was apt: he rode through the night to raise the alarm, and then stayed to help guard the walls.

Now two cautions about DNSSEC, so that you do not leave thinking it more than it is. The first is that it proves; it does not conceal. Answers are signed, not encrypted, and anybody watching your network can still read every name you ask about. The second is that it is far from universal. The large public resolvers validate signatures, and so do many providers' resolvers. But only a modest fraction of the world's domains are signed even now, because signing a zone carries a cost in care -- a zone whose signatures expire, or whose keys are changed carelessly, simply vanishes for everybody whose resolver checks -- and many owners have judged the protection not worth the risk of that particular self-inflicted wound.

The first caution -- that anybody can read your questions -- has its own remedy, and it is newer. Since two thousand and sixteen there has been a standard for carrying DNS questions inside an encrypted connection of the kind we shall meet later this evening; it is called DNS over TLS, and it listens on port eight fifty-three. And since two thousand and eighteen there has been another, which hides DNS questions inside ordinary encrypted web traffic, so that they cannot be told apart from it at all: DNS over HTTPS. In February two thousand and twenty, Firefox began to turn it on by default for its users in the United States, sending their questions to a resolver of Mozilla's choosing rather than their provider's -- which delighted the privacy-minded, and dismayed a good many network administrators and some governments, who had grown used to seeing, and steering, what their users asked.

The best known of the resolvers that serve those encrypted questions sits at one dot one dot one dot one, and it has the best origin story in this Part. The address belonged to the research group of APNIC, the Asia-Pacific registry you met last week, who had found it unusable for ordinary purposes: so much stray traffic, from carelessness all over the world, was already being sent to it that the address was, in their words, continuously overwhelmed by a flood of garbage traffic. Cloudflare offered to receive and study the garbage, in exchange for the right to run a public resolver on the memorable address. And they launched it, their chief executive explained, on the first of April, two thousand and eighteen -- because the address has four ones in it, and the first of April is the first day of the fourth month. Never mind, he added, that it was a Sunday; never mind that it was Easter, and Passover, and April Fools' Day.

And now for the ritual that sits above every other in this half of the evening, which is not a setting but a saying. There is a short verse, of no known authorship, in the form of a haiku, that is passed among the engineers who run large systems:

*It's not DNS.*

*There's no way it's DNS.*

*It was DNS.*

It is funny because it is true, and it is true because of everything you have heard in the last three Parts. Every service you run depends on the directory before it depends on anything else: before a single byte of a request is sent, a name must become an address, and if it cannot, nothing else matters. And the directory fails in ways that look like something else. On Friday, the twenty-first of October, two thousand and sixteen, beginning a little after seven in the morning on the American east coast, a very large number of compromised household devices -- internet-connected cameras and video recorders among them, conscripted into a network of hijacked machines of the kind the trade calls a **botnet** -- flooded the servers of Dyn, a company that answered DNS questions for a great many famous web sites. Twitter, Netflix, Reddit, GitHub, Spotify, Etsy, the New York Times, and many more became unreachable for large parts of the day, in waves. Their own servers were perfectly healthy. Nobody could find them. The lesson the trade took was a ritual you may have seen in a large company's configuration: a domain served by two independent DNS providers, so that the loss of one is not the loss of the name.

And in October of last year, Amazon's largest cloud region suffered an outage whose first cause, by Amazon's own account, was a subtle fault in the automation that manages the DNS records for one of its own databases, which left the name of that database's regional endpoint with an empty record. For about three hours in the middle of the night, nothing could find the database, though the database was there; and the effects rippled through the services that depended on it for much of the following day. It was DNS. It is very often DNS. And the largest DNS story of all, I am keeping for the last evening.

So: for most of its life, the directory believed any answer that looked like a reply. Kaminsky showed in two thousand and eight how dangerous that was, and the trade patched it together, on one day, with more randomness -- a mitigation, not a cure. The cure is DNSSEC: every zone signs its records; every parent vouches for its children's keys with a delegation signer record; and at the top, the root's key-signing key is vouched for by nobody, but built into every validating resolver as its trust anchor. That is why it is kept in a safe and used only in ceremonies, and why changing it takes years. DNSSEC proves but does not conceal; DNS over TLS and DNS over HTTPS conceal. And when something inexplicable happens to a service, it is worth asking the directory first. It was very likely DNS.

## Part Four -- Alice, Bob, and Eve

So much for names. Your laptop now has an address for the bank. It opens a TCP conversation with that address, in the manner of last week, and the conversation is open. And now the second half of tonight's question presses. How do you know that the machine at the far end of that conversation is the bank? And how do you know that nobody in between -- in the coffee shop, in the provider's building, in some router in some country your packets happen to cross -- is reading every word?

You know the answer already, in outline, because I gave it to you in the first evening, and again last week. In the first evening, I told you that secrecy is a function that the network cannot be trusted to perform: that even if every link encrypted its traffic, the data would sit in the clear inside every router along the way, and so encryption that protects you from the network must be done by the ends, with keys that only the ends hold. And last week, when we met the impostor on your local network, answering for the box in the hall, I said that the real defence was not to make impersonation impossible, but to make the impostor's position worthless -- because if the conversation is encrypted end to end, the man in the middle holds only sealed envelopes. Tonight we see how the envelopes are sealed.

The protocol that does it is called TLS, for *Transport Layer Security*. It is the protocol behind the padlock in your browser, and behind the S in HTTPS, and behind nearly every conversation between your services that you have been told, at some point, to *encrypt in transit*. It began life at Netscape, in the middle of the nineteen-nineties, under another name, SSL -- the *Secure Sockets Layer* -- and the old name has never quite died, so that you will hear engineers say *SSL certificate* to this day for a thing that has not used SSL in a decade. The version you use now is TLS one-point-three, published in two thousand and eighteen.

TLS makes three promises, and it is worth having them by heart, because every piece of machinery in the next three Parts exists to keep one of them. The first promise is **confidentiality**: nobody between the two ends can read what is said. The second is **integrity**: nobody between the two ends can alter what is said without being detected. The third is **authentication**: you are talking to the party you meant to talk to, and not to an impostor. Confidentiality, integrity, authentication. The first two are, by now, the easy part. The third is the hard part, and it is where the second tree of the evening grows.

Before the machinery, the cast. Cryptography has a standing company of players, and they are part of the syllabus, because the literature cannot be read without them. Their names appeared in the famous paper of nineteen seventy-eight that introduced the RSA cipher, whose authors, wishing to describe two parties exchanging messages, wrote that they would *suppose that A and B -- also known as Alice and Bob -- are two users of a public-key cryptosystem*. Alice and Bob they have been ever since: the two parties who wish to talk privately. The cryptographer Manuel Blum, in nineteen eighty-three, described them as recently divorced, mutually distrustful, and still obliged to do business together, which I think captures the relationship between most pairs of services in a large company. In nineteen eighty-eight they acquired an enemy: **Eve**, the eavesdropper, who hears every word that passes between them but cannot change any of it. And in time they acquired a worse one: **Mallory**, the malicious attacker, who can not only listen but intercept, alter, and inject -- who can stand in the doorway and pretend to each of them to be the other. Eve listens; Mallory meddles. Hold on to the difference, because it is the difference between the first two promises and the third.

Now the first tool, and the oldest. A **symmetric cipher** is a way of scrambling a message with a key, such that only somebody holding the same key can unscramble it. One key, shared by both ends, used in both directions. That is the kind of cipher the ancients used, and it is the kind that carries the bulk of your traffic today: the standard one, chosen in an open international competition at the turn of the century and adopted in two thousand and one, is called the Advanced Encryption Standard, AES, and was designed by two Belgian cryptographers. Modern symmetric ciphers are fast -- your laptop can encrypt several gigabytes a second -- and, used properly, they cannot be broken by anybody without the key in any time that matters. Better still, the modern way of using them seals each message as well as scrambling it, so that if Mallory alters a single bit in transit, the receiving end notices at once and throws the message away. You may remember from last week the figures on corruption that slips past TCP's own checksum -- one packet in many millions, on a bad day. The seal on an encrypted message is a different order of thing: a check that nobody, by accident or by design, can satisfy without the key. So with a shared key, Alice and Bob have the first two promises: confidentiality, because Eve cannot read; and integrity, because Mallory cannot alter undetected.

*With a shared key.* And there is the whole difficulty, which has occupied the human race for as long as it has kept secrets. How do Alice and Bob come to share a key in the first place, when they have never met, and every word they exchange is overheard by Eve?

Let me make the difficulty domestic. Suppose that Miss Xenia's school has a rule that anybody collecting her, other than her parents, must give a password agreed in advance. The rule is a sound one. But notice what makes it work: the password was agreed *in private*, face to face, in the school office, before it was ever needed. If the only way to agree it were to shout it across the playground at pickup time, in front of every other parent and every passing stranger, the password would be worthless. And that is precisely the position of two computers on the Internet. There is no school office. Every word they exchange crosses the playground. Throughout history, the answer was a courier: a trusted person who carried the key, by hand, in a locked bag, from one end to the other before the conversation began. Governments and armies had couriers. Banks had couriers. When the hotline between Washington and Moscow was set up in nineteen sixty-three, its messages were enciphered with keys printed on paper tape, and each side's tapes were delivered to the other, in advance, through the embassies -- the most important conversation on Earth, and it still needed somebody with a bag. Your browser, about to visit a web site it has never seen before, does not have a courier.

In November nineteen seventy-six, two researchers at Stanford, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, published a paper whose first sentence I have always thought the boldest opening line in the literature of computing: *We stand today on the brink of a revolution in cryptography.* They were not exaggerating. The paper showed how two strangers could agree on a secret key while a third party listened to every word they said -- and still the third party would not know the key. Shouting across the playground, and yet the password stays private. It is called the **Diffie-Hellman key exchange**, and it is, as I told you in the first evening, the most beautiful idea in this course.

The usual way to explain it is with paint, and I shall use the usual way, because it is very good. The cast: Alice, Bob, and Eve, who sees everything that passes between them. And one fact about paint: mixing two colours is easy, but *unmixing* them -- taking a mixture and working out exactly which colours went into it -- is, for practical purposes, impossible.

First. Alice and Bob agree, in public, on a common starting colour. Yellow, say. Eve hears it. Everybody knows the yellow.

Second. Alice chooses a secret colour of her own -- red -- and tells nobody. Bob chooses a secret colour of his own -- blue -- and tells nobody.

Third. Alice mixes her secret red into a pot of the common yellow, and sends the mixture to Bob. Eve sees the pot go by: yellow-and-red, an orange. She cannot tell from it what the red was, because unmixing is impossible.

Fourth. Bob mixes his secret blue into a pot of the common yellow, and sends that mixture to Alice. Eve sees that pot go by too: yellow-and-blue, a green.

Fifth. Alice takes Bob's green, and stirs into it her own secret red. She now has yellow, and blue, and red. Bob takes Alice's orange, and stirs into it his own secret blue. He now has yellow, and red, and blue. The same three paints, in the same amounts, added in a different order -- and so exactly the same colour, a muddy brown that is now their shared secret.

And Eve? Eve has the yellow, the orange, and the green. To make the brown, she would need one of the secret colours -- and the only way to get one would be to unmix it from the orange or the green, which she cannot do. Every pot that crossed the playground, she saw. The secret was never in any of them.

That is the whole idea, and I should like you to have it so firmly that you could draw it on a napkin. Each side keeps one secret and sends one mixture. Each side adds its own secret to the other's mixture. Both arrive at the same result. And the listener, who saw only mixtures, cannot arrive there, because mixing is easy and unmixing is hard.

In the real thing, the paints are very large numbers, and *mixing* is a particular kind of arithmetic -- raising a number to a secret power, in a system of arithmetic that wraps around, like the hours on a clock -- which is easy to do and, as far as anyone knows, infeasible to undo when the numbers are large enough. Modern versions use a more compact family of mathematical objects called elliptic curves, which do the same job with much shorter keys. But the shape is the paint: public mixtures, private secrets, and a shared result that never crossed the wire.

There is a coda to the story, of a characteristically British kind. In nineteen ninety-seven, the British government's signals intelligence agency, GCHQ, revealed that its own mathematicians had found the same ideas years earlier, and had been forbidden to say so. One of them, James Ellis, had shown in nineteen seventy that what he called *non-secret encryption* was possible in principle. A young mathematician named Clifford Cocks, newly arrived, found a practical way to do it in nineteen seventy-three, reportedly in an evening. And his colleague Malcolm Williamson found what amounts to the Diffie-Hellman exchange in nineteen seventy-four. All of it was kept secret for more than twenty years. Ellis died a few weeks before the announcement, never having been able to tell anybody outside his work what he had done. Diffie and Hellman, meanwhile, received the Turing Award, the highest honour in computing, for the work of nineteen seventy-six -- in two thousand and sixteen, forty years after the paper. Hellman has said that the exchange ought really to bear the name of a third man as well, Ralph Merkle, whose related ideas came first; if names are to be attached to it, he wrote, it should be called Diffie-Hellman-Merkle.

Now the second tool, which grew from the same seed. Diffie and Hellman's paper also proposed something larger: a cipher with *two* keys instead of one, such that what one key locks, only the other can unlock. You publish one of the two keys to the world, and keep the other private. The scheme is called **public-key cryptography**, and the first practical version of it was found by three researchers at MIT -- Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman, whose initials make the R, S, and A of the RSA cipher -- in nineteen seventy-seven, and published the following year, in the paper in which Alice and Bob made their debut.

The use of public keys that matters most tonight is not secrecy but the **digital signature**. Run the two-key idea backwards. If Bob processes a message with his *private* key, anybody in the world can check the result with his *public* key, and the check succeeds only if the message was processed by the holder of that private key and has not been altered since. Nobody without Bob's private key can produce a signature that checks against his public key. So a signature proves two things at once: who made it, and that the message has not been changed. That is the tool I asked you to take on trust in the last Part. Every DNSSEC signature, every signature on every certificate, is one of these.

And now you can see why the paint is not enough on its own, and why the third promise is the hard one. Diffie-Hellman lets Alice agree a secret with *somebody* in perfect privacy. It does not tell her *who*. Suppose Mallory stands in the doorway between them. Then Alice, believing she is mixing paint with Bob, may in fact be mixing paint with Mallory -- while Mallory, at the same time, mixes a second pot with Bob, and passes messages between the two of them, reading and altering each one as it goes by. Each of the two exchanges is perfectly private. It is simply private with the wrong person. The trade calls Mallory's position the **man in the middle** -- the newer literature prefers *machine in the middle*, or an *on-path attacker* -- and it is the reason the key exchange must always be accompanied by proof of identity. Eve, the mere listener, is defeated by the paint. Mallory, who meddles, is defeated only by signatures.

So the bank must *sign* its half of the exchange, with a private key whose public half your browser knows belongs to the bank. If the signature checks, the mixture came from the bank, and no substitute can have come from Mallory, who cannot sign as the bank. That is authentication. There is one more thing to understand about the arrangement before we leave the cast, because it explains the shape of every secure conversation you have ever had. Public-key operations are slow -- thousands of times slower than a symmetric cipher -- and so nobody uses them to encrypt a conversation. They are used only at the beginning, and only for two jobs: to agree a fresh symmetric key, by the paint; and to prove who is on the other end, by a signature. Once that is done, the slow machinery is put away, and the whole of the conversation that follows is carried by the fast symmetric cipher, under the key just agreed. The trade sometimes calls this a *hybrid* design, and it is universal. The public-key machinery is the courier, arriving at the start with the key in a locked bag. The symmetric cipher is the conversation that follows.

And that leaves exactly one question, which is the question of the next Part. How does your browser, which has never visited this bank before, know which public key belongs to the bank?

So: TLS promises confidentiality, integrity, and authentication. Symmetric ciphers give the first two quickly, once both ends share a key. Diffie-Hellman gives them a shared key in public: each keeps a secret, each sends a mixture, each adds its own secret to the other's mixture, and the listener, who sees only mixtures, is left behind, because mixing is easy and unmixing hard. But a private conversation with an impostor is no use, and so each side must also prove who it is, with a digital signature made by a private key. The paint defeats Eve. Only signatures defeat Mallory.

## Part Five -- Papers, Please

How does your browser know which public key belongs to the bank?

The naive answer is a list: every web site's name, and beside it, its public key, kept somewhere official and copied to every browser in the world. And you will recognise that answer, because you heard it at the start of the evening. It is HOSTS dot T-X-T all over again: one list, kept in one place, copied everywhere, whose cost grows as the square of the network, and which can never keep up with the world. The phone book that could not be one book cannot be one book of keys either.

So the Internet did with keys exactly what it did with names. It built a tree, and handed out the branches.

The instrument is called a **certificate**. A certificate is a small document, a few hundred bytes to a few thousand, that says, in effect -- for a bank whose site, for tonight's purposes, is *www dot example dot com* -- *the public key written here belongs to whoever controls the name www dot example dot com; this statement is good from this date until that date; and it is made by such-and-such an organisation* -- and the whole document is signed with the private key of the organisation that made it. The organisation is called a **certificate authority**, CA for short, and its business is to check that an applicant really does control a name, and then to sign a certificate saying so. The format of the document is a standard with the unlovely name of *X-five-oh-nine* -- written as a capital X, a dot, and five hundred and nine -- which began life in nineteen eighty-eight as part of a grand international scheme for a single world directory of everything, a scheme that never came to pass, leaving behind only its certificates, which conquered the world instead.

Let me give you the important parts of a certificate in two small groups. First, what it says about the site: the *names* it covers, written in a field the trade calls the *subject alternative name*, which may list one name or many; and the site's *public key*. Second, what it says about itself: who *issued* it; the *validity period*, from and to; and, at the bottom, the issuer's *signature* over all the rest. Names and key; issuer, dates, and signature.

And how does your browser know the certificate authority's public key, to check its signature? By the same move as DNSSEC: by building a few of them in. Every operating system and every browser ships with a **root store**: a list of root certificates -- well over a hundred of them, belonging to several dozen certificate authorities -- chosen and policed by the makers of the browsers and operating systems, and believed by fiat. They are trust anchors, exactly as the root key in Culpeper is a trust anchor. Everything is checked against them; they are checked against nothing.

The root certificate authorities, in turn, guard their private keys very much as the root of the directory does: in hardware security modules, in safes, used rarely, in ceremonies, in front of witnesses. They do not sign certificates for web sites directly; that would mean taking the root key out of the safe every few seconds. Instead, each root signs a small number of certificates for **intermediate** certificate authorities, whose keys are kept online and do the daily work. And the intermediates sign the certificates for web sites, which the trade calls *leaf* certificates, since they hang at the end of the branch.

Now walk it, with the cast announced: your browser; the bank's server; the bank's leaf certificate, for *www dot example dot com*; an intermediate certificate; and, in your browser's root store, a root certificate.

First. When your browser connects, the server sends its leaf certificate, and with it, the intermediate certificate that signed it. That bundle is called the *certificate chain*.

Second. Your browser checks the signature on the leaf, using the public key in the intermediate. It checks out -- *if* the intermediate is genuine.

Third. Your browser checks the signature on the intermediate, using the public key of the root that signed it. And that root, your browser already has, in its store. So the signature checks against a key it believes by fiat. The chain is complete.

Fourth. Your browser checks that the leaf covers the name you actually asked for -- that *www dot example dot com* appears among its names -- and that today's date falls within its validity period.

Fifth, and essential: the server proves that it actually holds the private key matching the public key in the leaf certificate, by signing something fresh during the handshake, which we shall see in the next Part. Without that step, anybody could present a copy of the bank's certificate, which is, after all, a public document.

So: leaf, signed by intermediate; intermediate, signed by root; root, in your store, believed. Name checked; dates checked; possession of the key proved. And look at the shape of it. It is the same shape as DNSSEC. A chain of signatures, each vouching for the one below, up to a key at the top that is simply believed. That is the evening's thesis, for the second time: names and trust, two trees, each hanging from a handful of keys.

Now the rituals, of which this Part has several, every one of them a message from the chain.

The first is the error message you have met about a *hostname mismatch* -- the certificate is valid, but not for this name. It means exactly what it says: the fourth step failed. The certificate presented does not list the name you asked for. Perhaps you connected to a service by its address, or by an internal name, and it presented a certificate for its public name; perhaps somebody forgot to add a new name to the certificate when the service acquired one. There is a historical wrinkle here, too. Certificates once carried the site's name in a field called the *common name*, and the newer field for the list of names was added later. The standards said as long ago as two thousand that the list must be used and the old field was deprecated; but for years browsers quietly fell back on the old field when the list was missing. In April two thousand and seventeen, Chrome stopped falling back, and a great many internal certificates, made by hand with the old field only, failed overnight. If you have a script somewhere that makes certificates, and a comment in it about *subject alternative names* written in a tone of some feeling, that is why.

The second ritual is the one that works in the browser but not in your code. Your service connects to a partner's server, and fails with a complaint that it is *unable to get local issuer certificate*; yet when you open the same address in your browser, the padlock appears, and all is well. Nine times in ten, the partner's server is sending its leaf certificate *without* the intermediate -- skipping the first step's bundle. Your browser shrugs and supplies the missing intermediate itself, because browsers keep collections of known intermediates and will go and fetch one if necessary. Your program's library does no such thing; it cannot build the chain, and gives up. The fix belongs on the server: send the whole chain.

The third ritual is a flag, and I should like you to be a little afraid of it. When a TLS check fails in a hurry, somebody reaches for the option that turns the checking off -- in the command-line tool *curl*, the flag is called *insecure*, or *k* for short; in many libraries it is a parameter with a name like *verify*, set to false. And the request succeeds, and the problem appears to go away. Consider what has actually happened. The conversation is still encrypted: the paint is still mixed, and Eve, the listener, is still defeated. But the fifth step, and the third, and the fourth -- the steps that prove who is on the other end -- have been switched off. You now have a perfectly private conversation with *whoever answered*. Mallory, in the doorway, is delighted. The flag turns a sealed letter addressed to the bank into a sealed letter addressed to whoever opens the door. It has its uses on a laptop at three in the morning. It has no business in anything you deploy.

Now, the arrangement I have described has a flaw, and the flaw is in its generosity. There are well over a hundred root certificates in your browser's store, belonging to several dozen authorities, and many more intermediates beneath them -- and *any* of them may sign a certificate for *any* name. Your bank chose its certificate authority. But your browser will equally believe a certificate for your bank's name signed by any other authority it trusts. The strength of the whole system is therefore the strength of its *weakest* certificate authority. A specification written in nineteen ninety-six had said so in so many words: *a dishonest certificate authority can do tremendous damage*. Fifteen years later, a merely careless one showed how much.

In the summer of two thousand and eleven, intruders got into the systems of DigiNotar, a certificate authority in the Netherlands. On the tenth of July they used its keys to issue a certificate covering every name under *google dot com*. DigiNotar's staff discovered a number of rogue certificates and revoked them that month, but did not discover them all, and told nobody. At the end of August, a user in Iran posted a question on Google's help forum, asking whether what their browser had just shown them was a man-in-the-middle attack on their Gmail. It was. Chrome had refused the certificate, because Google had built into its own browser a record of which keys Google's certificates should be using -- the trade calls the practice **pinning** -- and the rogue certificate's key was not among them. A later investigation found that DigiNotar's keys had been used to issue at least five hundred and thirty-one rogue certificates, and that in the weeks before the discovery, some three hundred thousand distinct addresses had checked the status of the forged Google certificate -- more than ninety-nine in a hundred of them in Iran. The browsers removed DigiNotar from their root stores within days. The Dutch government took over its operations. On the twentieth of September, DigiNotar was declared bankrupt. A certificate authority that cannot be trusted is not worth anything at all, and is very quickly worth exactly that.

The trade's remedy took a few years, and it is one of the best ideas in this course, because it does not try to make the authorities honest; it makes them *visible*. It is called **Certificate Transparency**, and it was devised by engineers at Google and published in two thousand and thirteen. The rule is simple. Every certificate that a browser is to accept must first be recorded in public logs -- logs that can be added to, but never altered or erased, and whose integrity anyone can check. Since the spring of two thousand and eighteen, Chrome has refused any newly issued certificate that has not been logged. So a certificate authority can still, in principle, issue a certificate for your name to somebody else. But it cannot do so *secretly*. And any domain owner can watch the logs, and be told within minutes whenever a certificate is issued for one of its names -- a service several companies provide, and one worth subscribing to. A forgery like DigiNotar's, made today, would have to be logged before any browser would accept it -- and Google, watching the logs for its own names, would see it. Transparency does not prevent the lie. It makes sure the lie is told in public.

Then came the change that made all of this ordinary. Until the middle of the twenty-tens, certificates cost money, were bought by hand, installed by hand, and renewed by hand, once a year or once every few years -- and so a great part of the web simply did without, and sent everything in the clear. In two thousand and fifteen a non-profit authority called **Let's Encrypt** issued its first certificate, and in December of that year it opened to the public: certificates free of charge, issued and renewed entirely by machine, through a protocol it devised for the purpose, called ACME. The protocol proves that you control a name in the ways you heard about earlier this evening: by placing a particular file on your web server, or a particular piece of text in a TXT record in your zone. The effect was transformative. At the end of September last year, Let's Encrypt issued more than ten million certificates in a single day, and it now protects something approaching a billion web sites. The padlock, once a mark of a site that had spent money, is now simply the way the web is.

And with automation came the last of this Part's rituals, which is about time. Certificates expire, and the industry has decided, for good reasons, that they should expire sooner and sooner. The reason is that the alternative -- revoking a certificate whose key has been stolen -- has never worked well, because browsers cannot afford to check, on every connection, whether every certificate has been revoked; a certificate that expires quickly limits the damage of a theft without any checking at all. So the maximum lifetime of a certificate, which was once several years, was cut to three hundred and ninety-eight days in September two thousand and twenty. Under rules the industry adopted last year, it fell to two hundred days this March; it falls to a hundred days in March of next year, and to forty-seven days in March two thousand and twenty-nine. Let's Encrypt has issued ninety-day certificates from the start, and now offers some that last six days.

The point of lifetimes measured in days is that no human being can renew certificates by hand at that pace, and that is entirely deliberate. The industry is forcing everyone to automate, because the alternative has been tried, and here is how it goes. On Thursday, the sixth of December, two thousand and eighteen, the mobile data service of the British network O-two failed for about a day, for some thirty-two million people, together with SoftBank's in Japan and others in several countries -- and in London, the signs at bus stops that tell you when the next bus is coming went blank. The cause, said the chief executive of the equipment supplier, Ericsson, was an expired certificate in its software. In February two thousand and twenty, Microsoft's Teams stopped working for about three hours one morning, and Microsoft's statement was a single sentence: an authentication certificate had expired. The ritual of a calendar reminder for every certificate is a confession that renewal is done by hand; the ritual of automatic renewal, with an alarm for any certificate within a few weeks of its end, is the cure. A certificate always expires exactly when it said it would. It is the only thing in this field that is perfectly punctual.

So: your browser learns a site's public key from a certificate -- names and key; issuer, dates, and signature -- signed by a certificate authority. The authority's key is vouched for by an intermediate or a root; the roots are built into your device's store and believed by fiat. The chain is checked from leaf to root, the name and dates are checked, and the server proves it holds the private key. Any authority can sign for any name, so the system is as strong as its weakest member -- as DigiNotar showed -- and Certificate Transparency now makes every certificate public. Let's Encrypt made certificates free and automatic, and lifetimes are falling to weeks, to force the automation that would have spared O-two its bad Thursday. And the shape, once more, is the shape of the directory: a tree of signatures, hanging from a handful of keys.

## Part Six -- One Round Trip

We have all the pieces now. Paint, to agree a key in public. Signatures, to prove who mixed it. Certificates, to say whose public key is whose. And a fast symmetric cipher, to carry the conversation once the key is agreed. Let me show you how TLS one-point-three puts them together, in a single exchange of messages that your browser performs every time it visits a web site for the first time -- and which takes, from start to finish, one round trip.

The cast is short: your browser, which the protocol calls the *client*; and the bank's server. The TCP conversation of last week is already open. Its handshake has cost one round trip. Now comes the second.

First message, from the client. It is called the **ClientHello**, and it says, in effect: *Hello. I speak TLS one-point-three. Here are the ciphers I know. The name I want is www dot example dot com.* And then, without waiting to be asked, it does something bold: *and here, to save time, is my half of the paint already mixed* -- a mixture made with the common colour the client guesses the server is most likely to accept. That pre-mixed contribution is called the **key share**. In older versions of the protocol, the two sides first agreed on which paints to use, and only then mixed them, which cost an extra round trip. TLS one-point-three guesses, and nearly always guesses right.

Second message, from the server: the **ServerHello**. *Hello. We shall use this cipher, and your choice of paint. Here is my half of the mixture.* And at this instant -- with one message each way -- both sides have everything they need. Each adds its own secret colour to the other's mixture, and each arrives at the same shared secret. From this point on, everything in the conversation is encrypted.

The server does not stop to wait. In the same flight, now encrypted, it sends, among a few smaller matters, three things that count. First, its certificate chain: leaf and intermediate, for the client to check against its root store, as in the last Part. Second, a message called **CertificateVerify**: a signature, made with the private key that matches the certificate, over the entire conversation so far -- both hellos, both halves of the paint, everything. This is the step that defeats Mallory. It proves that the server holds the bank's private key, and it binds that proof to *this* exchange of paint, so that nobody in the middle can have substituted a mixture of their own. Third, a message called **Finished**: a short check computed over the whole handshake with the newly agreed key, so that if anybody had tampered with any earlier message, the two sides would discover that they disagreed about what had been said.

Third message, from the client: its own Finished. And in the very same packet, the client can send its request -- *GET me the account page* -- encrypted under the new key.

So: hello, with a pre-mixed share; hello back, with the other share, the certificate, the signature over everything, and a check; and the client's check, with the request riding alongside. One round trip for the encryption, on top of TCP's one round trip for the introductions. You may remember the arithmetic of the first evening, which charged a page in London two round trips before the first byte of the request could even be sent, and warned that the previous version of TLS added a third. This is why. TLS one-point-two needed two round trips for its handshake, because it agreed on the paints before mixing them. TLS one-point-three, published in August two thousand and eighteen, guesses the paint and does it in one.

It had taken a long road to get there. The protocol was born at Netscape, as SSL, in February nineteen ninety-five, written in a hurry for the first commercial web browser; its second version, the first released, had serious flaws, and a third, SSL version three, followed in November nineteen ninety-six. Netscape's chief scientist in those years, whom the trade calls the father of SSL, was Taher Elgamal -- who had earned his doctorate at Stanford under Martin Hellman, of Diffie and Hellman. The student of the man who invented the paint built the padlock. The standards body then took the protocol over, renamed it TLS, and issued versions one-point-oh in nineteen ninety-nine, one-point-one in two thousand and six, one-point-two in two thousand and eight, and one-point-three in two thousand and eighteen, each closing holes the last had left open; the two oldest versions of TLS were formally retired in two thousand and twenty-one, and every version of SSL long before. If a scanner of your company's systems ever complains that some old server still offers TLS one-point-oh, it is asking you to retire a protocol older than some of your colleagues.

Now four consequences of that design, each of which you will meet.

The first is called **forward secrecy**, and it is the reason the paint is mixed fresh for every single connection. In older versions of the protocol, there was another way to agree a key: the client simply made one up, locked it with the server's long-term public key -- the one in the certificate -- and sent it across. Only the server could unlock it. That was secure on the day. But consider somebody who records the encrypted conversation, and keeps the recording. If, years later, that somebody obtains the server's private key -- by theft, by a bug, or by a court order -- they can unlock the recorded key, and read every recorded conversation, all the way back. With fresh paint on every connection, that cannot happen. The server's long-term key is used only to *sign*, never to lock the key; the key itself never crosses the wire; and the secret colours are thrown away the moment the handshake is done. Steal the server's key tomorrow, and yesterday's conversations stay sealed. Google made fresh paint the default for its services in November two thousand and eleven. Two years later, after the revelations of that summer about the scale of government surveillance of the Internet, much of the industry followed in a hurry, and the IETF published a short document whose title was itself the policy: *Pervasive Monitoring Is an Attack*. And in April two thousand and fourteen came the argument that settled it: a bug called Heartbleed, in OpenSSL, the most widely used TLS software of all, which for two years had allowed anybody on the Internet to read small pieces of a server's memory -- pieces which might contain, among other things, its private key. Every site that had used the old method had to assume that every conversation it had ever recorded, or that anybody had recorded, might now be read. TLS one-point-three ended the matter by removing the old method entirely. Every full handshake now uses fresh paint. Forward secrecy is no longer a setting. It is the protocol.

The second consequence is a shortcut for returning visitors, and it comes with a warning label. A browser that has talked to a server recently may remember a secret from last time, and use it to send its request in its *very first message*, before the handshake has completed -- with no round trip for the encryption at all. It is called *zero round-trip resumption*, or in the trade's shorthand, *zero R-T-T*. The warning is written into the specification in plain words: data sent that way is not forward secret, and *there are no guarantees of non-replay between connections*. Which is to say: somebody in the middle could record that first message and send it again, and the server might act on it twice. You know from the first evening what to do with a message that might be delivered twice. Only an idempotent request -- one that does no harm if repeated -- may safely ride in that first message. Reading a page, yes. Making a payment, never. The servers that accept zero R-T-T data are normally configured to accept it only for requests of the harmless kind, and now you know why.

The third consequence is a story about the world's middleboxes, and it is one that you will hear judged at the end of the course. TLS one-point-three's designers found that a great many boxes in the middle of the Internet -- firewalls, inspection appliances, corporate proxies -- had been built to recognise the previous versions of TLS, and became confused or hostile when they saw anything new. Some servers, too, failed outright when a client announced a version they did not know. The specification describes the problem drily: *experience has shown that many servers do not properly implement version negotiation*. So TLS one-point-three goes about in disguise. In the field where its first message states its version, it claims to be TLS one-point-two -- and tucks its real version into a later part of the message that the old boxes do not read. And in the arrangement most software uses by default, it sends a few meaningless messages that make the whole handshake *look*, to a box that does not look too closely, like the resumption of an old TLS one-point-two conversation. The newest version of the Internet's most important security protocol travels the world wearing its predecessor's clothes, because the boxes in the middle would not let it through otherwise. There is a charming detail on the other side of the ledger. If a server that speaks TLS one-point-three is ever pushed down to an older version, it writes into the random bytes of its reply a small signal, which spells, in letters, *DOWNGRD* -- the word *downgrade* without its vowels -- so that a client that could have done better knows that somebody has interfered. Remember the disguise. It is evidence in a case we shall try in the last evening.

The fourth consequence is about what TLS does *not* hide, which the boy should know as clearly as what it does. Everything inside the conversation is sealed. But the envelope is not. The addresses and ports on every packet, from last week and the week before, are in the clear, as they must be, or the packets could not be delivered. The sizes of the messages and their timing are visible. The question your laptop asked the directory a moment before is visible, unless the directory question was itself encrypted, as in Part Three. And for most of TLS's history, one more thing was visible, right at the top of the ClientHello: the *name* of the site you wanted. It has to be there, because a single address may serve thousands of sites -- think of a content delivery network -- and the server must know which certificate to present before any key has been agreed. That field is called the **server name indication**, SNI, and it means that anybody watching your traffic can see the names of the sites you visit, even though they cannot see what you do there. In March of this year, after years of work, a standard was published to seal that too: **Encrypted Client Hello**, whose specification calls the name in the clear *perhaps the most sensitive information left unencrypted in TLS one-point-three*. The key needed to seal the hello is published -- where else? -- in the directory, in the HTTPS record of Part Two; so Encrypted Client Hello is of real use only alongside encrypted DNS. Chrome and Firefox already support it, and a growing share of sites. The envelope, slowly, is being sealed as well.

One ritual belongs here, because it guards the moment before TLS begins. When you type a site's name without saying *https*, your browser may make its very first request the old, unencrypted way, and be told by the site to come back securely. That first, unencrypted moment is exactly where a meddler in a coffee shop would stand, to keep you on the unencrypted road. So sites send, with their secure pages, an instruction called *Strict Transport Security*, which tells your browser: *for the next year, say, never speak to me except over TLS* -- and the browsers keep a built-in list of sites that have asked for this permanently, so that even the very first visit is secure. When a security review asks you to add that header, with a long lifetime, this is the door it is closing.

Two last matters, both from inside your own buildings.

When your browser visits the bank, only the bank proves who it is; you prove who you are later, with a password, inside the encrypted conversation. But between services, both sides can present certificates, and each can check the other's. That is called **mutual TLS**, and it is what your security team means when it says that every call between services must be *mTLS*. In the systems the trade calls service meshes, every running service is issued its own certificate automatically, with a name that says not *www dot something dot com* but *this is the payments service, in the production namespace, of this cluster* -- written in a standard form whose name you will see in configuration files, a URL-shaped identifier beginning with the word *spiffe*, which stands, with some effort, for *Secure Production Identity Framework For Everyone*. Every conversation inside the building checks papers in both directions. Where exactly the encryption begins and ends, as a request passes through the load balancers and proxies of a large system, is a question for the machine room, next week.

And the last matter is the future, which has arrived early. All the paint I have described -- every variety of Diffie-Hellman, every elliptic curve -- rests on the belief that unmixing is hard. A sufficiently large quantum computer, if one is ever built, would make it easy. Nobody has built one. But somebody who records encrypted traffic today could keep it until somebody does -- the trade calls the strategy *harvest now, decrypt later* -- and so the browsers have not waited. Since two thousand and twenty-four, Chrome has mixed a second paint into its handshakes alongside the classical one: a new kind, which works rather differently but does the same job, called ML-KEM -- said *M-L-kem* -- standardised by the American government in August two thousand and twenty-four, and believed to resist quantum computers. Both paints are mixed; the key depends on both; and an eavesdropper must unmix both to read anything. By the end of April this year, Cloudflare, one of the largest networks in the world, reported that more than two-thirds of the human traffic reaching it was protected that way. There is a fair chance that the connection which delivered this very episode to you was sealed with paint that no quantum computer could unmix.

So: TLS one-point-three does its work in one round trip. The client says hello, with a pre-mixed key share and the name it wants. The server answers with its own share, so that both now hold the key; then, encrypted, its certificate chain, a signature over the whole handshake to prove it holds the private key, and a check; and the client replies with its own check, and its request. Fresh paint on every connection gives forward secrecy: steal the key tomorrow, and yesterday stays sealed. Zero R-T-T saves a round trip for returning visitors, but may be replayed, so only idempotent requests may use it. The new protocol wears the old one's clothes to get past the boxes in the middle. TLS seals the letter, not the envelope: addresses, sizes, and -- until Encrypted Client Hello -- the name remain visible. Inside your buildings, mutual TLS checks papers both ways. And the paint itself is already being made ready for the quantum age.

## What the Boy Should Have Been Told

So, sir: the fourth lecture, as the boy should have heard it, and as I hope you will carry it out of the house with you.

The Internet's directory began as a single file, kept in California and copied to every machine, whose cost grew as the square of the network; and it was replaced by a tree. The root knows the top-level domains. Each top-level domain knows the names beneath it. Each owner knows its own names. Each level hands responsibility to the next by delegation, and the servers that answer for each zone are its authoritative servers. At the very top sit the root servers: thirteen names, and more than two thousand machines, by anycast. And the top of the tree is, in the end, an agreement among a few organisations about whose list to copy -- as Jon Postel demonstrated, for one remarkable week in nineteen ninety-eight.

When you ask a question of that tree, your laptop's stub resolver asks a recursive resolver, and the resolver walks the tree from the root by referrals -- the root points to *com*, *com* points to *example dot com*, and *example dot com* answers. Every answer carries a time to live, and is remembered by everyone who received it for exactly that long. That cache is why the directory is fast. It is also why it is slow to change. Nothing in DNS propagates: old answers simply expire, on the schedule you gave them. Hence your rituals. You lower the time to live a day before a migration, so that the world forgets the old address quickly when the day comes. You cannot put a CNAME at the apex of your zone, because the apex must carry other records. You are told a name does not exist for an hour after creating it, because somebody asked too soon and the *no* was cached. Your Java service once never heard of a migration, because its cache never expired. Your Kubernetes pods ask eight questions where one would do, unless you give the name its final dot. And you open TCP port fifty-three beside UDP, because large answers travel by TCP.

For most of its life, the directory believed any answer that looked like a reply. In two thousand and eight Dan Kaminsky showed how dangerous that was, told the vendors privately, and the trade patched the world in a single day with more randomness: a mitigation, not a cure. The cure is DNSSEC. Every zone signs its records. Every parent vouches for its children's keys. And the root's key-signing key is vouched for by nobody, but is built into every validating resolver in the world as its trust anchor -- which is why it lives in a safe, in Culpeper or El Segundo, why it is used only in ceremonies before witnesses, and why replacing it, as is being done on the eleventh of October, takes years of preparation. DNSSEC proves but does not conceal; encrypted DNS conceals. And when something inexplicable happens, ask the directory first. It was very likely DNS.

Then trust. TLS makes three promises: confidentiality, integrity, and authentication. A symmetric cipher gives the first two quickly, once both ends share a key. And the key is agreed by the most beautiful idea in this course, the one I hope you could now draw on a napkin, for this is the third time you have heard it. Alice and Bob agree in public on a common paint. Each keeps a secret colour. Each mixes their secret with the common paint, and sends the mixture across. Each adds their own secret to the other's mixture -- and both arrive at the same colour, while Eve, who saw every pot that crossed the playground, saw only mixtures, and cannot unmix them. That is Diffie and Hellman's key exchange, of nineteen seventy-six, and every secure connection you make begins with it.

But the paint defeats only the listener. Mallory, who stands in the doorway, can mix one pot with Alice and another with Bob, unless each side proves who it is -- by a digital signature, made with a private key, whose public half the other side knows to belong to it. And that knowledge comes from a certificate: names and key; issuer, dates, and signature. The certificate is signed by a certificate authority; the authority's key is vouched for by an intermediate or a root; and the roots are built into your device's store, believed by fiat. Any authority can sign for any name, so the system is only as strong as its weakest member, as DigiNotar showed in two thousand and eleven; Certificate Transparency now makes every certificate public, so that no forgery can be made in secret. Let's Encrypt made certificates free and automatic, and lifetimes are falling to weeks, to force the automation that keeps O-two's bus stops lit. Your rituals here were these. *Unable to get local issuer certificate*: the server forgot to send its intermediate, and your browser was kind enough to hide it. *Hostname mismatch*: the certificate is genuine, but not for this name. The *insecure* flag: a sealed letter to whoever opens the door. And the alarm on every certificate within a few weeks of its end.

TLS one-point-three does all of this in one round trip: a hello with a pre-mixed key share and the name you want; a hello back, with the other share, and then, encrypted, the certificate chain, a signature over the whole handshake, and a check; and your own check, with the request riding alongside. Fresh paint on every connection gives forward secrecy, so that a key stolen tomorrow cannot unlock yesterday. A returning visitor may skip even that round trip, but only for requests that are harmless to repeat. The newest protocol wears the old one's clothes to get past the boxes in the middle of the Internet, which is evidence we shall weigh next week. TLS seals the letter, not the envelope: the addresses, the sizes, and until lately the name, remain visible. Inside your own buildings, mutual TLS checks papers in both directions. And the paint is already being mixed against the quantum computers of the future.

And here, for the third time, is the evening's thesis, which I would have you keep if you keep nothing else. The Internet's names and the Internet's trust are two trees of delegated authority, built by different people for different purposes, which turn out to have the same shape. Each level vouches for the level beneath it. At the top of each sits a handful of keys -- the root key in its safe, the root certificates in your browser's store -- that are not checked against anything, but simply believed. Whoever controls the top of either tree controls, in principle, everything beneath it. The ceremony in Culpeper, with its safes within safes and its three of seven, is what it looks like when people take that fact seriously; and Certificate Transparency is what it looks like when they decide that even the people at the top must work in public.

For reading: Michael Driscoll's *The Illustrated TLS one-point-three Connection*, which is online, free, and explains every single byte of a real TLS one-point-three handshake, in the order it is sent, with a patience I can only admire. Julia Evans's writing on DNS, whose clarity puts most textbooks to shame. And, for the history of the paint and of the secret British mathematicians who mixed it first, Simon Singh's *The Code Book*, which is as good a story as anything in this course, and makes a fine companion for a long run.
