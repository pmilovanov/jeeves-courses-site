Episode One — Lo and Behold

Best Effort — A Remedial Course in Networking

Two Letters at Half Past Ten

Good day, sir.

On the evening of Wednesday the twenty-ninth of October, nineteen sixty-nine, in a room on the third floor of Boelter Hall at the University of California, Los Angeles, a student programmer named Charley Kline sat down at a terminal to try something that had never been done. Beside him, humming in a grey cabinet about the size of a refrigerator, stood a machine called an Interface Message Processor — an IMP, as everybody called it: a Honeywell minicomputer, ruggedised and programmed by a firm of engineers in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and flown out to the university at the end of August, so that it would arrive on the day promised. Some three hundred and fifty miles to the north, at the Stanford Research Institute in Menlo Park, stood a second IMP, connected to the first by a leased telephone line, and beside it sat a second programmer, Bill Duvall.

The plan was modest. Kline would log in to the computer at Stanford from the terminal in Los Angeles. To log in, he had to type the word LOGIN. The two men were on the telephone to each other while he did it — the first message of the new network supervised, with a certain irony, over the old one.

Kline typed an L. "Do you see the L?" "Yes, we see the L."

Kline typed an O. "Do you see the O?" "Yes, we see the O."

Kline typed a G, and the system at Stanford crashed.

It was not the network that failed, I should say in fairness to the network. It was the computer at the far end, and it failed by trying to be helpful. The Stanford machine had been taught to save its users trouble: on seeing L, O, G, it would complete the word LOGIN on their behalf and send the rest of it back. The code that handled that little courtesy overran a buffer, and down it went. The first crash in the history of the Internet was, near enough, caused by autocomplete.

So the first message ever sent across the network that would become the Internet was the two letters L, O. Leonard Kleinrock, whose laboratory it was, has said ever since that one could not have asked for a more succinct, more powerful, more prophetic message: lo, as in lo and behold. Duvall enlarged his buffer, and about an hour later they tried again, and the login went through. In the laboratory's log for that night, against the time twenty-two thirty and initialled by Kline, is a line that has since become one of the most photographed entries in any notebook in the history of computing: talked to SRI host to host.

I begin there, sir, for three reasons.

The first is that it is a very good story and we have an hour and a half to fill.

The second is that the story is, in miniature, the whole of networking. A message is sent. Part of it arrives. The far end falls over. The near end tries again until it works. Every protocol you will hear about in this course — every handshake, every acknowledgement, every timeout, every retry with its little dose of random jitter — is an elaboration of what those two men did on the telephone that night: do you see the L? Yes, we see the L. The network has been dropping things ever since, and the ends have been asking, patiently, whether the letter arrived.

The third reason is the one that matters. You have described yourself to me, sir, in the third person, as a boy who studied computer science, never properly took a networking course, and cargo-culted his way to staff engineer. I accept the commission with pleasure, though I am not persuaded the boy was ever at fault: the course was optional, the network never complained, and a good many of the people who did take it have forgotten it.

But let us take the phrase seriously, because it is apt, and the man who coined it would have approved. In nineteen seventy-four, Richard Feynman gave the commencement address at the California Institute of Technology, and called it Cargo Cult Science. He described islanders in the South Seas who had seen, during the war, aeroplanes land full of wonderful goods. After the war, he said, they wanted the aeroplanes to keep coming, so they built runways, lit fires along the sides of them, and built a little wooden hut for a man to sit in, with two pieces of wood on his head like headphones and bars of bamboo sticking out like antennas — and they waited. The form was perfect. Everything looked exactly as it had looked before. But no aeroplanes landed.

The anthropologists, I am obliged to add, have been kinder to the islanders than Feynman was; the movements he caricatured were about a good deal more than aeroplanes, including the dignity of people under colonial rule, and they were not nearly so foolish as the parable needs them to be. But the parable is about us, not them, and it is about something real. An engineer can perform the right ritual for years — setting an option because a colleague set it, lowering a number before a migration because a runbook said so, adding a random delay to a retry, choosing a size for a network block from a dropdown — and get the right result, without ever knowing what the ritual does.

Your case, sir, if I may say so, is the reverse of Feynman's. Your aeroplanes land. They land thousands of times a second, every day, and have for your entire career. What you lack is not the aeroplanes but the theory of flight. That is a far more comfortable deficiency, and a far more easily remedied one; and remedied is the word I prefer for the undertaking. You called the course remedial. I take the word in its oldest sense, from the Latin for a cure: not a punishment for the inattentive, but a remedy for a gap that nobody chose.

So: the rituals. I shall collect them as we go, and each time one of them turns up I shall stop and show you the mechanism it operates, at the moment the mechanism becomes visible. By the end of the fifth evening I should like there to be nothing you do with a network that you do not understand.

You also put to me a question, and it deserves a straight answer at the start rather than at the end. You asked: if this is all you ever learn about networking, what should it be?

It should be this. The Internet's core promises nothing but its best effort. Every guarantee you rely on — that your bytes arrive, that they arrive in order, intact, unread by strangers, from whom they claim to come, and quickly — is manufactured at the edges, by the machines at either end, which trust nothing in between. Learn where each promise is manufactured, and you will know where each one breaks.

That is the whole course in a paragraph, and the remaining four evenings are an inventory of the promises. It divides naturally into five questions, which I shall give you now so that you can hang everything that follows upon them.

What is a network? That is tonight: the packet, the layers, the promise of nothing, and the physics of distance.

Where does a packet go, and how does it find its way? The second evening: addresses and routes, from the wire in your hall to the treaties between the networks of nations.

Did it arrive? The third evening: the transport, and the elaborate good manners by which two machines hold a reliable conversation through a post that loses letters.

Who is speaking, and who is listening? The fourth evening: the directory that turns names into addresses, and the system of credentials and ciphers that lets a stranger prove an identity and keep a secret in public.

And why is it all so slow — and what do the great machine rooms do about it? The fifth evening: the web's long war on the round trip, the front doors and fabrics of the data centre, the extraordinary networks that join the accelerators of your own trade, and then the whole journey of a single page, walked once more at full resolution, with every promise and every point of failure in view.

Tonight's route, then. We begin with a choice made in the early nineteen-sixties between two ways of building a network — the telephone's way and the letter's — and the two men, on two sides of the Atlantic, who chose the letter. Then the building of the first network, and the much harder business of joining networks to one another. Then the arrangement of envelopes within envelopes on which everything rests. Then the promise of nothing, and why a network that promises nothing turned out to be the only kind that could grow to the size of the world. Then the speed of light, which is the one constraint in your architecture that no vendor can improve. And then, to close, a first look at the journey your browser makes every time you press the return key — a map of the season.

Ninety minutes, sir. We begin a few years before that night in Los Angeles, with a telephone.

Part One — The Telephone and the Letter

In nineteen sixty, if you wished to send information from one place to another faster than a train could carry it, there was one great machine for the purpose, and it belonged, in the United States, almost entirely to one company. The American Telephone and Telegraph Company — A-T-and-T, the Bell System, Ma Bell to a nation that had a curiously affectionate relationship with its monopolist — ran what was, by most reasonable measures, the largest and most complex machine on Earth. And it worked on a principle that is worth understanding exactly, because the Internet was built as a deliberate rejection of it.

The principle is called circuit switching. When you lifted the receiver and dialled, the exchanges between you and the person you were calling connected, one after another, a continuous path from your handset to theirs. In the early days it was literally a path of copper, made by operators pushing plugs into sockets and later by great clattering banks of electromechanical switches; by the nineteen-sixties it was more often a slot reserved on trunk lines that carried many calls at once. But either way, the essential arrangement was the same. For as long as the call lasted, a fixed portion of the network's capacity, all the way from one end to the other, was yours. Nobody else could use it. You could talk, or you could fall silent for a full minute while you looked for a pen, and the capacity sat there, reserved and paid for, carrying nothing.

By the early nineteen-sixties the reservation had become beautifully precise. In nineteen sixty-two the Bell System began installing a digital trunk line, called T-one, which carried twenty-four telephone calls at once over a pair of wires by taking turns. Every hundred and twenty-five microseconds the line sent one small slice of each of the twenty-four calls in strict rotation — eight bits from call one, eight from call two, and so on round to call twenty-four, and then back to call one. Each call owned its slot in the rotation. If you were talking, your slot carried your voice. If you had put the receiver down on the table to fetch a pen, your slot went out anyway, every hundred and twenty-five microseconds, carrying silence, and nobody else could use it. That arrangement — a fixed slot in a fixed rotation for each conversation — is called time-division multiplexing, and it is the circuit's way of sharing a wire: scrupulously fair, perfectly predictable, and blind to whether anybody is actually speaking.

For a telephone call, this is close to ideal. Speech is a steady stream: so many syllables a second, more or less continuously, in both directions. Once the circuit is made, there is no waiting, no congestion, no doubt about the order in which things arrive. Your words reach the other end a fixed fraction of a second after you speak them, every time. The only price is the dialling — the time to set the circuit up — and the silence, which is wasted, but which in a conversation between two people is not very much of the time.

Now consider what happens when the conversation is between two computers, or between a person at a terminal and a computer far away.

That conversation does not flow; it lurches. You type a command: a few bytes. Then you wait while the computer thinks. Then a screenful of output comes back, all at once. Then you read it, for perhaps a minute, during which nothing whatever is sent. Then you type again. A file transfer is the opposite extreme: a torrent for a few seconds and then nothing at all. Engineers call such traffic bursty, and the defining fact about bursty traffic is that it is idle most of the time and frantic for the rest. A dedicated circuit for a terminal session is like hiring a coach and four, with driver, to stand outside your door all day, in case you should wish to send a postcard every quarter of an hour. The measurements of the period suggested that a circuit carrying interactive computer traffic was idle the overwhelming majority of the time. The telephone network was, for computers, an exquisitely engineered way of wasting money.

The alternative came from two men, working on opposite sides of the Atlantic, for entirely different reasons, neither knowing of the other.

The first was Paul Baran, an engineer at the RAND Corporation in Santa Monica, the think tank that advised the American air force. Baran's problem, at the end of the nineteen-fifties, was the most sombre in engineering: how to build a communications network that could survive a nuclear attack well enough for the country's leaders to go on communicating afterwards. He looked at the telephone network and saw a hierarchy — local exchanges feeding regional exchanges feeding a small number of enormous switching centres — and he saw that a hierarchy has a throat. Destroy a handful of the great centres and the whole thing falls apart.

Baran drew three pictures, which became famous. The first was a centralized network: a star, every node connected to one hub. Kill the hub and everything dies. The second was decentralized: a star of stars, several hubs each with their own spokes, the hubs joined to one another. Better, but kill the hubs and the spokes are orphaned. The third he called distributed: no hubs at all, just a mesh, like a fishnet, each node joined to a few of its neighbours and nothing more. And his simulations showed something remarkable. A distributed network did not need many links per node to be astonishingly robust. With each node joined to only three or four neighbours, the network could lose a large fraction of its nodes and links and still find a path between most of the survivors.

But a fishnet poses a problem the telephone never had to solve. In a hierarchy, the route from any point to any other is obvious and fixed: up to the hub, across, and down. In a mesh there are a vast number of possible routes, and after an attack you do not know which of them still exist. You cannot set up a circuit along a fixed path, because you do not know which paths survive. So Baran made the second and more radical move. Do not set up a path at all. Instead, cut every message into small blocks of standard size; write the destination address on every block; and let each node in the mesh, on receiving a block, pass it on to whichever neighbour seems at that moment to offer the best way toward its destination — quickly, without holding on to it, like a hot potato. Each block finds its own way. Blocks of the same message might take different routes. If a node is destroyed, the blocks simply flow around the hole.

Baran called the scheme, with the descriptive relentlessness of the period, distributed adaptive message block switching, and published it in nineteen sixty-four as a series of eleven memoranda under the title On Distributed Communications. Then he took it to the people who would have to build it, which in the America of the nineteen-sixties meant the telephone company.

The telephone company was not interested. It was, if anything, offended. As Baran remembered it, one of its senior men, Jack Osterman, summed up the company's position after a long discussion in two clauses: first, it couldn't possibly work, and if it did, damned if they were going to allow the creation of a competitor to themselves. One sees their point. They had spent the better part of a century perfecting the circuit, and here was a young man from a think tank proposing that the future belonged to a scheme in which nothing was reserved, nothing was guaranteed, and the switches did not even remember what they had switched. The air force did come round to the idea, eventually; but the agency that would have built the network was one in which Baran had little confidence, and rather than see the thing done badly he advised that it not be done at all. On Distributed Communications went onto the shelf. The telephone company's view of packets did not soften for years. Bob Metcalfe — whom we shall meet next week as the inventor of Ethernet — liked to tell how, at the first great public demonstration of the ARPANET in Washington in nineteen seventy-two, he was showing a party of A-T-and-T executives the new network when the system crashed in front of them, and he looked up to see them smiling.

The second man was Donald Davies, at the National Physical Laboratory in Teddington, on the outskirts of London — the British government's laboratory for the measurement of things. Davies came to the same idea in nineteen sixty-five, entirely independently, and from the opposite direction. He was not thinking about war. He was thinking about money, and about the new practice of time-sharing, in which many people at many terminals shared one large computer. He saw, as clearly as anybody ever has, that the traffic between a terminal and a computer was bursty, and that the telephone's circuits were therefore the wrong tool for it; and he proposed a national data network for Britain that would carry information in small, separately addressed units, interleaved on shared lines.

He needed a name for the units, and he chose, after consulting a linguist at the laboratory, the word packet: a small package. He wanted a word that could be translated into other languages without compromise, and packet can. Baran's "message blocks" had the misfortune to sound exactly like what they were. Davies's word won, and it is the word you have used every working day of your life.

Davies and his colleagues built a working network at the laboratory, and they talked about their ideas, which is how the story reaches America. In October nineteen sixty-seven, at a computing symposium in Gatlinburg, Tennessee, one of Davies's team, Roger Scantlebury, presented a paper on the British design. In the audience was Larry Roberts, the man then planning a network for the American defence research agency, ARPA. Roberts learned from Scantlebury of Davies's packets — and of Baran's earlier work, which he had not properly read. He went home, read it, adopted the packet and the word, and raised the speed of the lines he had planned for his network from two point four kilobits a second to fifty. The British group gave the field one more word at about the same time. A memorandum of theirs from the spring of nineteen sixty-seven is credited with the first use of protocol in its networking sense: the agreed rules by which two machines conduct an exchange. The word comes from diplomacy, where it means the etiquette by which two embassies conduct theirs, and you will find, as the course goes on, that the borrowing was more exact than anybody could have known.

Now let me take the idea apart into its three pieces, because each is a term of the trade, and each will be with us to the end of the course.

The first is packet switching itself: the message is cut into packets, and each packet carries the full address of its destination, so that it can be handled independently of every other. A telephone call is a reservation; a packet is a letter. The letter carries its own address, and the post office handles it on its own merits, without needing to know about any other letter you have sent.

The second is store-and-forward. A packet switch — what we should now call a router — receives a packet in full, stores it in its memory, examines the address, decides which of its outgoing links leads best toward the destination, and sends the packet out along that link. Then it forgets about it. Each switch takes one step of the journey and no more. No switch knows the whole route; each knows only which way to send things next. The sorting office in Toronto does not know how a letter will travel once it reaches Vancouver; it knows only that letters for Vancouver go in the Vancouver bag.

The third is statistical multiplexing, and it is where the money is. In a circuit-switched network, a link's capacity is divided into fixed slices and each slice is reserved for one conversation, used or not. In a packet-switched network, a link's capacity is not divided at all. It is shared among all the conversations that cross it, packet by packet, first come first served. A link carries whatever packets happen to be waiting, from whoever sent them. And the bet — it is quite literally a statistical bet — is that not everybody will want the link at the same moment. Suppose a link could carry a hundred steady streams at once. If each of your users is bursty, and active only a tenth of the time, that same link can serve something like a thousand of them, because on average only a hundred will be talking at any moment. Ten times the customers on the same wire. That is the economic engine of the Internet, and it is why your provider can sell you a gigabit connection for the price of a few dinners: it is betting, sensibly, that you and your neighbours will not all use your gigabits at once.

But on average is carrying a great deal of weight in that sentence, and here is the price of the bargain. Sometimes they do all talk at once. Sometimes more packets arrive at a router for a particular outgoing link than the link can send. What happens to the surplus? It waits. Every router keeps a queue — a buffer of memory — for each of its outgoing links, and packets that cannot be sent immediately join the end of it. If the burst passes, the queue drains, and the only cost was a little delay. If the burst does not pass, the queue fills; and when the queue is full, the next packet to arrive has nowhere to go, and the router throws it away. That is called dropping the packet, and I want to be clear that it is not a malfunction. It is the design's safety valve. A packet-switched network under load must either delay packets or discard them; there is no third option, and the art of the whole field, from that day to this, has been in choosing well between the two.

So consider what the letter has given up, compared with the telephone. Delay is no longer fixed: it depends on how long the queues are, which depends on everybody else's traffic. Delivery is no longer certain: a packet that meets a full queue is gone. And order is no longer guaranteed: because each packet is routed independently, two packets of the same message may take different paths, and the second may arrive first. In exchange, the network is efficient — links are shared rather than reserved — and robust, since no switch holds anything it would be a disaster to lose, and packets can flow around damage.

Let me make the bargain concrete with an example from nearer home. Suppose Mrs Milovanov wished to send a large canvas to a gallery in Vancouver. The telephone's method would be to reserve a private road from your front door to the gallery's for the duration of the shipment: very reliable, very fast once begun, and ruinously expensive, because nobody else may use the road while the painting is on it. The packet's method would be to cut the canvas into numbered strips, put each strip in its own envelope with the gallery's address on it, and drop them all in the post. The strips travel separately, by whatever van or train happens to be going that way. Some arrive tomorrow and some next week. Strip seven goes astray in a sorting office in Winnipeg. The gallery must lay the strips out in order of their numbers, notice that seven is missing, and write back to ask for another copy of it.

As a method of shipping a painting, I should not care to be the one who proposes it to Mrs Milovanov. As a method of shipping data it has conquered the world, for a reason that does not apply to paint: data can be copied perfectly, and reassembled without a seam. The gallery can ask for strip seven again, and the second strip seven is not a forgery or a repair but the very same strip, indistinguishable from the first. Everything we shall discuss over the next five evenings follows from that bargain — the efficiency and the robustness gained, and the delay, loss, and disorder accepted in exchange — and from the endless ingenuity spent, ever since, on living with its price.

Part Two — A Network of Networks

Let us go to Washington, to the Pentagon, in nineteen sixty-six, and into the office of a man named Bob Taylor.

Taylor ran the part of ARPA — the Advanced Research Projects Agency, the American defence department's agency for long-range research — that paid for computing. ARPA funded computer science at a dozen universities and research institutes, and in consequence there sat in a room beside Taylor's office three computer terminals. One was connected to a computer at MIT, one to a computer at the University of California at Berkeley, and one to a computer at a company in Santa Monica. Each terminal spoke to exactly one machine. Each machine had its own way of logging in, its own commands, its own everything. To move from one to another, Taylor had to get up and change chairs.

He described the moment afterwards, and his words deserve to be heard as he said them: I said, oh, man, it's obvious what to do: If you have these three terminals, there ought to be one terminal that goes anywhere you want to go where you have interactive computing. That idea is the ARPAnet. Behind the first thought stood a second, and it was the one that would sell the scheme: that the computers ARPA was paying for at a dozen sites ought to be able to share their programs, their data, and their people, rather than each site buying and building everything for itself. He walked down the corridor to the director of ARPA, Charles Herzfeld, and put the idea to him. By Taylor's account, the meeting took about twenty minutes, and he came out with a million dollars to begin.

I pause here, because there is a story about the origin of the Internet that you will certainly have heard, and it is not quite true. The story is that the Internet was built to survive a nuclear war. You can see where it comes from: Paul Baran's work at RAND really was about surviving an attack, and his ideas really did shape the network's design. But the motive for building the ARPANET was the one I have just described: sharing expensive computers among researchers. Taylor said so; so did Herzfeld, who wrote, with a certain weariness, that the ARPANET was not started to create a command and control system that would survive a nuclear attack, as many now claimed. The network inherited Baran's robustness; it did not inherit his mission. When somebody tells you over dinner that the Internet was designed to survive the bomb, you may say, with perfect courtesy, that it was designed to spare a man in the Pentagon from changing chairs.

Taylor hired Larry Roberts to build the thing — the same Larry Roberts who, at Gatlinburg in nineteen sixty-seven, heard about packets from the British. In nineteen sixty-eight ARPA asked for bids to build the packet switches, and the contract went not to a great computer company but to a small firm of consulting engineers in Cambridge, Massachusetts, called Bolt Beranek and Newman — BBN — where a team under Frank Heart set about turning a ruggedised Honeywell minicomputer into the first Interface Message Processor. The award did not pass unnoticed in Massachusetts. Senator Edward Kennedy sent the firm a telegram of congratulation on winning the contract for the Interfaith Message Processor, and thanked it for its ecumenical efforts; it is not recorded that anybody corrected him. One of Heart's programmers, Will Crowther, a caver in his spare time, went on a few years later to write the first of all computer adventure games, set in a cave modelled on one he had explored — so the team that built the Internet's first switches also, by a side door, founded a genre. The idea of putting a separate small computer at each site, to handle the network so that the big host computers need not, came from another computing pioneer, Wesley Clark, and it was a stroke of layering before anybody called it that: the hosts would talk to their IMPs, and the IMPs would talk to each other, and the messy business of moving packets would live in machines dedicated to nothing else.

The first IMP was delivered to UCLA at the end of the summer of nineteen sixty-nine. The second went to the Stanford Research Institute that autumn, and on the night of the twenty-ninth of October, as you know, the two of them exchanged two letters and a crash. By the end of the year there were four sites — UCLA, Stanford Research Institute, the University of California at Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah — and the network grew steadily from there: fifteen sites by nineteen seventy-one, across the continent, and across the Atlantic by nineteen seventy-three, to Norway and then to University College London. The Norwegian site, I cannot resist adding, was a seismic array whose purpose was to detect Soviet nuclear tests — so the first foreign node of the network that was never built to survive a nuclear war existed to listen for nuclear weapons. And a study made that same year found that three-quarters of all the traffic on the ARPANET was electronic mail. A network built so that researchers could share expensive computers had been taken over, within four years, by people writing one another letters. The letter, you will notice, keeps winning.

Now let me tell you about the documents, because you have spent your career reading them without perhaps knowing where they came from. In the spring of nineteen sixty-nine, before any IMP had been delivered, the graduate students at the first sites began meeting to work out how the host computers would actually talk to one another. Nobody was in charge; nobody had told them how to proceed; and they were, they felt, rather junior to be writing the rules for something so grand. So one of them, a UCLA student named Steve Crocker, wrote up the group's notes in a memorandum whose title was chosen to sound as unassuming as possible. As he put it many years later, the ground rules were that anyone could say anything and that nothing was official, and to emphasize the point, I labeled the notes 'Request for Comments.' It was called Host Software, it was dated the seventh of April, nineteen sixty-nine, and it was number one. Hafner and Lyon, the network's best historians, record that he wrote it late at night in the bathroom of the house he shared, so as not to wake his friends.

That series has never stopped. Every protocol in this course — IP, TCP, DNS, HTTP, TLS — is defined in a Request for Comments, an RFC, and the series has now run past nine thousand documents. A young man in the same UCLA group, named Jon Postel, took on the job of editing them, and kept it for nearly thirty years; you will hear his name again. And the tone Crocker set, of deference and provisionality, is the tone the Internet's standards have kept ever since: a specification is not a decree, it is a request for comments that happens to have been accepted.

Inside the ARPANET, the network took responsibility for getting packets through. The IMPs checked every packet as it crossed each line, retransmitted on errors, and delivered messages to the host in order. The hosts spoke a protocol known as NCP on top of a network they could trust. In that respect the ARPANET was still a little like the telephone: a clever network, carrying not-very-clever conversations.

And then the problem changed shape.

The ARPANET was not the only packet network for long. In Hawaii, Norman Abramson's group at the university had built ALOHAnet, which sent packets by radio between the islands — the first wireless packet network, and a design we shall meet again next week, because Ethernet was born from it. ARPA itself funded a packet radio network in the San Francisco Bay Area, for vehicles, and a packet satellite network across the Atlantic. In Britain there was Davies's network at the National Physical Laboratory. And in France, in the early nineteen-seventies, there was a network called CYCLADES, built by a team under an engineer named Louis Pouzin.

Each of these networks was different. Different speeds, different sizes of packet, different ideas of addressing, different reliability: a radio network loses packets constantly, a satellite link has long delays, a wired network is comparatively serene. A host on the ARPANET could not talk to a host on the packet radio network, any more than a telephone in Toronto could, in those days, ring a telegraph office in Lyon. And the question became: how do you join networks to one another, when every one of them is built differently, run by a different organisation, and none of them can be redesigned to suit the others?

The decisive idea came from France. Pouzin's CYCLADES was built on a principle quite unlike the ARPANET's. The network itself, in CYCLADES, did not promise to deliver packets reliably or in order. It carried each packet independently, did its best, and left everything else — noticing losses, asking again, putting things in order — to the computers at the two ends. A packet handled in this way, independently and without any promise, came to be called a datagram — a blend of data and telegram, coined not by Pouzin but by a Norwegian engineer named Halvor Bothner-By: a self-contained message, carrying its own address, that might or might not get through. The network became simpler, because it no longer had to be clever; and the hosts became more responsible, because they now had to be.

CYCLADES itself did not survive. The French state's telephone administration preferred a network of its own, built on the telephone company's model of reliable connections, and Pouzin's project lost its funding before the end of the decade. But the idea crossed the Atlantic and took root in the place where it mattered.

In nineteen seventy-three two American engineers set themselves the task of interconnecting networks. Bob Kahn had worked on the IMPs at BBN and gone on to ARPA, where he was running the packet radio work. Vint Cerf was at Stanford, and had been one of the graduate students in the UCLA group in nineteen sixty-nine. In May nineteen seventy-four they published their answer in a paper called A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication, and it is, as nearly as any single document can be, the founding charter of the Internet.

Their design had three essential features, and you will recognise all three from what I have told you.

First, every network would be left exactly as it was. Nobody would be asked to rebuild their network to suit anybody else's. Instead, between any two networks there would stand a gateway: a machine connected to both, whose job was to take packets off one network and put them onto the other. What we now call a router is the descendant of the gateway, and the old name survives in a phrase you see every time you configure a machine: the default gateway.

Second, every host on every network would speak one common protocol, layered over whatever its local network provided. The hosts would agree on a common format for packets, carrying a common form of address, so that a gateway could look at any packet and know where it was going, whatever network it had come from.

Third — and this is Pouzin's principle, adopted whole — the gateways would do as little as possible. They would forward packets, best effort, and nothing more. Responsibility for reliability, for ordering, for retransmission, would lie with the hosts at the two ends. A gateway joining a radio network to a satellite network need not understand the conversation it was carrying; it need only pass packets along.

A network of networks: in their documents it was an internetwork, which is a mouthful, and within a few months the specification for their protocol, written up as an RFC in December nineteen seventy-four, was using the shorter word internet. That is where the word comes from. The Internet with a capital letter is the one great internetwork that grew out of that design, and its name is, quite literally, a description of its architecture.

Cerf and Kahn called their protocol the Transmission Control Program — TCP — and at first it did everything: the addressing, the forwarding, and the reliability all in one. Over the next few years the design was tested and argued over in a series of meetings, and one argument in particular reshaped it.

The argument concerned the human voice. An engineer named Danny Cohen was working on sending speech over packet networks, and he pointed out that for voice, TCP's reliability was not merely unnecessary but harmful. If a packet carrying a fraction of a second of speech is lost, the worst thing you can do is stop everything and wait for it to be sent again: by the time it arrives the conversation has moved on, and the late syllable is worse than useless. Voice wants timeliness, not perfection. A little loss is fine; a delay is fatal. Others had been making related arguments about layering for some time. Jon Postel wrote a note in August nineteen seventy-seven that opened, with a directness the Internet's documents have rarely matched since: We are screwing up in our design of internet protocols by violating the principle of layering. TCP, he went on, was being asked to do two jobs at once — to carry a conversation between two hosts, and to package and route packets across the internetwork — and the two jobs should be separated.

So, in nineteen seventy-eight, the protocol was split in two. The part that every gateway must understand — addressing, and the forwarding of individual datagrams, best effort — became the Internet Protocol, IP. The part that only the hosts need — reliable, ordered streams of bytes, with retransmission and the rest — became TCP, now standing for the Transmission Control Protocol, layered on top of IP, used by those programs that wanted it. And for programs like Cohen's, which wanted IP's raw service with as little added as possible, a very small third protocol was defined, the User Datagram Protocol, UDP, which we shall meet properly in the third evening. That split is the moment the narrow waist of the hourglass was cut: IP alone in the middle, doing almost nothing, and everything clever above it.

There remained only the small matter of moving the whole ARPANET onto the new protocols. The ARPANET's hosts still spoke NCP; the new world spoke TCP and IP; and running both forever was not an option. So the managers of the network declared a flag day: a single date after which the old protocol would simply stop being carried. The date was the first of January, nineteen eighty-three. Sites that were not ready on the day found themselves cut off until they were. It was, by all accounts, a strenuous New Year for the people concerned, and some of them afterwards wore buttons announcing that they had survived the TCP transition. If the Internet has a birthday, that Saturday is as good a candidate as any.

So there is the second foundation. The Internet is not one network but a federation of thousands of separately owned networks, joined by gateways — routers — that do nothing but forward packets, best effort, toward their destinations. Every host on every network speaks IP, the common protocol, which is the glue; and everything that must be done properly is done by the hosts at the edges, in TCP and above. The design came from many hands: Baran's mesh, Davies's packets, Pouzin's datagrams, Cerf and Kahn's gateways, and a quarrel about the human voice that split one protocol into two. What it has never had, from that day to this, is an owner.

Part Three — Envelopes Within Envelopes

Consider, sir, how many separate jobs are concealed in the act of fetching a page. Something must turn bits into light, or voltage, or radio, and back again at the far end. Something must find a way across a planet of networks owned by thousands of strangers. Something must notice that a packet has gone missing and ask for it again, and put the survivors back in order. And something must actually want the page — must know what a web page is, and what to do with it when it comes. Four jobs, and each of them hard. Put them all in one program and you would have a program that nobody could change, because touching any part of it would disturb every other part.

So the engineers did what engineers have always done with a large and unruly job, which is to cut it into floors. The arrangement is called layering, and it is the first great idea of the field after the packet itself. Each layer does one job. Each layer uses the layer beneath it as a service, without caring how that service is provided. And each layer offers a service to the layer above, without caring what that layer intends to do with it. The floors of a well-run house, if you like: the kitchen does not need to know who is coming to dinner, only how many; the dining room does not need to know how the stove works, only that dinner arrives hot.

The Internet's own specification for how a host should behave — a document of nineteen eighty-nine called RFC eleven twenty-two, which we shall meet again — names four layers. From the bottom: the link layer, which moves bits across one physical link, one wire or one stretch of radio, to the next machine along. Above it, the internet layer, which is the Internet Protocol itself, and whose job is to get a packet from any machine on the Internet to any other, across as many links as it takes. Above that, the transport layer, whose job is to turn the internet layer's lottery of individual packets into something a program can use — for most programs, a reliable stream of bytes, delivered in order. And at the top, the application layer: the browser, the mail server, the database client, your own services — the programs that actually want something.

Now let me show you how the four collaborate, because the mechanism has a name, and once you have watched it work you will see it everywhere for the rest of your life. The name is encapsulation, and the image that goes with it is the oldest in the trade: envelopes within envelopes.

Here is the cast, stacked one above another inside your laptop. At the top, the browser, speaking the language of the web, which is HTTP. Beneath it, TCP, the transport. Beneath that, IP. And at the bottom, the link layer — the driver for your Wi-Fi card and the card itself. Four workers on four floors. Nothing will move except downward until the last of them has finished.

First, the browser writes its request. It is, in its oldest and plainest form, a few lines of ordinary text: the word GET, then the path of the page it wants, then the version of HTTP it speaks, and on the next line the name of the site — example dot com, let us say, a name reserved by the Internet's own rules for exactly this kind of illustration. That little block of text is the application's message. It is the letter.

Second, the browser hands the letter down to TCP. TCP does not read it. To TCP, the letter is so many bytes, and the bytes could be a web request or a love poem or a photograph of a cat. What TCP does is fix a small block of its own information to the front: which program on this machine is speaking, which program on the far machine is meant to listen, where these bytes fall in the stream of the whole conversation, and a few other particulars we shall come to in the third evening. That block of fields is called a header, and the letter with TCP's header fixed to the front is called a segment. The letter is now in its first envelope, and the envelope says something like page three of the conversation, for the program at the door marked four forty-three.

Third, TCP hands the segment down to IP. IP does not open the envelope either. It fixes its own header to the front: the address of this machine, the address of the destination machine, a counter called the time to live, which we shall meet next week, and a note saying that what is inside belongs to TCP. The segment with IP's header on the front is called a packet — or, in the Internet Protocol's own formal vocabulary, a datagram. Second envelope: this one bears the street address.

Fourth, IP hands the packet down to the link layer, which wraps it once more: a header in front, carrying the hardware addresses of this machine and of the next machine along the wire — not the final destination, mark you, only the next hop, the box in the hall that your laptop is speaking to — and a short trailer at the back carrying a checksum, so that the receiver can tell whether the radio garbled anything in transit. The whole bundle is called a frame. Third envelope — or, better, a mailbag, labelled only for the next sorting office. And the card turns the frame into radio and lets it go.

Let me say that once more, plainly, because the rest of the evening — and in truth the rest of the course — is built on it. The browser writes a letter. TCP puts it in an envelope marked with its place in the conversation. IP puts that in an envelope bearing the street address of the destination. The link layer puts that in a mailbag labelled for the next stop. A message, inside a segment, inside a packet, inside a frame.

Now follow the mailbag. It arrives at the box in your hall — your home router. The router's link layer checks the trailer's checksum, and, finding it good, strips off the frame header and the trailer and throws them away. The mailbag has done its work. What remains is the packet, and the router hands it up to its own IP layer, which reads the one thing it cares about: the destination address on the second envelope. It consults a table — the routing table, the subject of the next evening — to decide which of its links leads toward that address. It reduces the time-to-live counter by one. And it hands the packet back down to the link layer for the chosen link, which puts it into a fresh mailbag, labelled for the next sorting office along, and sends it on.

That happens at every router between you and the server — ten of them, twenty, sometimes thirty. At each one, the mailbag is opened and discarded, the street address is read, and the packet goes into a new mailbag for the next leg. The packet travels from end to end; the frames are made and unmade at every hop. And — this is the point — no router along the way opens the inner envelope. It does not read TCP's header. It certainly does not read your letter. The post office, sir, does not read your correspondence. In this one respect it is more discreet than a good many households.

At the far end, the process runs in reverse, floor by floor, upward. The server's link layer strips the frame. Its IP layer reads the street address, confirms that the packet is for this machine, and reads the note saying the contents belong to TCP. TCP strips its own header, uses the information in it to put this segment's bytes in their proper place in the conversation, and delivers the bytes, in order, to the program that was listening at door four forty-three. And the web server, at last, reads the letter: GET, a path, a version, a name. It has no idea that the letter crossed an ocean in thirty different mailbags. It has no need to.

Here is the subtle part, and it is worth a moment. Each header is a private memorandum from one layer to the same layer at the other end. The TCP header is written by TCP in your laptop and read by TCP in the server, and by nobody in between. The IP header is written by IP in your laptop and read by IP in every router and in the server — it is the only header the whole journey consults. The frame header is written for one link and read by the one machine at the other end of that link. The trade calls the layer at the far end a layer's peer, and the arrangement as a whole is this: each layer conducts a conversation with its peer, using the layer beneath as a sort of postal service, and neither knows nor cares how the postal service does its work.

Why go to all this trouble? Because it buys freedom, of a very particular kind. You can replace the link — Wi-Fi for cable, cable for fibre, fibre for a satellite — and TCP never notices; the same envelope travels by van or by train. You can invent a new application without asking a single router's permission, because routers do not read letters. You can improve TCP in the server and in the laptop without touching the network in between. Each floor of the house can be rebuilt while the others go on living in it. The Internet has been rebuilt in exactly this way, floor by floor, for half a century, without ever once being switched off.

The freedom has a price, and the price is in bytes. Every envelope costs something. The IP header is twenty bytes at the least; the TCP header another twenty, and usually a little more; the frame header and trailer another eighteen; and the link spends a few more bytes of preamble and silence between frames that never appear in any capture. On a full-sized packet — and on most of the networks you will meet, the largest packet a link will carry is fifteen hundred bytes, a figure we shall have cause to curse next week — the envelopes cost three or four percent. Nobody minds. But send a single keystroke to a remote machine, as a terminal does every time you touch a key, and you are posting one byte of letter in forty bytes of envelope, and paying postage on all of it. A postcard shipped in a crate. That indignity has a history, and a man's name attached to it, and an option in your code that you have very likely set without knowing why. All in good time; the third evening.

A word on vocabulary, because the trade is fussy here and the fussiness carries information. Frame belongs to the link layer, packet to the internet layer, segment to TCP, and message to the application — and an engineer who says frame is thinking about one wire, while one who says segment is thinking about TCP's bookkeeping. You will hear packet used loosely for all of them, as I shall sometimes use it myself when nothing turns on the distinction. But when a colleague says frame, it is a small courtesy to hear it as the clue it is.

Now, the numbers. I said the Internet's specification names four layers. You will nonetheless hear engineers talk about layer two, layer three, layer four, and layer seven, and no amount of counting to four will produce a seventh layer. The numbers come from somewhere else, and their provenance is a small war.

In nineteen seventy-seven the International Organization for Standardization — ISO, in the trade's shorthand — began work on a grand, principled, international architecture for connecting computers, called Open Systems Interconnection, OSI for short, and in nineteen eighty-four it published the reference model that went with it. The OSI model has seven layers. From the bottom: physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, application. Generations of students have learned them by a mnemonic — Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away — which is the sort of thing one never quite manages to forget, however hard one tries.

OSI was meant to be the future. It had the standards bodies, the telephone companies, and the governments behind it. European ministries backed it with enthusiasm, and the United States government published a profile of OSI in nineteen eighty-eight and made it mandatory, from nineteen ninety, for the networks its agencies would buy. The Internet's protocols, by comparison, were a research project's informal kit, documented in memoranda with the diffident title Request for Comments. And the Internet's protocols won — won completely — for a reason one of their designers put better than anyone. The occasion is worth knowing. In the summer of nineteen ninety-two the Internet's own architecture board proposed taking an OSI protocol as the basis for the next version of IP, and the working engineers of the Internet Engineering Task Force — the IETF, the open body that writes the Internet's standards — rose in something close to revolt. At their meeting that July, David Clark of MIT summed up their philosophy in two lines that have been quoted ever since: We reject: kings, presidents and voting. We believe in: rough consensus and running code. OSI had committees and votes and a magnificent design on paper. TCP/IP had working implementations, given away free with Berkeley's version of Unix, running on real machines that real people used every day. Running code beat the committee. It usually does. The American mandate for OSI was quietly withdrawn in nineteen ninety-five, by which time most of the world had stopped waiting for it.

But here is the curiosity. OSI's protocols are almost entirely gone; OSI's numbers survived, because they turned out to be useful vocabulary. When an engineer says layer two, the meaning is the link: Ethernet, Wi-Fi, hardware addresses, switches. Layer three means IP and routers. Layer four means the transport: TCP and UDP, and the port numbers that identify programs. And layer seven means the application: HTTP and its kin. Layers five and six — session and presentation — were orphaned; the Internet never had a use for them as separate floors, and when TLS came along, the encryption that guards your traffic, it sat so awkwardly between four and seven that engineers still argue over which floor it lives on. And engineers being engineers, the joke has long been that the hardest problems live at layer eight: the human being at the keyboard.

That vocabulary is the key to one of your rituals. You will have been asked, at some point, to choose between a layer-four load balancer and a layer-seven load balancer, and you will have chosen, I dare say, on the advice of someone who seemed to know. The numbers tell you what each one can see. A layer-four balancer reads the envelopes only as far as TCP's: the addresses and the port numbers. It can spread connections across servers, quickly and cheaply, but it cannot read the letter, so it cannot know that this request is for the checkout page and that one is for a picture of a teapot. A layer-seven balancer opens the letter. It reads the HTTP request, and so it can route by path, by header, by cookie — at the price of doing much more work, including, as we shall see, decrypting the letter in order to read it. Which to choose is a question for the fifth evening. That the choice is about how many envelopes the box is willing to open is something you now know.

One more picture, and the Part is done. Imagine the whole arrangement drawn as an hourglass. At the top, a great spread of applications: the web, mail, video calls, games, your company's remote procedure calls, things not yet invented. At the bottom, a great spread of links: Ethernet, Wi-Fi, fibre, the cellular network, satellites, and — we shall come to it — a pigeon. And in the middle, pinched down to a single protocol, the Internet Protocol. Everything above runs over IP; IP runs over everything below. Engineers call that pinch the narrow waist of the Internet — Steve Deering drew it exactly so, as an hourglass, in a celebrated talk to the IETF in two thousand and one — and the narrowness is the whole trick. Because every application need only speak to IP, and every link need only carry IP, the two halves of the hourglass can change independently, forever. An application written in nineteen ninety-five runs over a cellular network that did not exist until thirty years later, and neither had to be told about the other.

The waist has a cost, too, which is that it is very hard to change the waist itself. Everything depends on it; to alter it, everybody must alter at once. The Internet has been trying to replace its version of the Internet Protocol with a better one for about thirty years, with results we shall review next week, not without sympathy. And in principle, I said, the routers in the middle read only the street address. In practice a great many boxes in the middle of the Internet — firewalls, address translators, load balancers — have taken to opening the inner envelopes, peering at TCP's header and sometimes at the letter itself. Every such box is a small breach of the layering, and the layering, in its turn, has a way of punishing the breach. That quarrel runs through the whole course, and we shall render a verdict on it at the very end.

For now, the shape. Four jobs, four floors. Each floor wraps what it is given in an envelope of its own and hands it down; at the far end, each floor unwraps its own envelope and hands the rest up. The routers in between open only the outermost bag and read only the street address. A message, in a segment, in a packet, in a frame — and an hourglass, with IP at the waist.

Part Four — The Promise of Nothing

We have watched a packet travel. Let us now ask the question a careful man asks of any service he is about to depend upon: what, precisely, has it promised?

The Internet Protocol's answer is the title of this course. The network offers what the specifications call best effort delivery. It will try to get each packet to the address on the envelope. That is the whole of the undertaking. It does not promise that the packet will arrive. It does not promise that it will arrive only once. It does not promise that packets will arrive in the order they were sent, or within any particular time, or — quite — unaltered. And when it fails in any of these ways it will, as a rule, say nothing whatever about it.

I should like to take those failings one at a time, since each of them will cost you something before the course is out, and each has a perfectly mundane cause.

A packet may be lost. Most often it arrives at a router whose queue for the outgoing link is already full, and there is nowhere to put it, and so it is dropped — not mislaid, mark you, but deliberately discarded, because a router with no room has no other option. Sometimes a link fails, or a router restarts, and whatever was in flight goes with it. Sometimes a checksum reveals that a frame was damaged in transit, and the damaged frame is quietly thrown away.

A packet may be delayed, by any amount. It waits its turn in queues, and the queues are long or short according to everybody else's traffic, which you do not control and cannot see.

A packet may be duplicated. A link somewhere sends a frame, never hears that it arrived, and sends it again — and both copies survive.

Packets may be reordered. Two packets sent a microsecond apart take different paths, or different strands of a bundle of parallel links, and the second overtakes the first.

And a packet may, rarely, be corrupted in a way that every checksum along the way fails to notice. Rarely is not never; we shall see figures in the third evening that will make you thoughtful.

That is the service. Lost, late, doubled, shuffled, and very occasionally garbled, and nobody tells you. As a professional creed it has, I confess, a certain familiarity. A gentleman's gentleman undertakes to give satisfaction; he does not undertake that the weather will cooperate. But it is a startling thing to find at the foundation of an apparatus on which the world's banking, medicine, and commerce now depend. Why on earth would anybody build a network that promises nothing?

The contrast that answers the question is the telephone network, which promised a great deal. Lift the receiver, and the network gave you a dial tone, a circuit, a guaranteed rate, a clear line, a bill at the end of the month. To deliver all that, the network had to be clever: every exchange knew about every call passing through it, reserved resources for it, tracked it, and charged for it. And the cleverness was the network's own, owned and operated by the telephone company. If you wanted a new kind of service — a new way of using the line — you went to the company and asked, and you waited, sometimes for a decade.

The Internet's designers made the opposite bet. Keep the middle of the network as simple as it can possibly be: routers that forward packets and remember nothing about the conversations they belong to. Put all the cleverness at the edges, in the computers at either end, which are owned by the people who want the cleverness and can change it whenever they like. The bet has a name, and a paper, and it is the most important paper in this field that working engineers have never read.

In nineteen eighty-one, three researchers at MIT — Jerome Saltzer, David Reed, and David Clark, the same Clark who would later reject kings and presidents — presented a paper called End-to-End Arguments in System Design, published in its final form in nineteen eighty-four. It is short and lucid and I commend it to you without reservation. Its argument is usually stated as a slogan, and the slogan is usually misunderstood, so let me walk you through the example the authors themselves used, which they called careful file transfer.

The task is to copy a file from the disk of one computer — call it host A — to the disk of another, host B, across a network, and to be sure the copy is correct. The authors list the things that can go wrong, and the list repays attention. There are five.

First: the file, though written correctly to host A's disk long ago, may be read back incorrectly, because disks have faults.

Second: the software — the file system, the transfer program, the networking code — may make a mistake in copying the data from one buffer to another, on either machine.

Third: the hardware — the processor, the memory — may suffer a transient error while the data passes through it, on either machine.

Fourth: the network may drop a packet, or damage one, or deliver one twice.

Fifth: either computer may crash partway through, having done some unknown portion of the work.

Now the question. Suppose we build a perfect network: one that guarantees, absolutely, that every packet arrives once, in order, undamaged. Have we solved the problem of careful file transfer?

We have not. We have dealt with the fourth threat. The first, second, third, and fifth remain exactly where they were. The bytes can be spoiled on the disk before they leave, in a buffer on the way out, in memory on the way in, or abandoned by a crash. So the application must check anyway. And the way it checks is simple: when the copy is finished, host B computes a checksum over the whole file as written to its own disk, and compares it with a checksum computed by host A over the file as read from its own disk, and if they disagree, the transfer is done again.

And here is the turn of the argument. That one check, at the ends, catches every error — including the network's. Once the application has it, the network's guarantee was not needed for correctness at all. The authors put it this way, and the formulation has been quoted ever since: The function in question can completely and correctly be implemented only with the knowledge and help of the application standing at the end points of the communication system. Therefore, providing that questioned function as a feature of the communication system itself is not possible. And then, in parentheses, the sentence that keeps the argument honest: Sometimes an incomplete version of the function provided by the communication system may be useful as a performance enhancement.

Put the example in your own trade and it becomes rather less academic. At the end of a long training run you copy a checkpoint — some tens of gigabytes of model weights — from the cluster where it was written to the storage bucket where it will live. Every one of the five threats applies. A disk on the cluster may return a bad block; a buffer in the copying program may be mishandled; a memory chip may flip a bit as the data passes through; the network may damage a packet in a way its checksums do not catch; the job may be killed halfway. And the only defence that covers all five at once is the one you will find in every sensible copying tool: a checksum of the whole object, computed at the source and verified at the destination. If you have ever wondered why the storage service insists on a hash of what you uploaded, when the network underneath already has checksums of its own, you are looking at the end-to-end argument, enforced by somebody who learned it the hard way.

The authors applied the same reasoning to other functions, and one of them will matter greatly in the fourth evening: secrecy. Suppose the network encrypts every link — scrambles the data on each wire, and unscrambles it at each router. Is your data safe? Only if you trust every router, because inside each one the data sits in the clear, waiting to be scrambled again for the next wire. Encryption that protects you from the network must be done by the ends, with keys that only the ends hold. The network may encrypt its links as well, and that may help; but it cannot be trusted with the job. That is why the padlock in your browser is a matter between your laptop and the server, and none of the twenty routers in between is consulted.

The paper's authors had a story of their own to make the point, from their own network at MIT. A gateway there developed a fault — a transient one, in the part of the machine that copied data from one buffer to another — and began swapping a pair of bytes, about once in every million bytes that passed through it. Every link along the way had its own checksum, and every link's checksum was perfectly satisfied, because the damage was done inside the gateway, between links, where no link's checksum could see it. The corrupted data included source files of an operating system, and their owners, the authors note drily, were forced to the ultimate end-to-end error check: manual comparison with and correction from old listings. Every hop was reliable. The whole was not.

Let me say all of that in plain words, because it is the hinge on which the whole Internet turns. If a job can only be done properly by the programs at the two ends, then it must be done by them, whatever the network does. The network may help, to make things faster — a link that loses half its packets would make whole-file retries ruinous, so a little reliability in the middle is worth having — but the network cannot be trusted with the job, and need not attempt it in full.

You already practise the end-to-end argument, sir, whether or not you have ever heard its name. When your service calls another service and waits for its answer, the answer is the end-to-end acknowledgement. Note what the transport's own acknowledgement does not tell you. When TCP at the far end acknowledges your bytes, it is saying that they reached the far machine's kernel — not that the far program read them, still less that it acted on them, and certainly not that it acted on them correctly. The doorman has signed for the parcel. Whether the master of the house ever opened it is another matter entirely. Only the master's reply tells you that, and that is why every serious protocol between services carries its own acknowledgement at the top — the response — however reliable the layers beneath it claim to be.

And this explains one of your rituals, perhaps the most important of them. You retry failed calls. Of course you do: the network promises nothing, so the only remedy for loss is to ask again. But you have also been told, I imagine more than once and with some emphasis, that the call you retry must be idempotent — that doing it twice must have the same effect as doing it once. Now you can see why. When a call fails, you do not know where it failed. Perhaps your request was lost on the way out, and the far end never heard it. Or perhaps the request arrived, the work was done, and only the reply was lost on the way back. From where you stand the two are indistinguishable. Retry the second case and you have charged the customer twice. The network's best effort is the reason you must retry; the network's silence about what failed is the reason the retry must be safe.

Now, the end-to-end argument tells us where the cleverness should live. A second paper tells us why the Internet's designers cared so much about putting it there, and it explains more about the Internet's character — its virtues and its chronic ailments both — than any other document I know. In nineteen eighty-eight, David Clark, by then the Internet's chief architect, wrote down after the fact what the designers had been trying to achieve, in a paper called The Design Philosophy of the DARPA Internet Protocols. Its central passage is a list of goals, and Clark insists that the order of the list is the point: an entirely different network, he says, would have resulted had the order been different.

The fundamental goal was to find an effective way of using many existing, separately built networks together — an internet, in the lower-case sense, a network of networks. Beneath it, in order of importance, come seven more. I shall give you them in two groups, so that you can hold them.

The first three. One: communication must continue despite the loss of networks or gateways — survivability. Two: the Internet must support several types of service, not only reliable streams. Three: it must accommodate a variety of networks underneath.

The last four. Four: its resources must be capable of distributed management — many owners, no single one in charge. Five: it must be cost-effective. Six: attaching a new host to it must take little effort. And seven — last — the resources it uses must be accountable.

Survivability first. Accountability last. Clark is candid that the order reflects the context: the work was paid for by the American defence research agency, and a network meant for a hostile environment puts staying alive above keeping the books. Now watch how much of the Internet you know follows from that one ordering.

Survivability first means that communication must go on as long as any path at all exists between the two ends. Which means the network must not hold anything about a conversation that would be lost if a router died, because routers die. So the state of every conversation — what has been sent, what has been acknowledged, what must be sent again — is kept at the ends, in the two computers having the conversation, and nowhere else. Clark gave that arrangement a name of its own: fate-sharing. The state of a conversation shares the fate of the endpoint that holds it; it is lost only if the endpoint itself is lost, in which case, Clark observes, the conversation was doomed anyway. That is why a router in the middle of your connection can crash and restart while you are downloading something, and the download simply pauses and carries on: the router never knew the download existed.

The ARPANET itself had supplied an object lesson in what a clever middle can do. On the twenty-seventh of October, nineteen eighty, a fault in a single IMP corrupted a few bits of a routing message — one of the messages the IMPs exchanged among themselves to keep their picture of the network up to date. The damage produced three versions of the same message, each of which, by the IMPs' arithmetic for deciding which message was newest, appeared newer than another, in a perfect circle, like a game of rock, paper, scissors. Every IMP dutifully passed on whichever version it had decided was newest, forever, until the network's memory was full of them. The entire ARPANET went down for hours, and every IMP had to be restarted. The engineer who wrote up the postmortem, Eric Rosen, made the lesson plain: the more the network itself must remember and coordinate, the more ways it has to fail all at once. The Internet's routers do still exchange information about routes, as we shall see next week; but no router holds anything about your conversation, and that is a mercy.

Accountability last means, among other things, that the Internet was never built to know who sent what, or to charge for it, or to stop it. The source address on a packet is whatever the sender chose to write there; nothing in the core checks it. There is no billing in the protocol. There is no notion of a sender's reputation. Every chronic illness of the modern Internet — spam, forged source addresses, floods of junk traffic aimed at a victim by a hundred thousand hijacked machines — is the price of goal seven being seventh. Had the network been designed by a bank, the list would have run the other way, and we should have had an accountable, billable, auditable network that nobody would have been permitted to build anything on.

That last clause is not a joke. Because the core promises nothing and remembers nothing, anybody can build a new kind of application at the edges without asking the network's owners for anything. The web was built that way, at a physics laboratory in Switzerland, by a man who asked nobody's permission. So were video calls, and streaming, and the protocols your own company's services speak to one another. The telephone network promised everything and changed at the speed of its owners. The Internet promised nothing and changed at the speed of everybody else.

Before we leave the subject of what the network promises, let me hand you a catechism — a list of things the network does not promise, drawn up at Sun Microsystems in the nineteen-nineties: the first four named by Bill Joy and Tom Lyon, three more set down by Peter Deutsch, and the eighth added by James Gosling. It has been known ever since as the fallacies of distributed computing. They are the assumptions that every engineer new to networks makes, and that the network punishes. There are eight, and I shall give them in two fours.

The first four. The network is reliable. Latency is zero. Bandwidth is infinite. The network is secure.

The second four. Topology does not change. There is one administrator. Transport cost is zero. The network is homogeneous.

Every one of them is false, and this course is in large part an itemized account of how each is false and what to do about it. That the network is unreliable, you have heard tonight. That latency is not zero and bandwidth is not infinite is the subject of the next Part. That the network is not secure is the fourth evening. That topology changes and there are thousands of administrators, all strangers, is the second. And that the network is not homogeneous, you have already seen in the hourglass: everything is different underneath, and IP pretends otherwise on our behalf.

So: the network promises its best effort and nothing more. The ends do everything that must be done right — because only the ends can do it right, as Saltzer, Reed, and Clark showed with a careful file transfer; and because the network was built to survive the loss of any part of itself, as Clark explained, which meant keeping no conversation's state anywhere but at the ends. What looks at first like a deficiency is the design. The Internet's core is dumb on purpose, and it is the dumbness that made it immortal.

Part Five — The Speed of Light Is Not Negotiable

There are two quantities in networking that everybody confuses, including, now and then, people who should know better, and the confusion costs more money than any other single misunderstanding in the field. So let us separate them with some care.

The first is latency: how long it takes for a bit to get from here to there. One bit, one trip. It is measured in units of time — milliseconds, microseconds — and it is, at bottom, a matter of distance.

The second is bandwidth: how many bits per second a link can carry. It is measured in bits per second — megabits, gigabits — and it is, at bottom, a matter of how wide the pipe is.

Think of a water pipe running from a reservoir to your house. Bandwidth is the pipe's width: how much water it can deliver each second once the water is flowing. Latency is the pipe's length: how long it takes the first drop to arrive after you open the valve. A wide pipe across a continent and a narrow pipe across the street are different in both respects, and you can make a pipe wider without making it any shorter. That last observation is the whole of the matter. Bandwidth can be bought. You can lay more fibre, light more strands, buy a faster card. Latency, past a certain point, cannot be bought at any price, because past that point latency is the speed of light, and the speed of light is not negotiable.

Let me be more exact about where a packet's time goes, because "latency" is really four different delays added together, and each has its own cause and its own cure. I shall take them one at a time.

The first is propagation delay: the time for the signal to travel the length of the link. It is distance divided by speed, and nothing else. In a vacuum, light travels about three hundred thousand kilometres a second, which comes to just under thirty centimetres — about a foot — in a nanosecond. The late Rear Admiral Grace Hopper of the United States Navy, who could explain anything to anyone, used to hand out pieces of wire eleven point eight inches long at her lectures, and tell her audience that each was a nanosecond: the distance light travels in a billionth of a second. When admirals asked her why a message by satellite took so long, she would produce a much longer coil — a microsecond, nine hundred and eighty-four feet of it — and point out how many of those lay between the ground and the satellite. For picoseconds, she handed out grains of pepper.

But your packets do not travel in a vacuum. Across any distance that matters they travel as light in glass, inside optical fibre, and glass slows light down. The factor is called the refractive index, and for the fibre in the ground it is about one and a half. So light in fibre travels at roughly two hundred thousand kilometres a second — two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum. Here is the figure to carry away, if you carry away only one: five microseconds per kilometre. Or, if you prefer, one millisecond for every two hundred kilometres of glass. Admiral Hopper's foot of wire, in fibre, is about eight inches.

The second delay is transmission delay, which the trade also calls serialization delay. It is the time it takes to push all of a packet's bits onto the link, one after another, before the last of them has left. It is the packet's size divided by the link's rate. A full-sized packet of fifteen hundred bytes is twelve thousand bits. On a ten-gigabit link those bits go out in one point two microseconds. On a gigabit link, twelve microseconds. On an old ten-megabit Ethernet, one point two milliseconds. And on the fifty-six-kilobit modem that some of us remember with a fondness the device did nothing to deserve, about a fifth of a second — for one packet. The picture is a train passing through a station: propagation delay is how long the locomotive takes to get from one town to the next, and transmission delay is how long the whole train takes to pass the platform. A faster link means a shorter train, not a faster locomotive.

The third is queueing delay: the time a packet spends waiting at a router behind other packets that arrived first for the same outgoing link. On an idle link it is zero. On a busy one it can be the largest delay of all, and it varies from one packet to the next with everybody else's traffic. When people complain that "the network is slow", what they are usually describing, without knowing it, is a queue.

The fourth is processing delay: the time a router takes to read the header and decide where the packet goes. In modern hardware it is a matter of microseconds or less, and I mention it chiefly for completeness.

So: propagation depends on distance, and only distance. Transmission depends on packet size and link speed. Queueing depends on everybody else. Processing depends on the router. Three of the four can be improved with money and engineering. The first cannot be improved at all, except by moving the two ends closer together — which is, as it happens, the true function of a great deal of the Internet's most expensive infrastructure.

Let me put numbers on a single packet, so that you can see which of the four delays matters. Send one full-sized packet from Toronto to London. It crosses perhaps fifteen routers. At each, on links of ten gigabits a second, its transmission delay is about a microsecond, so fifteen of them add up to perhaps twenty microseconds. Processing, at each router, is less again. Propagation, across some six or seven thousand kilometres of real cable, is over thirty milliseconds. So on an uncongested path, propagation is something like a thousand times all the other delays put together, and the packet's journey is, to a first approximation, a pure function of geography. The other delays become interesting only when a queue somewhere is long — and then, as we shall see in a moment, they can become very interesting indeed.

Now, almost nothing useful on a network happens in one direction. You send a request; you wait for the answer. You offer to open a connection; you wait for the other side to agree. So the measure that governs the experience of using a network is the time for a packet to go and for the reply to come back: the round-trip time, which the trade abbreviates to RTT and says as three letters. The round trip is the unit of cost in networking, the way a disk seek once was in databases. If you remember one practical habit from this evening, let it be this: when you look at any protocol, any service call, any page load, count the round trips.

Let us count some, with the physics in hand.

From Toronto to Chicago is about seven hundred kilometres as the crow flies. Light in glass covers that in three and a half milliseconds — seven for the round trip — if the glass ran straight, which it never does. Cables follow railways and highways and the boundaries of property, and the packets pass through routers that queue and process. Round trips in practice are perhaps twice the physics.

From Toronto to London is about five thousand seven hundred kilometres. Twenty-eight milliseconds of glass one way, fifty-six for the round trip, at the very least; in practice, between those two cities, something like eighty or ninety.

From Toronto to Tokyo is a little over ten thousand kilometres: a hundred milliseconds for the round trip before a single router has had its say.

In two thousand and nine Jeff Dean of Google, giving a keynote talk on building large distributed systems, put up a slide of the numbers, as he called them, that everyone should know, and it has circulated ever since. Two lines on it concern us. A round trip within the same data centre: half a millisecond. A packet sent from California to the Netherlands and back: a hundred and fifty milliseconds. The first figure has fallen a great deal since — within a modern data centre, round trips of tens of microseconds are ordinary. The second has hardly moved, and never will by much, because the second is geography.

And here is a figure worth carrying about with you, because it is the floor under the entire planet. No two places on the Earth's surface are more than about twenty thousand kilometres apart. Twenty thousand kilometres of glass is a tenth of a second. So no round trip between two points on this planet, by fibre, can ever take less than about a fifth of a second — not with any technology, any budget, any amount of cleverness. Perth, in Western Australia, is most of the way round the world from Toronto, and the physics puts the round trip there at nearly a hundred and eighty milliseconds before anybody has done anything. The speed of light, sir, is the one constant in your architecture that no vendor can improve.

How much is a millisecond worth? We have an unusually exact answer, because some people have paid for them in cash. In two thousand and ten a company called Spread Networks finished laying a fibre-optic cable from Chicago to a data centre in northern New Jersey where the stock exchanges' computers live, at a reported cost of some three hundred million dollars. The existing cables wandered; this one, some eight hundred and twenty-five miles of it, was dug as straight as the ground permitted, largely in secret, through the Allegheny Mountains — and it brought the round trip between the futures market in Chicago and the equity markets in the east from about fourteen and a half milliseconds down to about thirteen. Traders leased the line at prices that made sense only because a millisecond's advantage in seeing a price move was worth a fortune; the story is the opening chapter of Michael Lewis's Flash Boys. And within a couple of years the fibre had been beaten — by chains of microwave towers along much the same route, which brought the round trip down to about eight and a half milliseconds. Microwaves travel through air, and air, unlike glass, barely slows light at all; and a line of towers can be made straighter than any trench. The cable had been laid with enormous labour to be the fastest possible path, and it lost to the sky.

The same logic has been applied to the Atlantic. In two thousand and fifteen a new cable called Hibernia Express came into service, at a cost of about three hundred million dollars, chiefly to bring the round trip between New York and London under fifty-nine milliseconds — some five or six milliseconds better than the cables before it. And there is now so-called hollow-core fibre, in which the light travels through air in a hollow channel at the centre of the cable rather than through the glass itself; Microsoft bought the British company that makes it in two thousand and twenty-two. Light in the hollow core travels at very nearly its full speed, so the delay over a given distance falls by about a third. The marketing department, you will not be surprised to learn, prefers to say that the light goes forty-seven percent faster, which is the same fact wearing a better suit. People spend hundreds of millions of dollars on milliseconds for exactly the reason I gave: latency is the one thing money cannot otherwise buy. When the only remaining lever is the path, they straighten the path; when the path is straight, they change the medium.

Now let us give bandwidth its due, because bandwidth and latency are entirely independent of each other, and the clearest way to see it is through a series of famously absurd networks.

Andrew Tanenbaum, the author of the textbook from which a generation learned this subject, offered the classic one: never underestimate the bandwidth of a station wagon full of tapes hurtling down the highway. Fill a car with storage, drive it across the country, and you have moved more data in a day than any network of the period could move in a month. The bandwidth is colossal. The latency is a day and a half and a tank of petrol. Amazon took the joke seriously. At its annual conference in two thousand and sixteen it drove a lorry onto the stage: a forty-five-foot shipping container of disks holding up to a hundred petabytes, offered to customers who wished to move a whole data centre's worth of data into its cloud. It has since been retired, but for a few years the largest-bandwidth network link on offer from the world's largest cloud was a truck.

And then there is the pigeon. On the first of April, nineteen ninety, the Internet's own series of technical memoranda published RFC eleven forty-nine, A Standard for the Transmission of IP Datagrams on Avian Carriers. It specifies, with perfect deadpan, how to print a packet on paper, roll it, fasten it to a pigeon's leg, and release the bird. And it contains a sentence that could stand as the epigraph of this course: Because IP only guarantees best effort delivery, loss of a carrier can be tolerated. Eleven years later, on a Saturday in April two thousand and one, the Linux users' group of Bergen, in Norway, actually did it. They sent nine ping packets by pigeon across some five kilometres of the city. Four came back, which the software duly reported as fifty-five percent packet loss; and the round trips were measured not in milliseconds but in minutes — the fastest about fifty-four of them, the slowest an hour and three quarters. It was, by the standards of the protocol, a complete success.

And in September two thousand and nine, in South Africa, a company frustrated with its broadband raced a pigeon against it. The pigeon, named Winston and eleven months old, carried a memory card holding four gigabytes some eighty kilometres in an hour and eight minutes. Counting the time to load the data at the far end, the whole transfer took a little over two hours — in which time the company's broadband line had managed to send about four percent of the same data.

The pigeon is a high-bandwidth, high-latency link. So is a lorry of disks. So, for that matter, is a satellite in geostationary orbit, nearly thirty-six thousand kilometres up: wide pipes all, and long ones. A message to the far side of the world and back by such a satellite spends something like half a second just climbing up to the satellite and back down twice. The satellite constellations now being flown in low orbit, a few hundred kilometres up, exist precisely to shorten that pipe.

Two consequences of all this matter to you directly, and I shall take each in turn.

The first is that small transfers are governed by latency, not bandwidth. A typical web page is assembled from many modest files, each needing at least a round trip and some needing several. On a fast connection the bits themselves take almost no time; the time goes into waiting for round trips. Double your bandwidth and the page barely notices. Halve your round-trip time and the page comes up in nearly half the time. A network engineer named Stuart Cheshire made this point in an essay of May nineteen ninety-six, under the title It's the Latency, Stupid, and its first two section headings say everything: Making more bandwidth is easy. And: Once you have bad latency you're stuck with it. That essay explains one of your rituals perfectly. Your broadband speed test says five hundred megabits a second, and yet the page is slow. Of course it is. The speed test measures the width of the pipe, by filling it. The page is waiting on the pipe's length.

The second consequence is subtler, and it is the foundation of a good deal of the third evening. A long, wide pipe holds a great deal of data in transit at any moment. Multiply the bandwidth by the round-trip time and you get the amount of data that must be in flight — sent but not yet acknowledged — to keep the pipe full. That product is called, reasonably enough, the bandwidth-delay product, and it is the volume of the pipe. A one-gigabit link to London, with a round trip of eighty milliseconds, holds eighty million bits in flight — ten megabytes. Now suppose your sending program, or your operating system, will only allow sixty-four kilobytes to be outstanding before it stops and waits for an acknowledgement. It sends its sixty-four kilobytes, which fill a thousandth of the pipe; then it waits eighty milliseconds for the acknowledgement to come back; then it sends sixty-four kilobytes more. Do the arithmetic and your gigabit link to London is delivering about six and a half megabits a second — under one percent of what you are paying for. Nothing is broken. Every component is behaving perfectly. The pipe is simply too long for the amount of data you are prepared to have in the air at once. How TCP learned to keep long pipes full is a story for the third evening; that the problem exists at all is a fact of geometry.

One last idea, and the Part is done: queues, and why networks are never run full.

There is a beautiful little result in queueing theory, due to John Little in nineteen sixty-one, which says that the average number of things in any stable queueing system equals the rate at which they arrive multiplied by the average time each spends inside it. It sounds like a tautology and is a theorem. But the result that matters to you here is what happens to waiting time as a link gets busier. In the simplest standard model of a queue — packets arriving at random, served one at a time — the average time a packet spends in the system is its service time divided by one minus the utilization. Let me give you that as numbers rather than as a formula. At fifty percent utilization, a packet spends twice its service time in the system. At eighty percent, five times. At ninety percent, ten times. At ninety-nine percent, a hundred times.

If the formula leaves you cold, think of a single checkout at the grocer's, with a clerk who takes a minute per customer on average — some customers have one item and some have forty — and customers who wander up at random. If one turns up every two minutes on average, the clerk is busy half the time, and you spend about two minutes at the checkout, waiting and being served together. If they turn up nine to every ten minutes, the clerk is busy ninety percent of the time — and yet you will, on average, spend ten minutes at that checkout for one minute's service, because the random clumps of arrivals pile up faster than the clerk can clear them, and there is never enough slack to catch up. The clerk is not lazy and the customers are not unreasonable. The arithmetic of random arrivals is simply unkind near the top.

Draw that and you get a curve like a hockey stick: flat and comfortable for most of its length, and then, as the link approaches full, a sudden wall. That is why operators run links well below their capacity; why a link that is "only" ninety percent busy can feel catastrophically slow; and why, when somebody says the network is slow, the first suspect is a queue that has crossed the bend of the hockey stick. Real traffic is burstier than the model, which makes matters worse, not better.

And now three of your rituals explain themselves. Why do you put a content delivery network in front of your site? Because you cannot make light faster, so you move the bytes closer to the people who want them — a copy in Toronto for the visitors in Toronto. Why are you told to keep chatty services in the same region, even the same zone? Because a call that crosses a continent costs tens of milliseconds of pure physics each way, and a service that makes fifty such calls to answer one request has spent a second on geography alone. And why are you told to batch calls rather than make them one at a time? Because fifty requests sent one after another, each waiting for its answer, cost fifty round trips; the same fifty sent together cost one. In every case the ritual is the same insight in a different coat: when you cannot shorten the pipe, use it fewer times.

So. Latency is the length of the pipe and bandwidth its width, and they are independent. Four delays make up latency — propagation, transmission, queueing, processing — and only propagation is fixed by nature, at five microseconds a kilometre of glass. The round trip is the unit of cost; count them. A long fat pipe needs much data in flight to stay full. And a queue near capacity is a wall, not a slope.

Part Six — A Page, at Low Resolution

We now have, between us, enough vocabulary for a first look at the whole journey — the thing that happens a few hundred times a day under your fingers, and which this course will spend its remaining four evenings taking apart. Tonight I shall walk it once, at low resolution, the way one first looks at a map of a city before learning its streets. In the fifth evening we shall walk it again at full resolution, and you will, I hope, be surprised by how much you can then see.

The scene. You are at your desk in Toronto. Your laptop is on the household Wi-Fi. You type the name of a web site — example dot com will do — and you press the return key.

Let me announce the cast before anything moves, because there are six of them and they will come and go quickly. One: your laptop. Two: the box in the hall — the home router, which is also the Wi-Fi access point, and which also does a little sleight of hand with addresses that we shall examine in the third evening. Three: your internet provider's network, a large and, from where you sit, invisible collection of routers. Four: a resolver — a server whose job is to look up names on your behalf; it may belong to your provider, or to a public service. Five: the Internet beyond your provider: thousands of other networks, each owned by somebody else. And six: at the far end, the front door of whatever machine room serves example dot com.

The first thing your laptop must do is discover where example dot com is. Packets are addressed with numbers, not names; the name is for you. So the laptop asks the resolver: what is the address of example dot com? The question is small enough to fit in a single packet, and so is the answer. If the resolver already knows — because somebody asked recently and it remembered — the answer comes back in one round trip to the resolver, which, if the resolver is nearby, is a matter of a few milliseconds. If it does not know, the resolver goes off and asks a series of other servers around the world, starting at the root of the whole naming system, and each of those consultations is a round trip of its own. The system that does this is the Domain Name System, DNS, and it is a distributed database of quite extraordinary size and quite extraordinary fragility, which is the subject of the first half of the fourth evening.

But wait. Before the laptop can ask the resolver anything, it has to know who the resolver is, and get a packet to it, and the resolver is not in the hall. Both facts were settled long before you pressed the key — when the laptop first joined the household network. At that moment it announced itself and asked, in effect, who am I here, and how do I get out? The box in the hall answered, by a protocol called DHCP, with three things: an address for the laptop to use on this network; the address of the box itself, to which everything bound for the outside world should be handed; and the address of a resolver to ask about names. Every machine you have ever connected to a network has had that little conversation, usually in less time than it takes to notice that the Wi-Fi symbol has filled in. So the laptop knows that the resolver is not on the household network, and that anything not on the household network goes to the box in the hall. To hand it over, the laptop needs the box's hardware address, which is a different thing from its internet address — why a machine needs two addresses is one of the pleasures of next week. Having found it, the laptop wraps the packet in a frame addressed to the box and sends it over the radio.

The box in the hall forwards the packet to your provider, and your provider's routers forward it onward, each one consulting its table and choosing the next hop. Somewhere in that table is knowledge of how to reach every network on Earth, learned from neighbours who learned it from their neighbours, under a protocol called BGP that runs on trust and money in roughly equal measure. Ten routers, twenty, each making a local decision with no knowledge of the whole route, and somehow the packet arrives. How it arrives — addresses, prefixes, tables, the longest match, and the treaties between networks — is the second evening.

Now the laptop has an address for example dot com. It wishes to send a request. But first it must open a connection, because the browser wants a reliable, ordered stream of bytes, and the network, you will recall, offers nothing of the kind. So TCP, in your laptop, sends a small packet to the server that amounts to may I; the server replies with a packet that amounts to you may, and may I; and your laptop replies you may. Only then can data flow. That exchange is the famous three-way handshake, and it costs one full round trip before a single byte of your request has been sent. Why three messages and not two, and what the numbers inside them are for, is the third evening.

The connection is open, but it is not yet private. Anybody on any of those twenty links could read what passes over it, and anybody who controls one of the routers could pretend to be example dot com. So the two ends now perform a second negotiation: they agree on keys with which to encrypt everything that follows, and the server proves that it really is example dot com by presenting a certificate — a document signed by someone your laptop already trusts. That is TLS, the protocol behind the padlock, and in its current version it costs one more round trip. How two strangers can agree on a secret while a third listens to every word they say is, I think, the most beautiful idea in this course, and it is the second half of the fourth evening.

Now, at last, the browser sends its letter: GET, a path, a version, a name. The server sends back the page. One more round trip, at least. For a large page, several more, because TCP begins every connection cautiously, sending a little, waiting to see whether the network can bear it, and only then sending more; the third evening explains why it must. And the page, when it arrives, turns out to refer to other files — stylesheets, scripts, fonts, pictures — each of which must be requested in turn, some over the same connection, some over new ones, each new one beginning the whole dance again.

Meanwhile, on the server's side, answering your one request may take a great deal of conversation of its own. The machine that received it asks a service for your account, another for the page's contents, a third for recommendations, a fourth for prices — dozens of calls, each a round trip, though round trips of microseconds inside one building rather than milliseconds across an ocean. Your single request fans out into a small storm of internal traffic and gathers back into one response, and the response cannot leave until the slowest of those internal calls has answered. That fact will matter enormously in the fifth evening, where it has a name of its own.

And at the far end, the "server" is not a server. It is a front door. Behind it, there is very likely a load balancer choosing among many servers; in front of it, quite possibly, a copy of the site held in a building in Toronto by a content delivery network, so that your packets need not cross the continent at all. Behind the servers lie the fabric of a data centre, with tens of thousands of machines talking to one another far more than they talk to you — and in some buildings, a network more expensive than any other, joining thousands of accelerators that are training the models of your own trade. That is the fifth evening: the machine room.

Now let us count, because counting round trips is the habit I asked you to form. Suppose the server is in London, ninety milliseconds away by round trip, and the resolver is nearby and already knows the answer. A few milliseconds for the name. Ninety for the handshake. Ninety for the encryption. Ninety for the request and the first part of the response. That is two hundred and seventy-odd milliseconds — more than a quarter of a second — before the first byte of the page arrives, and the server has done no work whatever; all of that time is the speed of light in glass, paid three times over. With the previous version of TLS, which needed two round trips for its negotiation, add another ninety. With a resolver that must consult the root and work downward, add more. And then the sub-resources, each with its own requests.

That arithmetic is the whole explanation for the frantic ingenuity of the last fifteen years of web engineering. Keep connections open and reuse them, so that the handshake and the negotiation are paid once rather than for every request. Combine the transport handshake and the encryption handshake into one, which is what the newest transport, QUIC, does. Let a returning visitor send data in the very first packet. And above all, move the server closer: if the copy of the page is in Toronto, five milliseconds away, the same three round trips cost fifteen milliseconds instead of two hundred and seventy. Every one of those techniques is an attack on the round trip, because the round trip is the one cost that bandwidth cannot buy down.

One more thing to notice on the map, because it will turn out to be the most useful thing on it. Each stage of the journey fails in its own characteristic way, and the symptom tells you the stage. If the name cannot be found, you are told that the host could not be resolved. If the route is broken, your packets vanish and you wait, and wait, for a timeout — or, sometimes, a router somewhere sends back word that the destination is unreachable. If the far machine is there but nothing is listening at the door you knocked on, the connection is refused, instantly and politely. If the certificate is wrong, the browser throws up a page of warnings. If the request reaches the server and the server is unwell, you get an error code from HTTP itself. And if the front door is there but the machines behind it are not, you get the particular family of errors that load balancers return when they have nobody to hand your request to. By the end of the course you should be able to hear any of those symptoms and say, with reasonable confidence, which floor of the house is on fire.

So there is the map, and here is how the evenings divide it. Tonight: what a network is — the packet, the layers, the promise of nothing, and the physics. Next week: where the packet goes — addresses and routes, from the wire in your hall to the treaties between nations' networks. The third evening: whether it arrived — the transport, and the manners by which two machines hold a reliable conversation through a post that loses letters. The fourth: who is speaking, and who is listening — the directory of names, and the system of credentials by which a stranger proves an identity. And the fifth: why it is all so slow, and what the great machine rooms do about it — before we walk this journey again, at full resolution, and see every promise being made, and every place where a promise can break.

What the Boy Should Have Been Told

So, sir. Had the boy sat the course, here is what he would have carried out of the first lecture, and what I hope you will carry out of this one.

A network can be built in one of two ways. The telephone's way reserves a path from end to end for the whole of a conversation, and guarantees everything along it, and wastes whatever the conversation does not use. The letter's way reserves nothing. It cuts every message into packets, writes the full address on each, and lets every switch along the way store the packet, read the address, forward it one step, and forget it. The links are shared among everybody, packet by packet, on the statistical bet that not everybody will want them at once; and when that bet loses, packets wait in queues, and when the queues are full, packets are thrown away. Paul Baran chose the letter to survive a war, and Donald Davies chose it to save money, and Davies named it, and the name stuck.

The Internet is a network of networks, joined by routers that forward packets and do nothing else, all speaking one common protocol, IP, at the narrow waist of an hourglass: every application above it, every kind of link below. Everything rides in envelopes within envelopes — a message inside a TCP segment inside an IP packet inside a frame — and each layer reads only its own envelope, so that each can be rebuilt without disturbing the others. The routers open only the outermost bag and read only the street address. The numbers you hear — layer two, layer three, layer four, layer seven — are the surviving vocabulary of a seven-layer model whose protocols lost a war to running code.

IP promises nothing but its best effort: packets may be lost, delayed, duplicated, reordered, or, rarely, garbled, and nobody will tell you. This is not a defect but a decision. Saltzer, Reed, and Clark showed, with a careful file transfer, that the things which must be done right can only be done right at the ends, so the middle need not try; and Clark showed why the designers wanted it so — survivability first, accountability last, with every conversation's state held at the ends where it shares the fate of the machines that hold it. The network is dumb on purpose, and that is why anybody could build anything on it without asking.

And the network has a length as well as a width. Bandwidth is how much it carries; latency is how long it takes; they are different things, and only one of them can be bought. Light in glass travels five microseconds a kilometre, so a round trip across an ocean costs tens of milliseconds and a round trip to the far side of the world costs a fifth of a second, whatever anybody spends. The round trip is the unit of cost, and the habit to form is to count them. A long, wide pipe holds a great deal of data in flight and must be kept full; and a queue near its capacity is not a slope but a wall.

Several of your rituals explained themselves along the way. You retry because the network promises nothing; and the retry must be idempotent because the network's silence cannot tell you whether it was your request or the reply that went astray. The difference between a layer-four and a layer-seven load balancer is how many envelopes the box is prepared to open. You put content behind a delivery network and keep chatty services in one region because light will not hurry; you batch calls because fifty round trips cost fifty times one. And the speed test that promises five hundred megabits while the page crawls is measuring the width of a pipe whose trouble is its length.

For reading, two recommendations, one for pleasure and one for the mind. For pleasure: Katie Hafner and Matthew Lyon's Where Wizards Stay Up Late, the story of the people who built the ARPANET, told as a story, with the night of the twenty-ninth of October in it. For the mind: Saltzer, Reed, and Clark, End-to-End Arguments in System Design. It is a short paper. It is the most important paper in this field that working engineers have never read, and you will not be one of them.

A word, finally, about the sentence with which I shall end each of these evenings. It is borrowed. It was written, a long time ago, for machines rather than for people — as advice on how a program should behave when it talks to other programs it did not write — and it became, for a generation of engineers, something closer to a creed. I shall tell you whose it is, and in which specification it was written down, when we reach that specification, which will be in the third evening. And at the very end of the course, I shall tell you whether I think it was right. For the present, sir, I offer it to you simply as good advice, which, as advice goes, it rather is.

A telephone company carries out the order, and within minutes, by means of a perfectly ordinary mechanism that every router on Earth obeys without question, YouTube vanishes for most of the world. To understand how, we shall have to learn how a packet finds its way — from the wire in your hall, through the box that holds two addresses for every machine, past the routing table and its single, ruthless rule, to the treaties by which some seventy-odd thousand networks agree to carry one another's mail. The rule, as it happens, is the title of the evening.

Be conservative in what you send, sir, and liberal in what you accept.
