The Monday Facebook Left the Internet

Good day, sir.

At about twenty to twelve in the morning, Toronto time, on Monday the fourth of October, two thousand and twenty-one, the traffic flowing to Facebook from the rest of the Internet very nearly stopped. A company that measures traffic for a living, watching the flows across a great many networks, said afterwards that it virtually disappeared, in a matter of moments. Facebook was gone. So was Instagram. So was WhatsApp, which, in a great many countries, is the telephone.

At a company called Cloudflare, which runs one of the large public resolvers you met last week, engineers noticed within minutes that their resolver had begun failing every question about Facebook's names. Their account, published that same day, records their first reaction with commendable honesty: Facebook can't be down, can it? — they thought, for a second. Then they looked at the Internet's routing, and saw something that had no business being there. At the very moment the trouble began, there had been a burst of routing changes from Facebook's own network. And by two minutes to noon, Toronto time, the announcements for the blocks of addresses that held Facebook's DNS servers had been withdrawn from the Internet entirely. The routes were simply not in anybody's table any more. As far as the rest of the world could tell, the servers that answered for Facebook's names had ceased to exist. It was, Cloudflare's engineers wrote, as if someone had pulled the cables from Facebook's data centres all at once, and disconnected them from the Internet.

You have everything you need, from the last four evenings, to understand what had happened. Let me tell it in order, because it is the finest single case study I know, and every Part of tonight will return to it.

Facebook runs a private network of its own — a backbone, the trade calls it — joining its data centres around the world to one another, and to the places where it meets the rest of the Internet. Like any network, the backbone needs routine maintenance, and that Monday a command was issued whose purpose, in the words of Facebook's own published account, was to assess the availability of global backbone capacity. That is to say: to check something. The command did not check. It took down every connection in the backbone, all at once, disconnecting every one of Facebook's data centres from every other. There was a tool whose whole purpose was to audit commands of that kind and stop the dangerous ones. It had a bug, and the command went through.

That alone would have been a very bad morning: data centres cut off from one another, services unable to reach their databases. But what turned a bad morning into one of the great outages in the history of the Internet was a piece of careful, sensible design.

Facebook's DNS servers — the authoritative servers for facebook dot com and its relations, in the language of last week — were built, quite rightly, to watch their own health. Each of them was announced to the Internet by BGP, the protocol of the second evening, so that the world could route questions to it. And each was designed so that if it found it could no longer reach Facebook's data centres, it would take that as a sign that something was wrong with its own corner of the network, and withdraw its own announcement — step out of the line, as it were, so that questions would be routed to a healthier colleague somewhere else. It is exactly the behaviour you would want, on any ordinary day, from one sick server among many.

That Monday, every one of them found it could no longer reach the data centres, because none of them could. And so every one of them, behaving exactly as designed, withdrew itself from the Internet. Facebook's account puts it in a sentence that I think deserves a place in every textbook: the end result was that our DNS servers became unreachable even though they were still operational.

Now follow the consequences outward, because they are the consequences of the fourth evening. Every resolver in the world that was asked about facebook dot com, and did not have a fresh answer in its cache, went to ask Facebook's authoritative servers — and found no route to them. So it answered its users with a failure. And the users' applications did what applications do, which is to say, they did not accept the failure. They retried. The apps on a vast number of telephones, finding that Facebook could not be found, asked again, and again, and again — many of them, Cloudflare observed, quite aggressively — so that at one point their public resolver was handling thirty times its usual volume of questions. Retries without restraint, of exactly the kind I warned you of in the third evening, were hammering the world's resolvers on behalf of a company that was not there.

And inside Facebook, the situation was worse, because of a dependency loop that you must learn to look for in every system you build. The tools that Facebook's engineers would ordinarily use to diagnose and repair a problem of this kind ran over Facebook's own network, and found Facebook's own machines by Facebook's own names. With the network down and the names gone, the tools were down too. The remote access that engineers would use to reach the backbone routers — including the separate, emergency access kept for exactly such a day — was down as well. So, as Facebook's account says, they sent engineers to the data centres in person. And the data centres, it adds drily, are hard to get into, and once you are inside, the hardware and routers are designed to be difficult to modify even when you have physical access to them. A reporter for the New York Times, quoting somebody inside the company, reported that morning that employees could not get into buildings because their badges were not working. Every protection the company had built against intruders now stood, with perfect impartiality, between the company and its own machines.

The engineers got in, in the end, and restored the backbone. And then they faced one last problem, which belongs to the fourth Part of tonight. When a service as large as Facebook's comes back all at once, everything that was waiting rushes in together. Individual data centres had, during the outage, been drawing tens of megawatts less power than usual, and suddenly reversing a dip of that size could put everything from the electrical systems to the caches at risk. So they brought the traffic back gradually — which they had, as it happens, rehearsed, in exercises they called storm drills, though never, they admitted, for the loss of the entire backbone. Shortly after nine o'clock in the evening, Greenwich time — a little after five in the afternoon in Toronto — the routes reappeared, the names resolved, and Facebook returned to the Internet. It had been gone for about six hours.

Facebook's engineers ended their account with a sentence I commend to you. The security that had slowed them down, they wrote, had been built against malicious activity; and the outage had been caused not by malicious activity, but by an error of our own making.

So let me count the mechanisms, because counting them is the point. A private backbone, and BGP announcements: the second evening. A health check that withdraws a route: a mechanism of tonight's second Part, the front door. Authoritative DNS servers, resolvers, and caches: the fourth evening. Retries, multiplied, without backoff: the third. A whole company's tools depending on the network they were needed to repair. And at the end, the power of a machine room, measured in tens of megawatts. Every piece was sensible. Every piece behaved as designed. And together, for six hours, they removed one of the largest companies on Earth from the world.

That is tonight's territory: the machine rooms, where the services you build actually live, and the networks inside them, and the reasons that everything in them is slower, and stranger, and more fragile than the diagrams suggest. Tonight's question is the last of the five: why is it slow — and what do the great machine rooms of the world do about it?

Here is the plan. We begin with the oldest war in web engineering, which is a war on round trips: the story of HTTP, from a single line typed at CERN to the protocol that replaced TCP. Then the front door: what actually stands between your users and your servers. Then inside the building: the fabric that joins tens of thousands of machines, and the arithmetic that makes the slowest of them everybody's problem. Then the most expensive network in the world, which happens to be the one your own trade runs on: the one that trains the models. Then, as I promised you in the first evening, we shall walk the whole journey of a single page once more, at full resolution, and find every promise where it is made. And at the very end, I shall keep my oldest promise, and tell you whether the sentence that closes these evenings is right.

Part One — A War on Round Trips

In the first evening I asked you to form one habit above all others, and I hope it has taken: counting round trips. A page in London, ninety milliseconds away, cost three of them before its first byte arrived — one for TCP's introductions, one for TLS's paint, one for the request itself — and every one of those round trips was the speed of light in glass, which no amount of money can buy down. The history of the web's own protocol, HTTP, is very largely the history of a war against those round trips. It has been fought for thirty years, and it is worth following, because every one of its battles left a ritual behind in your code.

It began with a proposal. In March nineteen eighty-nine, a British engineer at CERN, the particle physics laboratory near Geneva, wrote a document called Information Management: A Proposal, describing a system of linked documents that could be read across a network. His name was Tim Berners-Lee. His supervisor, Mike Sendall, wrote on the cover three words that have since become famous: vague but exciting. By December nineteen ninety, Berners-Lee had the first web server, the first web browser, and the first web site running, on a NeXT computer; in August nineteen ninety-one he announced the project to the world in a newsgroup post.

The protocol his browser spoke to his server was as simple as a protocol can be. The browser opened a TCP connection, and sent one line: the word GET, followed by the address of the document it wanted. The server sent back the document, and closed the connection. That was all. No headers; no status codes; no way even to say not found. It is known today as HTTP zero-point-nine — a version number given to it long afterwards, in retrospect, to distinguish it from what came next — and one author calls it, fairly, the one-line protocol.

What came next, by the middle of the nineties, was everything the one line lacked. The request grew headers: lines of extra information, in which the browser said what it was, what it would accept, and which site it wanted — that last one being what lets a single server answer for many names. And the response grew a status code: a three-digit number on its first line saying how things had gone. Two hundred, for all is well. Three-oh-one, for it has moved; look over there. Five hundred, for the server has had an accident. And four-oh-four, for not found — the only number from any protocol in this course to have passed into ordinary speech. That version was written down in nineteen ninety-six as HTTP one-point-oh, in a document that, as one author observes, was never formally a standard at all: it merely described what everybody was already doing.

Now count. One document, one connection. And every connection, you will remember, begins with a handshake — a full round trip before the first byte — and then goes through slow start, sending a little and waiting to see if the network can bear more. For a page of one document, that was tolerable. But pages soon acquired pictures, and each picture was a document, and each document needed its own connection, and each connection paid the handshake and the slow start afresh, and then threw away everything it had learned about the network at the very moment it had learned it.

The first remedy was the obvious one, and you have been performing it as a ritual for your whole career. The version of HTTP standardised at the end of the nineteen-nineties, HTTP one-point-one, made connections persistent by default: after a response, the connection stays open, and the next request goes down the same one. The trade calls it keep-alive. It is the third evening's lesson — keep your connections, and use them again — written into the web's protocol: pay the handshake once, and let slow start's window stay open.

But one connection carrying requests one after another is still slow, because each request must wait for the previous response before it can be sent: one round trip per request. So HTTP one-point-one offered a second remedy, called pipelining: send several requests down the connection at once, without waiting, and let the responses come back in turn. It was a sound idea on paper, and it failed in practice, for a reason you now have the vocabulary to state. The responses had to come back in the order the requests were sent. If the first response was slow — a large picture, say, or a page the server had to think about — every response behind it waited, finished or not. That is head-of-line blocking, which you met in the third evening, now at the level of HTTP; and on top of it, a great many proxies and servers in the middle of the Internet mishandled pipelined requests in creative ways. The browsers quietly left pipelining switched off, and in time removed it: Chrome around two thousand and fourteen, citing, in the words of its developers, known crashing bugs and known front-of-queue blocking issues; Firefox in two thousand and seventeen.

So the browsers fell back on brute force. If one connection could carry only one request at a time, they would open several. The specification of nineteen ninety-nine said, rather sternly, that a single user's client should not maintain more than two connections with any server. The browsers settled on six. To this day, in the source code of Chrome, there is a line that sets the limit of six connections to a single host, with a comment above it that I treasure, explaining that a larger number may cause many problems, such as home routers blocking the connections — followed, in the original, by an exclamation mark and a question mark, twice over. Even the engineers of the world's most popular browser, it seems, are not entirely sure what the boxes in your hall will do.

Six connections per host bred a whole generation of rituals, and if you have ever built a web front end, you performed them. If six connections per name was the limit, then a site that served its pictures from several names — images one dot example dot com, images two dot example dot com, and so on — got six connections for each. The trade called that domain sharding. If every picture cost a request, then many small pictures could be combined into one large one, and the browser told to show only the relevant corner of it at a time; that was called spriting. If every script cost a request, all the scripts could be concatenated into one enormous file. These were not superstitions. They were sound engineering against a protocol that charged a round trip for every file. And every one of them was made obsolete by what came next.

In November two thousand and nine, two engineers at Google announced an experimental protocol called SPDY — said speedy — which they described as a way to a web twice as fast; in their tests, the most popular sites loaded up to fifty-five percent faster. SPDY's central idea was multiplexing. Instead of six connections, each carrying one request at a time, use one connection, and carry many requests and responses on it at once, interleaved, each in its own numbered stream, chopped into small frames, so that no response need wait for another to finish. Let me walk it with a cast of two: a large picture, and a small script, requested at the same moment on the same connection. The picture is stream one; the script is stream three. The server sends a frame of the picture, labelled stream one; then a frame of the script, labelled stream three; then another frame of the picture; then the last frame of the script, marked end of stream three. The browser, reading the labels, sorts the frames into their two piles. The script is complete, and runs, long before the picture has finished arriving. Nobody waited for anybody. The picture and the script travelled side by side, frame by frame, down one connection, through one handshake, under one congestion window. SPDY became the basis of HTTP two, standardised in May two thousand and fifteen. It also compressed the headers that every request repeats — the browser's description of itself, the cookies, and so on — which had grown absurdly large; though its first method of compression turned out, rather embarrassingly, to leak secrets, and had to be replaced by a new scheme designed specifically for the purpose.

With HTTP two, the rituals of six connections became worse than useless: sharding a site across several names now forced the browser to open several connections, each paying its own handshake and its own slow start, which was precisely what multiplexing had been invented to avoid. The guidance of the day said, in so many words, to remove the old workarounds. Some sites are still sharded, years later, by teams who have never been told why they are.

And yet HTTP two could not win the war on its own, because it ran on TCP, and TCP had a head-of-line problem of its own, one layer down. You heard it at the end of the third evening, and here it bites. TCP promises to deliver bytes in order. If one packet of the one connection is lost, TCP holds back everything behind it until the lost packet has been resent — including frames of perfectly unrelated streams, which arrived intact and are sitting in the receiver's buffer, waiting. On a clean network, HTTP two is a triumph. On a lossy one — a mobile telephone on a train, say — a single lost packet stalls every request on the connection at once, and HTTP two can do worse than the six independent connections it replaced.

The only cure was to replace TCP. And here we arrive at the question I promised you in the third evening: why would anybody rebuild TCP from scratch? The answer is that the head-of-line problem is woven into TCP's single promise of one ordered stream, and cannot be fixed without changing what TCP is. And TCP itself could not be changed, for a reason that is the subject of the last Part tonight: the boxes in the middle of the Internet had learned what TCP looks like, and would not let anything else through.

So, beginning in two thousand and twelve, a team at Google led by an engineer named Jim Roskind built a new transport protocol, and called it QUIC — said quick. It was standardised in May two thousand and twenty-one, and HTTP three, which runs on it, a year later. Let me give you its four great moves, one at a time.

The first move is where it lives. QUIC does not ask the Internet to carry a new kind of packet, because the boxes in the middle would drop it. It travels inside UDP — the third evening's thin postcard service, which nearly every box already lets through — and builds everything TCP did, and more, inside the postcards, in the programs at the two ends. Reliability, ordering, congestion control: all manufactured at the ends, exactly as the first evening said they must be, but now in software that can be updated with a browser rather than with the operating system of every machine on Earth.

The second move is the handshake. QUIC merges TCP's introductions and TLS's paint into a single exchange, so that the first round trip both opens the conversation and agrees the keys. Count again: where TCP and TLS one-point-three cost two round trips before the request, QUIC costs one; and for a returning visitor, with the zero R-T-T of the last evening, none at all.

The third move is the cure for head-of-line blocking. A QUIC connection carries many independent streams, and it orders bytes only within each stream. A lost packet holds up only the stream it belonged to; every other stream carries on. The train passes through its tunnel, one picture arrives a moment late, and nothing else waits for it.

And the fourth move is the one you feel most, without knowing it. A TCP connection, you will remember, is defined by its five-tuple: both addresses, both ports, and the protocol. Change any of them, and the conversation is a different conversation. So when your telephone walks out of the house and moves from your Wi-Fi to the mobile network, its address changes, and every TCP connection it had is dead, and must be built again from the handshake up. A QUIC connection is identified instead by a connection ID, a number chosen by the two ends and carried in every packet. Its specification says that the primary function of a connection ID is to ensure that changes in addressing do not cause packets for a connection to be delivered to the wrong place. Walk out of the house, change networks, and the conversation simply carries on.

QUIC is not yet universal. By the measurements of one large network, about a fifth of the web requests it saw last year travelled over HTTP three, half over HTTP two, and the rest — getting on for a third — still over HTTP one-point-one; something like four web sites in ten now offer HTTP three. One reason is a ritual of a different kind: a great many corporate firewalls block UDP to the web's port, four forty-three, out of a habit formed when UDP there meant nothing good. When they do, the browser notices, and silently falls back to TCP. Your laptop at the office may never have spoken QUIC in its life.

One last battle of the war, because it is fought inside your own services. In February two thousand and fifteen, Google released as open source a framework for calls between services that it had been using internally, under another name, for over a decade. It is called gRPC, and its maintainers have kept up a running joke that the g stands for something different in every release — good, green, gentle, gregarious, and so on. gRPC runs on HTTP two, and so it inherits multiplexing: a client opens one long-lived connection to a server and sends all its calls down it, as many streams at once. That is wonderfully efficient, and it collides head-on with the way a great deal of load balancing works.

You will remember from the first evening the difference between a load balancer that works at layer four, seeing only addresses and ports, and one that works at layer seven, seeing the requests themselves. A layer-four balancer balances connections: each new connection goes to a server, chosen fairly. But a gRPC client opens one connection, and keeps it for hours. So every call that client ever makes goes to the same one server, however many servers stand behind the balancer, and the fair distribution of connections becomes a thoroughly unfair distribution of work. Kubernetes's own blog put it with some sympathy in two thousand and eighteen: many new gRPC users are surprised to find that Kubernetes's default load balancing often doesn't work out of the box with gRPC. Hence the rituals you may have met: balancing on the client side, with the client keeping connections to every server and spreading its own calls; or a layer-seven proxy in the middle, which understands the streams and balances each call. The cure for one round-trip problem created a load-balancing problem, and it was solved, as everything in this Part was solved, by moving the intelligence to where it could see what it needed to see.

So: HTTP began with one line and one connection per document. Keep-alive kept connections, and paid the handshake once. Pipelining failed on head-of-line blocking, and six connections per host bred sharding and sprites. HTTP two multiplexed many streams on one connection and made those rituals obsolete, but could not escape TCP's own head-of-line blocking. QUIC escaped it by rebuilding transport inside UDP, at the ends: one round trip for introductions and paint together, independent streams, and connections that survive a change of address. And gRPC's single long connection turned a layer-four balancer's fairness into unfairness, and sent the balancing up to layer seven, or out to the client.

Part Two — The Front Door

In the first evening I told you that the server at the far end of your request is not a server. It is a front door. Let me now take you through it, from the outside in, because a large service's front door is not one thing but a series of doors, each choosing among the doors behind it, and each one is a place where your request can be steered, or delayed, or lost.

The first door is the directory itself. When you ask for the address of a large site, the authoritative servers for its name do not give everybody the same answer. They look at where the question came from — which is to say, at the address of the resolver that asked — and give each part of the world the address of the site's nearest or least busy entrance. A resolver in Toronto is told one address; a resolver in Frankfurt, another. And because those answers carry short times to live, as you heard last week, the site can change its mind within a minute or two: drain a region for maintenance, or steer around a fire. The directory, which we met as a phone book, is also the first load balancer, and the coarsest.

The second door is anycast, which you met in the second evening: one address, announced into the Internet's routing from many places at once, so that every user's packets go to whichever place is nearest in routing terms. The large content networks use it; so do the public resolvers, and the root servers. And it has a property you have now seen in action. An anycast site that falls ill can simply withdraw its announcement, and the world's routers, within seconds, send its users to the next nearest. That is how anycast heals itself. And it is exactly the mechanism by which Facebook's DNS servers, each one detecting a sick network and politely stepping aside, removed every one of themselves from the world at once. A health check with a global reach is a fine servant and a terrible master.

Behind those two doors stands the machinery that most engineers mean when they say load balancer, and it comes in the two kinds that the first evening distinguished. Let me take the layer-four kind first, because its problem is the more beautiful.

A layer-four load balancer sees only the envelope: the five-tuple of addresses, ports, and protocol. It cannot read the request inside. Its job is to choose, for each new connection, one of the servers behind it, and then to send every later packet of that connection to the same server — because a server that received the middle of a conversation it never started would answer with a reset. Now, a large site does not have one balancer. It has many, because no single machine could carry the traffic. The routers in front of them spread the packets across the balancers using a technique we shall meet properly in the next Part: each router hashes the five-tuple of each packet, and uses the result to pick a balancer, so that all the packets of one connection go the same way.

And there is the difficulty. Suppose one of the balancers fails. The routers notice, and begin sending its share of the packets to the others. So a balancer suddenly receives packets for connections it has never seen — connections that the dead balancer had assigned to particular servers. How can it possibly know which server each one belongs to? It must reach exactly the same decision that the dead balancer reached, without ever having spoken to it.

The answer is a family of techniques called consistent hashing, and it is worth having in your hands, because it is everywhere: in load balancers, in caches, in databases that spread their data across many machines. Let me begin with the naive method, and why it fails. The naive method is to take a hash of the connection's five-tuple — a large, scrambled number — divide it by the number of servers, and use the remainder to pick one. Ten servers: the remainder runs from zero to nine. That works perfectly, and every balancer computing it reaches the same answer. Until the number of servers changes. Add an eleventh server, and now you divide by eleven, and the remainder for almost every connection changes. Nearly every connection, and nearly every cached item, suddenly belongs somewhere else. Ten in eleven of them, as it happens, must move, because one server joined.

Consistent hashing fixes that with a picture, and the picture is a clock face. Imagine a ring, like the dial of a clock, with a very great many positions around it. First move: place each server on the ring, at a position given by hashing its name. Second move: place each connection on the ring, at a position given by hashing its five-tuple. Third move: each connection belongs to the first server you come to, going clockwise from where the connection sits. That is the whole rule.

Now watch what happens when things change. Remove a server: only the connections that belonged to it move, each to the next server clockwise. Everybody else stays exactly where they were. Add a server: it takes over only the connections lying between it and its counter-clockwise neighbour, and nobody else is disturbed. Instead of nearly everything moving, almost nothing does. And because every balancer uses the same ring, every balancer reaches the same answer independently. The trick is stated in one sentence: hash the servers and the work onto the same ring, and let each piece of work go to the next server along.

The idea was published in nineteen ninety-seven by a group at MIT that included a professor of applied mathematics, Tom Leighton, and his graduate student, Danny Lewin, who were trying to solve a problem that Tim Berners-Lee himself had posed at MIT two years earlier: how to stop the web from collapsing under crowds of people all wanting the same page at the same moment. The following year, the two of them, with two partners, founded a company to put the idea to work, and gave it a Hawaiian word for clever: Akamai. Their company built the first great content delivery network, of which more in a moment. Danny Lewin did not live to see what it became. He was killed on the eleventh of September, two thousand and one, aboard the first of the aircraft hijacked that morning. He was thirty-one.

Google's layer-four balancer, which it called Maglev, has carried its traffic since two thousand and eight, and its published design is a fine example of the whole arrangement: routers spreading packets across many Maglev machines by hashing, each Maglev using a variety of consistent hashing to pick the server, so that every packet of a connection reaches the same server no matter which Maglev received it, with a table of recent connections kept as a safety net. A single Maglev machine, the paper reported, could fill a ten-gigabit link with small packets. Facebook later published one of its own, called Katran, which does the same job inside the Linux kernel, at the very first moment a packet arrives, before the rest of the operating system's network code has even seen it.

Behind the layer-four balancers, in most large systems, stands a layer-seven load balancer: a proxy that ends the TCP connection, and the TLS connection too, reads each HTTP request in the clear, and decides what to do with it. It can send requests for one path to one service and another path to another. It can balance each request separately, which is what gRPC needed in the last Part. It can retry a failed request on another server, enforce timeouts, and add headers. And one of those headers explains a ritual. Because the layer-seven balancer opens its own connection to your server, your server sees the balancer's address as the source of every request, not the user's. So the balancer writes the user's real address into a header, conventionally called X-Forwarded-For, and every web framework you have used has a setting about whether to believe it. Believe it only from your own balancers; anybody else may write anything they like in it.

Every balancer, of either kind, needs to know which servers behind it are well. So it asks them, every few seconds, with a health check: a small request — often to an address with a name like healthz — that a healthy server answers at once. A server that fails a few checks in a row is taken out of rotation; when it recovers, it is put back. That is simple, and it hides two traps, one on each side. A health check that checks too little — is the process running? — will keep sending traffic to a server that is running but cannot actually serve. And a health check that checks too much — can I reach the database, and the cache, and the other service? — will, on the day the database stumbles, fail on every server at once, and the balancer will take every server out of rotation and serve nobody at all. A health check should ask whether this server can do its job, and no more. Facebook's DNS servers, asking whether the whole network was reachable, were the second trap at planetary scale.

The opposite of a health check is draining: taking a server out of rotation deliberately, for a deployment, without hurting anyone. The balancer stops sending it new connections, and waits for the existing ones to finish, before the server is stopped. And here is a ritual you may have copied into a Kubernetes configuration without being able to defend it: a pre-stop hook that does nothing but sleep for a few seconds. Here is why. When Kubernetes decides to stop a pod, two things happen at once. The pod is told to shut down. And, separately, the news is sent to every node's proxy and every load balancer that the pod should no longer receive traffic. That news takes a few seconds to arrive everywhere. If the pod shuts down at once, then for those few seconds, requests are still being sent to a door that has just closed — and your users see a burst of connection refusals, and the balancer's errors, at every single deployment. The sleep holds the door open while the world learns to stop knocking. It looks like superstition. It is a timing diagram.

Now, encryption. In the last evening I left a question for tonight: where exactly, in a large system, does the encryption begin and end? The answer is: in several places, and the places are a matter of policy. Your browser's TLS conversation usually ends at the first layer-seven proxy it meets — at the edge of a content network, or at the company's own front door — because that proxy must read the request in order to route it. The trade calls that TLS termination. Behind it, the request travels on inside the company's own network. For many years, the inside was simply trusted, and requests crossed it unencrypted, on the theory that the walls of the castle were enough. After the revelations of two thousand and thirteen about government surveillance of the links between data centres, the large companies began encrypting their internal traffic as well — and the most thorough way to do it is the one you heard about last week: mutual TLS between every pair of services, each with its own certificate.

Doing that by hand in every service would be miserable, so the trade invented a piece of machinery to do it for you. Beside every running service it places a small proxy — the trade calls it a sidecar — through which every call in or out of the service passes. The service speaks plainly to its own sidecar, on the same machine; the sidecar speaks mutual TLS to the sidecar of the service at the other end, presents its certificate, checks the other's, and also retries, times out, balances at layer seven, and counts everything. A fleet of such proxies, managed together, is called a service mesh. When your platform team announced that every call between services would henceforth be encrypted and observable without any change to your code, this is what they meant: a proxy in every pod, holding the papers, checking everybody's at every door.

The last door is the outermost, and it answers the deepest problem of the whole course: the speed of light, which no protocol can improve. If London is ninety milliseconds away, then three round trips cost a quarter of a second, and nothing can be done about it — unless the page is not in London. A content delivery network keeps copies of a site's content in buildings close to its users, so that your laptop in Toronto talks to a machine in Toronto, a few milliseconds away, and the round trips cost almost nothing. Akamai, the company built on consistent hashing, now reports more than four thousand three hundred points of presence in over a hundred and thirty countries. Netflix went further and built its own: since two thousand and eleven it has placed its own servers inside the networks of Internet providers — some eighteen thousand of them, by its own count, in some six thousand locations — so that when you watch a film, it very likely comes from a box in your provider's building, a few miles away. The speed of light is not negotiable. The distance is.

And the content network gives you one last ritual, which is the fourth evening's lesson in a new coat. A content network caches what it is given, for as long as the site's headers say it may, and — as with DNS — nobody can make every cache in the world forget sooner. So when you change a file, you cannot reliably make the world fetch the new one. What the trade does instead is give the new file a new name: the fingerprint of its contents, written into its file name, so that app dot js becomes app dot followed by a string of letters and digits dot js, and the page that refers to it is changed to refer to the new name. The old file stays cached forever, and nobody asks for it again. The trade calls it cache busting, and it is the only reliable way to make a cache forget: never ask it to.

So: a large service's front door is a series of doors. The directory hands each region of the world a different address, and can change its mind within a time to live. Anycast sends each user to the nearest site, and heals by withdrawal — which can also, as Facebook found, be how a service vanishes. Layer-four balancers choose a server per connection, and many of them agree, without speaking, by consistent hashing: servers and work on one ring, each piece of work to the next server along. Layer-seven balancers end TCP and TLS, read the request, balance each one, and tell your server the user's address in a header. Health checks must ask about this server only; draining, and the sleep before stopping, let a server leave without slamming the door. TLS ends at the first proxy that must read the request, and service meshes re-encrypt everything inside, with a sidecar in every pod. And content networks answer the speed of light by moving the content close to you — and are made to forget, when they must, by giving the content a new name.

Part Three — Inside the Building

Now let us go through the last door and into the building itself, where your services live. And the first thing to understand about the network inside a modern data centre is that it does not exist chiefly to serve you. When your one request arrives, the machine that receives it asks a dozen services for a dozen things, and each of those asks others, and the answers flow back and are assembled — so that for every byte that goes out of the building to a user, a great many bytes travel between machines inside it. The trade distinguishes the two directions with a pair of compass points: traffic in and out of the building is north-south, and traffic between machines inside it is east-west. In a modern data centre, east-west is by far the greater. The network inside the building is built for machines talking to machines.

How do you join tens of thousands of machines so that any of them can talk to any other at full speed? The answer, remarkably, was published in nineteen fifty-three, for telephones. An engineer at Bell Labs named Charles Clos was working on telephone exchanges, which have exactly this problem: a great many lines coming in, a great many going out, and the need to connect any idle caller to any idle callee without anybody hearing a busy signal merely because the exchange's internal wiring was full. The obvious solution, one enormous switch connecting every input directly to every output, was impossibly expensive at large sizes. Clos showed that you could build the same thing out of stages of small switches, cross-connected, using far fewer parts in total — and still guarantee that an idle input could always reach an idle output.

Half a century later, when the large companies began building data centres too big for any single switch, they rediscovered Clos. In two thousand and eight, a group at the University of California, San Diego, showed that the cheap, ordinary switches of the day, arranged in the Clos fashion, could give every one of some twenty-seven thousand servers its full bandwidth to every other. And the arrangement you will find in nearly every large data centre today is a descendant of that idea, called leaf and spine.

Let me describe it, because it is simple and it matters. In every rack of servers sits a switch, at the top of the rack, called a leaf; every server in the rack is plugged into it. Above the leaves stands a row of larger switches called spines. And here is the rule that makes it work: every leaf is connected to every spine. So to get from a server in one rack to a server in any other rack, a packet goes up to its own leaf, across to any spine, and down to the other rack's leaf. Leaf, spine, leaf — never more than that. And because every leaf connects to every spine, there is not one path between any two racks, but as many paths as there are spines, all of the same length. Need more capacity? Add spines. Need more servers? Add leaves. The largest fabrics add further stages above the spines, in the same pattern, and Google's, called Jupiter, has grown in under a decade from one-point-three petabits a second of capacity to more than thirteen — enough, the company observed, to carry a video call for every one of the eight billion people on Earth at the same time.

Many equal paths between every pair of racks raises the question of which one each packet should take. The answer has a name you will meet in every network diagram of a data centre: equal-cost multipath, written ECMP. Each switch, faced with several equally good next hops, hashes the packet's five-tuple and uses the result to choose. And it hashes the five-tuple, rather than, say, choosing at random for each packet, for a reason the third evening gave you. If the packets of a single TCP connection were scattered across different paths, they would arrive out of order — and you will remember that a receiver seeing packets out of order sends duplicate acknowledgements, and three duplicates make the sender assume a loss, resend, and halve its window. Spraying one connection's packets across many paths would make TCP believe the network was losing packets when it was merely being clever. So every packet of a connection follows the same path, and the many connections are spread across the many paths.

That works beautifully on average, and has a known weakness, which you should recognise from the gRPC story. Hashing spreads connections evenly, not bytes. Most connections are small, but a few are enormous — the trade calls them elephants, and the small ones mice — and if two elephants happen to hash onto the same link, that link is overloaded while another sits idle, for as long as the two elephants live. A paper of two thousand and ten put it in a sentence: per-flow static hashing can cause substantial bandwidth losses due to long-term collisions. Much of the cleverness in modern data-centre networks goes into spreading elephants better than a hash can.

Now the pathology that is particular to machine rooms, and it has a name of its own. Suppose one machine asks forty others for a piece of data each, all at once, and all forty answer at once. Forty responses converge on one switch port, the one leading to the asking machine, at the same instant, and that port's queue is small, because switches in data centres have small buffers. The queue overflows, and some of the responses are dropped. And now recall what TCP does about a loss it cannot repair quickly: it waits for its retransmission timer. On Linux, you will remember, that timer is never shorter than a fifth of a second — two hundred milliseconds. In a building where a round trip takes a few dozen microseconds, a two-hundred-millisecond wait is an eternity — thousands of round trips spent doing nothing. Researchers who studied it in two thousand and nine found throughput collapsing by up to ninety percent. The trade calls it incast: many senders casting their answers in towards one receiver at once. The cures came from the third evening's vocabulary. One was to make the retransmission timer far shorter inside data centres. Another, from Microsoft and Stanford in two thousand and ten, was a variant of TCP called Data Center TCP, which uses the explicit congestion marks of the third evening — the tap on the shoulder rather than the broken crockery — to have senders slow down in proportion to how crowded the queue is getting, before anything is lost. Its authors, who had begun by measuring a production cluster of six thousand servers, reported that it gave the same or better throughput than ordinary TCP while using ninety percent less buffer space.

And now the most important idea of this Part, which the first evening promised you would have a name of its own. Let me give you the arithmetic first, in the words of the paper that named it.

In two thousand and thirteen, two of Google's most senior engineers, Jeff Dean and Luiz Barroso, published a short article called The Tail at Scale. It begins with a hypothetical server that typically responds in ten milliseconds, but one time in a hundred takes a full second. One time in a hundred: that is its ninety-ninth percentile, its tail. For a single request to that server, the tail hardly matters; ninety-nine requests in a hundred are fast. But now, they wrote, suppose that a user's request must collect responses from a hundred such servers in parallel — which is an ordinary day's work for a large service. Then sixty-three percent of user requests will take more than one second.

Let me walk that, because it is the single most important piece of arithmetic in the machine room. The user's request is answered only when all hundred servers have answered. So the request is fast only if every one of the hundred is fast. Each is fast ninety-nine times in a hundred. The chance that all hundred are fast is ninety-nine hundredths, multiplied by itself a hundred times — which comes to only about thirty-seven in a hundred. So in the other sixty-three, at least one of the hundred is slow, and the whole request waits for it. A slowness that afflicts one request in a hundred at each server afflicts nearly two requests in three for the user. That is tail latency, and the fact the paper teaches is this: in a system that fans out, the rare slowness of the parts becomes the common slowness of the whole.

I can put it in domestic terms, because next week, I understand, Miss Xenia turns five, and there is to be a party. Suppose twenty small guests are invited, and each of them, on a typical Saturday, is punctual nineteen times in twenty. Each guest, taken alone, is reliably on time. But the cake cannot be cut until the last guest has arrived, and the chance that all twenty are punctual on the same afternoon is a little over a third. So nearly two parties in three wait for somebody — and it is never the same somebody. The party starts when the last guest arrives. So does every response that fans out to a hundred servers. I leave it to the household to decide whether to cut the cake at the ninety-fifth percentile.

That is, in fact, exactly what the paper recommends, in a technique called a hedged request. Send the request to one server. If no answer has come back by the time an answer would normally have arrived — say, by the ninety-fifth percentile of the usual response time — send the same request to a second server that holds the same data, and take whichever answer comes first, cancelling the other. The cost is small, because the second request is sent only in the slow cases: deferring it to the ninety-fifth percentile, they found, limits the extra load to about five percent. And the benefit is enormous. In one of their benchmarks, hedging after just ten milliseconds cut the slowest one-in-a-thousand latency, for a job fetching a thousand values from a hundred servers, from one point eight seconds to seventy-four milliseconds, at a cost of two percent more requests. You cannot make every server fast. But you can stop waiting for the slow one.

And here I pay a small debt from the second evening, where I told you that your browser's habit of trying IP version six and version four nearly at once — the trade calls it Happy Eyeballs — was a small instance of a large idea. This is the idea. Your browser starts a connection over version six; if it has not succeeded within a fraction of a second, it starts one over version four as well, and keeps whichever finishes first. It is a hedged request, made by your laptop, on your behalf, dozens of times a day.

Now a word about the networks you actually see, which are not the physical ones. When you create a virtual private cloud, the second evening's slash sixteen with its subnets, you are not given any switches. You are given an illusion, and it is maintained by encapsulation — the first evening's envelopes within envelopes, once more. Every packet your virtual machine sends is caught by software on the physical host, wrapped in a further envelope addressed to the physical host where the destination machine really lives, carried across the real fabric, and unwrapped at the far end, so that the virtual machine inside never knows. Amazon introduced its virtual private clouds in August two thousand and nine, and Google published the design of its own, which it calls Andromeda, in two thousand and eighteen. A common standard form of the wrapping is called VXLAN, and it gives you two rituals at once. The first is that it adds about fifty bytes of envelope to every packet; and so, on a physical network whose packets can be fifteen hundred bytes, the packets inside a VXLAN overlay must be fifty bytes smaller — which is why you will find the number fourteen hundred and fifty in so many container network configurations, the second evening's packet-size problem returning in a new coat. The second is a port number: VXLAN was assigned port forty-seven eighty-nine, but Linux uses eighty-four seventy-two by default, and the kernel's source explains why with a candour I find refreshing — for compatibility with early adopters. If you have ever opened a firewall for port eighty-four seventy-two to make a Kubernetes cluster work, now you know whose early adoption you were honouring.

And so to Kubernetes, the machine room inside the machine room, where three debts come due at once. Kubernetes's own model is admirably simple: every pod gets its own address, valid across the whole cluster, and every pod can reach every other pod directly, without any address translation. But the Services you define — a stable name and address standing in front of a changing set of pods — are not machines at all. A Service's address is a virtual one, which no machine holds. On every node, a component called kube-proxy writes rules into the Linux kernel saying: a packet addressed to this Service's address should be rewritten to the address of one of these pods instead. That rewriting is address translation — the third evening's NAT, in the middle of your cluster — and, like every NAT, it must remember each conversation it has rewritten, so that the replies can be rewritten back. It remembers them in the connection-tracking table, conntrack, which I promised in the third evening we would meet again in the machine room. Here it is. The table has a maximum size, and on a busy node, with many short connections, it can fill; and when it fills, the kernel drops new connections and writes, in its log, a line saying nf conntrack: table full, dropping packet — the N-F and the conntrack joined, in the original, by an underscore. In recent kernels the prefix is printed twice, as a small bonus. If that line has ever appeared on one of your nodes during a traffic spike, you were watching a NAT table in the middle of your cluster run out of room.

The third debt is a famous delay, which the fourth evening left for tonight. For years, Kubernetes users reported that DNS lookups from their pods would, every so often, take exactly five seconds. Not roughly five; five. In two thousand and eighteen an engineer named Martynas Pumputis traced it to the conntrack table again. The resolver libraries in most Linux programs ask for a name's IP version four and version six addresses at the same moment, sending two UDP packets from the same socket, at the same instant. And in the kernel's connection tracking, two packets of that kind racing through at the same moment could collide, and one of them would be dropped. The resolver, getting only one answer, waited for the other — for its default timeout, which is five seconds — and then asked again. Exactly five seconds: the timeout of a library written long before anybody imagined two packets racing through a NAT table in a cluster of containers. The cures, which you may have seen as rituals, were an option telling the resolver to send its two questions from separate sockets, with the unlovely name single-request-reopen; a small DNS cache on every node, so that the questions never pass through the NAT at all; and, in time, fixes in the kernel itself.

So: inside the building, traffic between machines far exceeds traffic to users, and the fabric that carries it is a Clos network of nineteen fifty-three, rebuilt as leaf and spine: every leaf joined to every spine, so that any two racks are leaf, spine, leaf apart, by many equal paths. ECMP spreads connections over those paths by hashing, keeping each connection on one path so TCP does not mistake reordering for loss, and elephants sometimes collide. Incast drowns a receiver's port in simultaneous answers, and TCP's two-hundred-millisecond timer turns it into a collapse, cured by shorter timers and Data Center TCP's gentle marks. Fan-out turns rare slowness into common slowness — sixty-three requests in a hundred, from a slowness of one in a hundred at each of a hundred servers — and the hedged request, sent only when the first is late, stops the whole from waiting for its slowest part. Virtual networks are envelopes within envelopes, fifty bytes thicker. And in Kubernetes, every Service is a NAT, with a conntrack table that can fill, and a race that once cost every unlucky DNS lookup exactly five seconds.

Part Four — The Expensive Network

There is one network in the machine room that is unlike all the others, and it happens to be the one your own trade runs on. It joins the thousands of accelerators that train a large model, and, port for port, it is the most expensive network ever built. Everything you have learned in this course applies to it — and in places, it applies in reverse.

Let me begin with why the network matters so much to training, because it is a matter of where the network sits in the loop. In the simplest arrangement, called data parallelism, every accelerator — let me say GPU, as the trade does — holds a copy of the model, and each takes a different slice of the training batch. Each GPU runs its slice forward and backward, and arrives at its own gradient: its own opinion of how every weight in the model should change. And now nobody may take the next step until all of those opinions have been averaged, so that every copy of the model changes in exactly the same way. Every GPU must contribute its gradient, and every GPU must receive the average. That operation is called an all-reduce — reduce, because many gradients are combined into one; all, because everybody gets the result — and it happens at every single step of training, many thousands of times an hour. The network is not off to one side, carrying the occasional message. It is in the inner loop.

The obvious way to do an all-reduce is to send every GPU's gradient to one place, add them up there, and send the average back to everybody. And you can see at once, from everything you know, what is wrong with it. The one place has one link. Double the number of GPUs, and twice as much traffic must squeeze through that one link in each direction. The more machines you add, the slower each step goes. A thousand GPUs all shouting their gradients at one machine is the incast of the last Part, by appointment.

The way the trade actually does it is one of the prettiest algorithms in this course, and it is called the ring all-reduce. Let me walk it with a small cast: four GPUs, sitting in a ring, so that each one sends only to its neighbour on the right and receives only from its neighbour on the left. And let every GPU cut its gradient into four equal pieces, which I shall call chunks one, two, three, and four.

The walk has two phases. The first phase is called reduce-scatter, and its aim is that each GPU should end up holding the complete sum of one chunk.

First step. Each GPU sends one of its chunks to its right-hand neighbour — each GPU a different chunk — and each neighbour adds the chunk it receives to its own copy of the same chunk. Now four chunks each hold the sum of two GPUs' contributions.

Second step. Each GPU passes on the partial sum it has just made, to its right, and again the receiver adds its own copy. Now each GPU holds one chunk carrying the sum of three GPUs' contributions.

Third step. Once more, pass and add. And now, after three steps — one fewer than the number of GPUs — each GPU holds one chunk that contains the contributions of all four. A different complete chunk on each GPU.

The second phase is called all-gather, and it is simpler. The completed chunks travel round the ring, each GPU passing on what it has just received, with no adding now, only copying. After three more steps, every GPU has all four completed chunks: the whole averaged gradient. Done.

Now count, because the counting is the beauty of it. Each GPU sent one chunk at each of six steps — three in each phase. Each chunk is a quarter of the gradient. So each GPU sent one and a half gradients' worth of data in total. In general, with any number of GPUs in the ring, each one sends just under twice its gradient — and that figure does not grow as you add GPUs. Four GPUs, a thousand GPUs: each sends just under twice its gradient, to one neighbour, over one link. The load on every link stays the same however large the ring grows, which is why the method is called, by the people who proved it in two thousand and nine, bandwidth-optimal. The ring's weakness is the number of steps, which does grow — twice the number of GPUs, near enough — and each step pays a small delay; so at very large sizes the libraries switch to trees, and to hierarchies of rings within rings. But the core idea is the ring, and it is worth saying once more in a sentence: everyone passes one piece to the right, adding as they go, until each holds one finished piece; then everyone passes the finished pieces round, until all hold all.

The idea came from the world of supercomputers, and was brought to deep learning in two thousand and seventeen by a team at Baidu, and popularised that same year by Uber's open-source library, Horovod. The library your training jobs actually call today is NVIDIA's, called NCCL — said nickel — for the NVIDIA Collective Communications Library, which chooses for you between rings and trees and much else, according to the machines it finds itself on.

And the two halves of the ring have lives of their own, which you will meet in your configuration files. The more economical forms of data parallelism — the trade calls them sharded, and you will know them by names like ZeRO, or fully sharded data parallel — do not give every GPU a whole copy of the model at all. Each GPU holds only a slice of the weights. Before computing, the GPUs perform an all-gather, assembling for a moment the weights they need. After computing, they perform a reduce-scatter, so that each ends up with the summed gradient for its own slice only. The two phases of the ring, taken apart and used separately. And other forms of parallelism talk more often still. When a single layer of the model is split across several GPUs — the trade calls it tensor parallelism — those GPUs must exchange partial results inside every layer, many times in every step; which is why that kind of parallelism is kept inside a single server, on its fastest links, and never asked to cross the slower network between servers. The rule is one you have heard before, in another form: put the most talkative conversations on the shortest wires.

And where it finds itself is a hierarchy of networks, each faster and more expensive than the last, as you go inward.

Inside a single server, the GPUs are joined by NVIDIA's own links, called NVLink, which bypass the ordinary connections of a computer altogether. The first generation, in two thousand and sixteen, gave a GPU a hundred and sixty gigabytes a second; the generation of two thousand and twenty-four gives each GPU one point eight terabytes a second. And the largest systems now stretch that inner network beyond the single server to a whole rack: seventy-two GPUs joined as if they were one machine, by a spine of copper cable that NVIDIA describes as five thousand cables, over two miles in length, in a single rack. You may remember from the second evening that the first Ethernet carried about three million bits a second, on a cable you could see. That spine carries something like a hundred and thirty terabytes a second, in aggregate, and you could not see it for the copper.

Between servers, the GPUs reach one another through network cards of four hundred gigabits a second each — usually one card per GPU — over one of two kinds of network. The first is InfiniBand, a network designed in the late nineteen-nineties as a fast interconnect for servers, adopted above all by supercomputers, and now made chiefly by Mellanox, a company that NVIDIA agreed to buy in two thousand and nineteen, for nearly seven billion dollars, for exactly this reason. And the second is Ethernet — but Ethernet asked to behave like InfiniBand, which is where this Part's story turns.

Both kinds carry a technique called RDMA, for remote direct memory access. In ordinary networking, as you know it, a message from one machine to another passes through the operating system on each side: through the sockets, through TCP, through the kernel's buffers, with the processors on both sides doing the work. RDMA cuts all of that out. The network card on one machine writes the data directly into the memory of the other machine — even straight into a GPU's memory — without either operating system's TCP, and without the remote machine's processor lifting a finger. A message crosses in a microsecond or two, and the processors are left free for other work. TCP's manners, all of them, are skipped.

But RDMA was designed for InfiniBand, and InfiniBand makes a promise that the Internet never made: it does not drop packets. Inside an InfiniBand network, a sender may transmit only when the receiver has said it has room — every link is turn-taking, in the third evening's terms, enforced in hardware. And so RDMA's own recovery from loss is crude, because it was never expected to be needed; a single lost packet can cost a great deal. So when the trade decided to run RDMA over ordinary Ethernet — an arrangement called RoCE, said rocky, for RDMA over Converged Ethernet — it had to make Ethernet lossless too. The mechanism is called priority flow control: when a switch's queue for a given class of traffic fills up, it sends a pause message back to whatever is sending into it, saying stop, for a moment. Nothing is dropped. The sender simply waits.

Now think about what that does, because it is the third evening turned inside out. On the Internet, a crowded router drops packets, and the senders, hearing of the loss, back off — restraint, by voluntary arithmetic, at the ends. On a lossless network, a crowded switch pauses the switch behind it; that switch's queue fills, and it pauses the switch behind it; and the congestion spreads backwards through the fabric, hop by hop, freezing traffic that had nothing to do with the original crowd. Head-of-line blocking, again, now at the scale of a building. Engineers at Microsoft, describing their experience of running RDMA over Ethernet at scale, reported pause storms, livelocks, and a deadlock caused by the pauses themselves — and added, in their paper's abstract, two words in parentheses that I have always admired: yes, it happened! The cure, once again, was restraint at the ends: a congestion-control scheme that uses the third evening's explicit congestion marks to have senders slow down before the pauses are needed. You cannot escape the third evening. You can only choose which version of it to have.

You can see all of this in the clusters your own industry has published. In March two thousand and twenty-four, Meta described two training clusters of twenty-four thousand five hundred and seventy-six GPUs each, built identically except for the network: one with InfiniBand, and one with RDMA over Ethernet, each giving every GPU four hundred gigabits a second. It trained Llama three on the Ethernet one. And the paper describing that model's training describes the network as — you will recognise the words — a three-layer Clos network: pods of some three thousand GPUs each with full bandwidth among themselves, joined by a layer above with only one-seventh of that bandwidth. So the training system was arranged so that the most talkative kinds of parallelism stayed inside a pod, and only the more restrained kinds crossed the thin layer between pods. Charles Clos's telephone exchange of nineteen fifty-three, carrying the gradients of a language model.

There is one more trick in the wiring that you should know by name, because it explains a diagram you may have seen and not understood. A training server typically holds eight GPUs, each with its own network card. In what the trade calls a rail-optimised network, card one of every server is plugged into one leaf switch, card two of every server into a second leaf, and so on — eight leaves, eight rails. Why? Because in the collective operations of training, GPU number one in one server talks mostly to GPU number one in the others, and number two to number two; and on its own rail, each GPU is just one hop from all of its partners. The wiring follows the conversation.

And now the tail returns, as I promised it would, in its most extreme form. Synchronous training is the purest fan-out there is: at every step, every GPU must hear from every other before anyone may move. The step takes as long as the slowest GPU, on the slowest link, at that moment. The paper on Llama three's training says it plainly: even a single straggler can slow down thousands of other GPUs, often appearing as functioning but slow communications. Over a fifty-four-day stretch of training, it recorded four hundred and sixty-six interruptions to the job, nearly four in five of them caused by hardware. A slowness that affects one machine in a thousand affects every step. It is the party that cannot start until the last guest arrives, with sixteen thousand guests, every few seconds, for months.

And here is a detail from that same paper which I cannot resist, because it closes a circle with the opening of tonight. Because all the GPUs compute in lockstep and then all fall quiet together to communicate, the power drawn by the whole data centre swings up and down with every step — by, the paper says, tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid. Tens of megawatts: the same figure that Facebook's engineers had to bring back so carefully at the end of their six hours. The network does not merely sit in the machine room. Its rhythm can be read on the electricity grid.

The trade is not standing still. In two thousand and twenty-three a group of companies — chipmakers, switch makers, and the largest users, Meta and Microsoft among them — formed the Ultra Ethernet Consortium, to design a successor to RDMA over Ethernet built from the start for this kind of traffic: able to spray the packets of a single conversation across many paths at once, rather than hashing each conversation onto one, and to recover from loss gracefully rather than forbidding it. Its first specification appeared in June of last year. Multiple paths, graceful loss, restraint at the ends. It is, if I may say so, the second and third evenings, rebuilt at four hundred gigabits.

And the rituals of this Part are written in the launch scripts of your training jobs, as environment variables whose names begin with the letters N-C-C-L. The most useful of them, written with underscores between the words, is NCCL debug, set to info: it makes the library tell you, at startup, which network it has found and which transport it has chosen. Read that output once, on every new cluster. The failure it catches is silent and expensive: a library that cannot find the RDMA cards will not stop with an error. It will fall back, politely, to ordinary TCP sockets — and your job will run, and every step will be many times slower, and nothing will tell you why. Other variables of the same family tell the library which network interfaces and which cards to use, and they exist because a server with eight cards on eight rails, and a ninth for everything else, gives a library a great many wrong ways to wire itself.

So: data-parallel training puts an all-reduce in the inner loop of every step. The ring all-reduce passes pieces to the right, adding as it goes, until each GPU holds one finished piece, then passes the finished pieces round — and each GPU sends just under twice its gradient, however large the ring. Inside a server, NVLink; across a rack, a spine of copper; between servers, four hundred gigabits a GPU, over InfiniBand or RDMA over Ethernet. RDMA skips TCP entirely and writes straight into another machine's memory, but it needs a network that does not drop — so Ethernet is made lossless with pauses, and the pauses spread, and the cure is restraint at the ends once more. The fabrics are Clos networks with rails that follow the conversation. And the tail rules everything: every step waits for the slowest GPU on the slowest link, with a rhythm you can read on the power grid.

Part Five — A Page, at Full Resolution

In the first evening, I walked you through the journey of a single page at low resolution, the way one first looks at the map of a city, and promised that in the fifth evening we would walk it again, street by street. Here we are. The scene is the same. You are at your desk in Toronto; your laptop is on the household Wi-Fi; you type the name of a web site, and press the return key. But this time you know what everything is called, and at every step I shall ask three questions: what is happening; which promise is being made, and by whom; and how it breaks, and how you would know.

The cast, before anything moves. Your laptop. The box in the hall. Your provider's network. A resolver, and the directory's servers behind it. The content network's machine in Toronto — the edge. The site's own front door, in a data centre far away. And the services behind the front door. Eight parties, and between them, about two dozen moves.

Before the return key. When your laptop joined the household network — perhaps this morning, perhaps a week ago — it took part in the four-message exchange of the second evening, DHCP: discover, offer, request, acknowledge. It was given an address for itself, the address of the box in the hall, and the address of a resolver. And it learned, by asking aloud on the local network, the hardware address that belongs to the box's IP address — ARP, in the second evening's terms — so that it can address frames to the box over the radio. Promise made: none; this is introductions. How it breaks: if DHCP fails, your laptop gives itself one of those one-six-nine-dot-two-five-four addresses of the second evening, and nothing beyond the hall can be reached. If you ever see such an address on your own machine, you know at once which floor is on fire: the ground floor, before anything else has begun.

The name. Your browser asks your laptop's stub resolver for the site's address. The stub sends the question to the resolver — perhaps by plain UDP to port fifty-three, perhaps, these days, encrypted inside HTTPS, as last week described. The resolver very likely knows the answer already, from its cache. If not, it walks the tree: the root points to com, com points to the site's authoritative servers. And here is the first thing you could not have seen at low resolution. The site's own servers do not answer with an address. They answer with a CNAME — an alias — pointing to a name belonging to the site's content network. So the resolver asks again, of the content network's own authoritative servers, and they answer with an address chosen for the region the question came from — an address, very likely, announced by anycast from dozens of places, of which the nearest to you is in Toronto. The answer carries a short time to live, perhaps a minute, so that the content network can steer you elsewhere at short notice.

Your browser, in fact, asked for two kinds of address at once — the quad-A for IP version six and the A for version four — and will try version six first, starting version four a moment later if six is slow: the hedge called Happy Eyeballs. Promise made: the directory's — this name is at this address — which, unless the site has signed its zone, rests only on the circumstantial evidence of the fourth evening. How it breaks: a resolver that cannot answer gives you could not resolve host; a stale cache sends you to yesterday's address; and the tool for the floor is called dig — its name is short for domain information groper, and I promise you I did not make that up — which asks any resolver you like any question you like, and shows you every record, every alias, and every time to live in the answer.

The hall. Your browser now sends its first packet to the edge's address. Your laptop sees that the address is not on the household network, and so wraps the packet in a frame addressed to the box in the hall, and sends it over the radio, taking its turn by the listen-before-you-speak manners of the second evening. The box receives it and does the third evening's sleight of hand: it rewrites the packet's private source address and port into its own public address and a port of its own choosing, and writes a line in its connection-tracking table so that it can translate the replies. If your provider also translates, at a carrier-grade NAT, the same thing happens again in a large building somewhere in the city. Promise made: none, except the box's private promise to remember you. How it breaks: the box forgets an idle conversation, as the third evening warned, and a reply arrives to which it has no line.

The road. Your provider's routers take the packet. Each looks up the destination in its table, finds the longest matching prefix, and sends it on, decrementing the time-to-live counter of the second evening as it goes. The route to the edge's anycast prefix was learned, by BGP, from the content network itself — very likely at an exchange point in a building on Front Street, where your provider and the content network meet and hand traffic across, or even from a server the content network has placed inside your provider's own building. A few hops; a few milliseconds. Promise made: none. This is the network of the first evening, which promises nothing, and delivers anyway, almost always. How it breaks: a route withdrawn or hijacked, a link congested, a packet too large for some link on the path. And the tools for this floor are the oldest in the trade: ping, written in a single evening in nineteen eighty-three, which asks are you there, and how long did that take; and traceroute, Van Jacobson's of nineteen eighty-eight, which uses the time-to-live counter to make each router along the path reveal itself in turn. A descendant called mtr — it began as Matt's traceroute, and became, under its second maintainer, my traceroute — runs the two together continuously, and shows you, hop by hop, where packets are being lost.

The introductions and the paint. At the edge, your browser and the content network's machine now open a conversation. If both speak QUIC, and no firewall in your office blocks UDP to port four forty-three, they do it in a single round trip: QUIC's introductions and TLS one-point-three's paint, together. Otherwise, a TCP handshake first — may I; you may, and may I; you may — and then TLS on top. Your browser's hello carries its key share and, in the clear, the name of the site, unless the site and your browser both speak Encrypted Client Hello. The edge answers with its own key share, and then, encrypted, the site's certificate, and a signature over the whole handshake. Notice what that usually means: the content network holds a certificate, and the private key, for the site's name. The site has delegated not only its traffic but its identity to the content network — another branch of the fourth evening's tree, grafted on by contract. Your browser checks the chain up to a root in its store, checks the name, checks the dates. Promises made: from here on, confidentiality, integrity, and authentication, all manufactured at the two ends. How it breaks: refused, timed out, or reset, in the catechism of the third evening; or a page of certificate warnings, for which the tool is openssl's own client, which connects, performs the handshake, and prints the whole certificate chain the server sent, so that you can see with your own eyes the missing intermediate or the wrong name.

The request. Your browser sends its request — GET, a path, a version, the site's name — on a stream of its QUIC connection. And the edge looks in its cache. If it has the page, and the page's headers say it is still fresh, the answer goes straight back: a few milliseconds away, from a building in your own city. The speed of light, answered by moving the content. That is how most of the bytes you receive every day reach you.

But suppose it is a page that cannot be cached — your own account, say. Then the edge must ask the site itself, and here is the second thing invisible at low resolution. The edge does not open a fresh connection to the site's data centre for your request, paying a handshake and a slow start across the continent. It has, very likely, been holding a warm connection open to the site's front door for hours, with its congestion window wide open, and it sends your request down that. Every lesson of the third evening — keep your connections, and use them again — is being applied on your behalf, between two machines you will never see.

The front door. The request arrives at the site's data centre: at an address that may itself be anycast, behind routers that spread packets across a row of layer-four balancers by hashing the five-tuple; and each balancer, by consistent hashing, sends the connection to the same layer-seven proxy that any of its colleagues would have chosen. The proxy ends the TLS, reads the request, writes the client's address into a header, picks a healthy server by path and by load, and forwards the request, on a connection of its own, perhaps re-encrypted with the proxy's own certificate. Promises made: the proxy's promise to choose a healthy server, which rests on its health checks. How it breaks: and here the front door has its own family of errors, which you should be able to read at a glance. Five-oh-two, bad gateway: the proxy reached a server, and the server's connection failed or was reset under it — very often the idle-timeout trap of the third evening, where the server was the less patient of the two. Five-oh-three, service unavailable: the proxy has no healthy server to send you to — look at the health checks, and at what they are checking. Five-oh-four, gateway timeout: the proxy sent your request, and the server did not answer in time — the server is slow, or is waiting on something that is.

The building. The server that receives your request is one of many copies of a web service, in a pod, on a node, behind a Kubernetes Service. To build your page, it asks a dozen other services for a dozen things: your account, your recent orders, the prices, the recommendations. Each of those calls leaves through the pod's sidecar, which encrypts it with mutual TLS, presents the service's certificate, and checks the other's. Each call goes to a Service's virtual address, which the node's kube-proxy rules translate to a real pod, writing a line in the conntrack table as they go. Each crosses the leaf-and-spine fabric — leaf, spine, leaf — on a path chosen by ECMP's hash. Some of those services call others; one fans out to a hundred shards of a database at once. And the page cannot be finished until the slowest of all those calls has answered. Somewhere in that fan-out, one call in a hundred is slow, and the tail at scale ensures that your request is very probably waiting for it — unless the service hedges, sending a second copy of a slow call to another replica after a few milliseconds and taking whichever answers first. Promises made: dozens of small ones, each manufactured at the two ends of each internal call. How it breaks: in every way this course has described, now multiplied by fan-out — which is why a single slow dependency, deep in the building, appears to you as a slow page.

The way home. The answer travels back the way it came. The service returns it to the proxy; the proxy to the edge, down the warm connection; the edge sends it to your laptop on the stream your browser opened, governed by its congestion window, growing as the acknowledgements return. At the box in the hall, each packet of the answer is translated back, by the line written on the way out, to your laptop's private address and port. And your browser reads the page, finds that it refers to forty other files — scripts, styles, pictures — and asks for them all at once, as forty streams on the one connection, most of them answered from the edge's cache in Toronto.

Now count, as I asked you to in the first evening. If the site had to be reached in London, ninety milliseconds away, the first byte would cost three round trips of ninety: more than a quarter of a second of nothing but light in glass. At the edge in Toronto, five milliseconds away, QUIC's single round trip for the introductions and the paint, and one more for the request, cost about ten milliseconds. Everything else in the journey — the warm connection across the continent, the front door, the fan-out in the building — is the site's business, and the site's engineers will have spent years making it fast. The difference between a quarter of a second and a hundredth of a second is the entire history of web engineering since the year two thousand, and you have now heard every chapter of it.

And notice, above all, where each promise was made. The network itself — the box in the hall, the provider's routers, the exchange on Front Street — promised nothing at all. It made a best effort, and delivered. Reliability was manufactured at the two ends of each conversation, by QUIC or by TCP. The name was vouched for by a tree of delegated authority. The identity and the secrecy were manufactured at the ends by TLS, under a certificate checked against a root in your browser. The speed was manufactured by moving the content to the edge, by keeping connections warm, and by hedging inside the building. Not one guarantee lived in the middle. The first evening's thesis, at full resolution: the core promises nothing; everything is built at the edges.

So let me give you, before we leave the journey, the catechism by floors that the first evening promised — what to ask, in order, when something is wrong, and which instrument to reach for. Does my machine have an address, and not a one-six-nine-dot-two-five-four one? If not, the ground floor. Can the name be resolved? Ask with dig. Can the address be reached at all? Ask with ping, though many machines decline to answer it; and if not, find where the road ends, with traceroute, or mtr. Can a connection be made to the port? Refused means nobody is listening; timed out means silence, and a firewall or a broken path. Does the handshake of TLS succeed? Ask with openssl's client, and read the chain. Does HTTP succeed? Ask with curl, which began life in nineteen ninety-six as a little program called HttpGet, and which, given its verbose flag, will tell you everything it did — the name it resolved, the address it connected to, the handshake, the certificate, the request it sent and the headers it received — in the order it did them, which is the order of this Part. And if the status code is a five-oh-two, a five-oh-three, or a five-oh-four, it came from the front door, and tells you which part of the front door to look at. And when all of those still leave you puzzled, there is the last resort, which is also the final authority: the packet capture, with tcpdump, which Van Jacobson wrote too, with two colleagues at the laboratory on the hill in Berkeley, or with Wireshark, which shows you, in colour, every word that was actually said. The error tells you the floor. The capture tells you the truth.

Part Six — Conservative in What You Send

And so, at last, to the sentence.

I have closed every one of these evenings with it. In the first, I borrowed it without saying whose it was. In the third, I told you: it is Jon Postel's, from the specification of TCP, September nineteen eighty-one, where it reads be conservative in what you do, be liberal in what you accept from others. He had written it in other words the year before, in the specification of IP: an implementation, he wrote then, should be conservative in its sending behavior, and liberal in its receiving behavior. And in nineteen eighty-nine the great document that gathered up the requirements for every Internet host turned it round into the form I have been using — be liberal in what you accept, and conservative in what you send — and added a warning of its own, which I think the best sentence ever written in a standards document: sooner or later a packet will come in with that particular combination of errors and attributes, and unless the software is prepared, chaos can ensue.

I promised you a verdict on whether the principle was right. Let me first make the case for it, then the case against, and then I shall tell you what I think.

The case for it is the Internet itself. In nineteen eighty-one there were dozens of independent implementations of TCP, written from a prose specification by people at different institutions, on different machines, most of whom had never met. Every one of those implementations had bugs, and read some sentence of the specification a little differently from the others. If each had insisted on perfection from the rest, none of them could have talked to any other, and the network would have been a collection of private conversations among identical machines. Instead, each was strict with itself and forgiving of others, and so they all interoperated, bugs and all, and the network grew. Postel's principle is the reason the early Internet worked at all. It is a principle of courtesy among strangers, and the Internet was a network of strangers.

Now the case against. It has two counts, and the first is called ossification — from the Latin for bone — and it is the turning of a protocol's flexible joints into bone.

Here is how it happens. Suppose an implementation sends something slightly wrong — a field filled in carelessly, a rule half-followed — and every receiver, being liberal, accepts it. Then the sender's authors never learn of the mistake, and never fix it. More implementations are written; they copy the habit, because it works. And now any new implementation that is strict, and rejects the mistake, will find that it cannot talk to half the Internet. So it, too, must accept the mistake. The error has become part of the protocol — unwritten, but compulsory. The document I shall come to in a moment puts it precisely: a flaw can become entrenched as a de facto standard. Any implementation of the protocol is required to replicate the aberrant behavior, or it is not interoperable.

You have a monument to this on every web page you have ever written, and it is the first line. In the nineteen-nineties, the browsers were magnificently liberal in what they accepted: they would display almost any mess of markup that anybody cared to send them, guessing at what was meant. So authors sent messes, because messes worked; and every browser's particular guesses became something that real pages depended on. When the browsers later wished to follow the standards properly, they found they could not, without breaking a great part of the web. So they kept both behaviours: a strict one, and an old, forgiving one, that faithfully reproduces the bugs of the nineties, and which the trade calls, with admirable frankness, quirks mode. And the switch between them is that short line at the top of every page — doctype html — which you have typed, or copied, a thousand times. It means, in effect: I promise to behave; please do not imitate the browsers of nineteen ninety-seven. The modern standard for the web's own language goes further still, and specifies, step by step, exactly how a browser must interpret every conceivable broken page, so that every browser will guess the same way. The liberality became so entrenched that the errors themselves had to be standardised.

And there is a second, worse form of ossification, which you have watched happen across five evenings without my naming it, because the evidence has been accumulating in the boxes in the middle. The firewalls, the inspection appliances, the load balancers, the boxes in a great many halls: their makers did not, on the whole, follow Postel's principle at all. They were liberal in the wrong direction. They were built to recognise what the traffic of their day looked like, to let through what they had seen before — errors and all — and to reject, or mangle, anything they had not seen before, however correct. And so every new idea in the Internet's protocols has had to fight its way past boxes that had learned what the old ideas looked like.

Let me present the evidence, because you have heard every piece of it, and it is worth laying out side by side. In the third evening: explicit congestion notification — the tap on the shoulder instead of the broken crockery — standardised in two thousand and one, and kept switched off for years because old boxes in the middle mishandled the marks. In the fourth evening: TLS one-point-three, the newest version of the Internet's most important security protocol, travelling the world in its predecessor's clothes, claiming to be TLS one-point-two in its first message and sending meaningless messages to look like an old conversation, because, in the words of its own specification, a significant number of middleboxes misbehave when they see the real thing. And tonight: QUIC, which could not be built as a new protocol beside TCP, because the boxes would not carry one, and so had to hide inside UDP — and which, having got through, encrypts nearly every part of its own headers, even the numbers on its own packets — for a purpose the IETF's own guidance names outright: encrypting the headers, it says, helps avoid transport protocol ossification by middleboxes. What a box cannot read, it cannot learn to depend on.

And the most eloquent exhibit of all is a document published in May two thousand and twenty-three, defining QUIC version two. Here is how it describes itself: QUIC version two is identical to QUIC version one except for some trivial details. Its purpose is to combat various ossification vectors. A new version of a protocol, whose only purpose is to prove that a new version is possible — because if version one were the only version ever seen, the boxes would learn that QUIC means version one, and the door to version three would quietly close.

The trade's most ingenious answer to ossification has a name you may have seen in a packet capture and not understood: GREASE. It was described in two thousand and twenty by an engineer at Google, David Benjamin, and the letters stand — with some effort, as is usual — for Generate Random Extensions And Sustain Extensibility. The idea is this. A protocol like TLS has places where future versions may add new values — new options, new extensions. The danger is that, because no new values have appeared for years, careless implementations will have learned to reject any value they do not recognise, and the day a real new value is needed, it will break the Internet. So the browsers began, deliberately, to include meaningless values in those places in every handshake — values reserved for the purpose, which mean nothing at all — so that any implementation that chokes on an unknown value chokes now, visibly, while the mistake is rare and can still be fixed. Its specification says it plainly: peers that do not tolerate unknown values will fail to interoperate, revealing the mistake before it is widespread. You keep the joints from turning to bone by moving them. Every handshake your browser makes is doing a little physiotherapy on the Internet.

The second count against the principle is security, and I shall be brief with it. A program that is liberal in what it accepts accepts a great many things its authors never imagined, and two programs that are liberal in slightly different ways can be made to disagree about what a message means — one reading it one way, one another — and the space between their two readings is where a great deal of mischief has lived. In two thousand and eleven Eric Allman, who wrote one of the oldest mail programs on the Internet, published a reconsideration of the principle that named exactly this: two problem areas, he wrote, are affected by it — orderly interoperability and security. Others went further, and argued that the principle's careless application had done much to make the Internet insecure.

And in June two thousand and twenty-three the Internet Architecture Board — the IETF's own guardians of architecture — published the trade's formal reconsideration, as RFC ninety-four thirteen. It is titled, soberly, Maintaining Robust Protocols. But its first draft, years before, carried a more candid title — The Harmful Consequences of Postel's Maxim — and a file name that ended, I am delighted to report, with the words postel was wrong. Its argument is the one I have been laying out. Time and experience show, it says, that negative consequences to interoperability accumulate over time if implementations silently accept faulty input. Its prescription is the opposite of liberality: protocols need active maintenance, by people who watch how they are actually used; and implementations should be strict, and should fail loudly. Choosing to generate fatal errors for unspecified conditions instead of attempting error recovery, it says, can ensure that faults receive attention.

So. The verdict. You will have gathered by now that I have one.

For protocols, sir, Postel was right for his time, and is wrong for ours. In a young network of strangers, forgiveness was the price of having a network at all, and it was worth paying. But liberality is a loan against the future, and the interest is ossification, compounded silently, year after year, until the day you need to change something and find that you cannot. A mature protocol should be conservative in what it sends — always, and without exception; that half of the sentence has never been wrong. And it should be liberal in what it accepts only where its specification deliberately leaves room, never silently, and never in a way that lets a mistake go unnoticed: strict at the edges, loud about errors, and kept limber by exercise, as GREASE keeps it. Be conservative in what you send; be exact in what you accept; and keep the joints moving.

But you will have noticed that the sentence at the end of these evenings is not addressed to a protocol. It is addressed to you. And for gentlemen, the maxim stands, entirely. Be exacting in your own conduct, and generous in your reading of other people's. Send nothing you would not stand behind; and when a colleague, or a stranger, sends you something a little malformed, assume the meaning that was intended, and carry on. That is not a protocol, and nothing in it ossifies. It is simply good manners. And it was, by every account, Jon Postel's own — the man who, told that he was the god of the Internet, replied that there was no god of the Internet; only a lot of people cooperating to do things together.

And that, sir, brings me to the thesis of this whole course, which I gave you in the first evening, and which the last five evenings were written to prove. The course is called Best Effort, after the only promise the Internet's core has ever made: that it will try. It does not promise to deliver; it does not promise order, or speed, or secrecy, or identity. And every one of those things that you rely on was built, instead, at the edges — reliability by TCP and QUIC at the two ends of a conversation, restraint by the voluntary arithmetic of every sender, names by a tree of delegated authority, identity and secrecy by keys held only at the ends, speed by moving the ends closer together. Wherever the middle has tried to do more — to translate, to inspect, to be clever — it has made the Internet harder to change, and has had to be worked around, encrypted against, and disguised from. The end-to-end argument of the first evening is not one principle among many. It is the reason the thing works. A network that promises nothing, built by people who promise a great deal to one another at its edges: that is the Internet. And on a good day, it is not a bad description of a household, either.

What the Boy Should Have Been Told

So, sir: the fifth lecture, as the boy should have heard it. And then, because it is the last, the whole course, in the form I would have you keep.

Tonight's question was why it is slow, and the first answer is the round trip. The history of HTTP is a war on round trips. One line and one connection per document gave way to connections kept alive, so that the handshake was paid once. Pipelining failed on head-of-line blocking, and six connections per host bred sharding and sprites. HTTP two carried many streams on one connection, and made those rituals obsolete — but could not escape TCP's own head-of-line blocking, one layer down. So QUIC rebuilt the transport inside UDP, at the ends, where the boxes in the middle could not interfere: one round trip for the introductions and the paint together, independent streams so that one loss stalls only its own, and connections that survive a change of address. And gRPC's single long connection taught everybody that a layer-four balancer balances connections, not work.

The second answer is the front door, which is a series of doors. The directory sends each region of the world to a different address; anycast sends each user to the nearest site, and heals by withdrawal — which is also how Facebook's DNS servers, each one politely stepping aside, removed a company from the Internet for six hours. Layer-four balancers agree among themselves, without speaking, by consistent hashing: the servers and the work placed on one ring, each piece of work going to the next server along, so that when a server comes or goes, almost nothing moves. Layer-seven proxies end the TLS, read the request, balance each one, and tell your server who the user really was. Health checks must ask only whether this server can do its own job; the sleep before a pod stops holds the door open while the world learns to stop knocking. Service meshes put a proxy beside every service to carry its papers. And content networks answer the speed of light by moving the content close to you — and are made to forget, when they must, by giving the content a new name.

The third answer is the tail, and it is the most important idea of the evening, so here it is for the third time. When one request fans out to many servers and must wait for all of them, the rare slowness of each becomes the common slowness of the whole: a server slow one time in a hundred, asked a hundred times at once, makes sixty-three requests in a hundred slow. The party starts when the last guest arrives. The cure is not to make every server fast, which cannot be done, but to stop waiting for the slow one: a hedged request, sent to a second replica only when the first is late, at a cost of a few percent more work. Inside the building, the fabric is a Clos network of nineteen fifty-three, rebuilt as leaf and spine, with every leaf joined to every spine, and connections spread across the many equal paths by hashing, each kept to one path so that TCP does not mistake reordering for loss. Incast drowns a port in simultaneous answers; Data Center TCP's gentle marks relieve it. Your virtual networks are envelopes within envelopes, fifty bytes thicker. And every Kubernetes Service is a NAT, with a conntrack table that can fill, and a race that once cost unlucky DNS lookups exactly five seconds.

And in your own trade's machine room, the network sits in the inner loop of training, because every step ends in an all-reduce. The ring all-reduce, for the third time: everyone passes one piece to the right, adding as they go, until each holds one finished piece; then everyone passes the finished pieces round, until all hold all — and each GPU sends just under twice its gradient, however large the ring. NVLink inside the server, a spine of copper across the rack, four hundred gigabits a GPU between servers, and RDMA writing straight into memory, skipping TCP entirely — on a network forbidden to drop, whose pauses spread, and whose cure is restraint at the ends once again. Every step waits for the slowest GPU on the slowest link. And if your training job is mysteriously slow on a new cluster, set NCCL debug to info, and read what it says it found.

Then we walked the journey at full resolution, and found that the network itself made no promise at any point: reliability at the two ends, by QUIC or TCP; names by a delegated tree; identity and secrecy by keys at the ends; speed by moving the ends closer together. And we weighed the sentence at the end of these evenings, and found it a fine rule for strangers founding a network, a poor one for a mature protocol — where liberality, unmaintained, turns into ossification, as ECN, TLS one-point-three's disguise, QUIC's hiding place, and the first line of every web page all testify — and an excellent one, still, for a gentleman.

And now the whole course. You asked me, at the beginning, a question I have kept before me every evening since: if this is all I ever learn about networking, what should it be? Here is my answer, in eight sentences, each of which you have now heard at least three times.

First. The network promises nothing. Its core makes a best effort to deliver your packets, and every guarantee you rely on — reliability, order, secrecy, identity, speed — is manufactured at the edges, by the two ends of the conversation. That is the end-to-end argument, and it is the reason the Internet could grow from four machines to tens of billions without being redesigned.

Second. Count round trips. Latency is physics — about five microseconds for every kilometre of glass — and bandwidth is merely money. A round trip is the one cost no budget can buy down, and every great performance technique in this course is a way of paying fewer of them: keep connections warm, merge the handshakes, move the content closer.

Third. Everything is envelopes within envelopes. Each layer adds its own envelope, its own address, and its own way of failing, and so the error tells you the floor: could not resolve, the directory; timed out, the road or a firewall; refused, nobody at the door; a certificate warning, the papers; a five-oh-two, the front door.

Fourth. Addresses are handed out in blocks, routes are chosen by the longest match, and the routes between the world's networks are agreements among strangers — believed, not proved — which is why a single announcement from Pakistan could take YouTube off the air.

Fifth. Reliability is made by numbering, acknowledging, keeping a copy, and resending. And the Internet survives at all because every sender, of its own accord, slows down when the room is crowded — halving its window at the first sign of loss, with no referee enforcing it. That voluntary restraint, shared by billions of machines that have never met, is the most surprising fact in this field.

Sixth. Names and trust both hang from a handful of keys, at the top of two trees of delegated authority — the root key in its safe, the root certificates in your browser — that are not checked against anything, but simply believed.

Seventh. At scale, the tail is everything. Fan-out turns rare slowness into common slowness, and retries without restraint turn a struggling service into a collapsed one. Hedge; set timeouts on everything; back off, with jitter; and keep a budget for your retries.

And eighth. The boxes in the middle forget, and they ossify. They forget your idle conversations, so keep your conversations alive on purpose. And they learn the shape of yesterday's traffic and reject tomorrow's, so keep your protocols exercised, encrypt what nobody in the middle needs to read, and put your guarantees at the ends, where nobody can take them away.

In the first evening I told you Richard Feynman's story of the cargo cults — the islanders who built runways and control towers out of bamboo, and waited for the aeroplanes. The boy in this story, you will remember, cargo-culted his way to staff engineer. I hope you will allow that the cargo, in his case, was real all along. The keepalives and the jittered backoff, the lowered time to live before a migration, the trailing dot, the sleep before a pod stops, the setting called no-delay, the long string of letters and digits in the name of a cached file: they worked, and they worked for reasons. A ritual is a practice whose reasons have been forgotten. You have them back now, every one — and with them, the far more useful knowledge of which rituals are no longer needed at all.

There is a great deal this course has not told you, and I would be failing in my duty if I pretended otherwise. A deeper season, should you ever want one, would have the ground to cover: wireless and cellular networks, where the medium itself misbehaves in ways a wire never does; the path a packet takes inside the Linux kernel, from the network card to your program's socket; the routing protocols in earnest, rather than in sketch; the defence of networks, as a discipline of its own; and the mathematics of queues, which governs everything from your checkout line to your tail latency. I promise none of it. But the door is there.

For reading, this last evening: Jeff Dean and Luiz Barroso's The Tail at Scale, which is short, and which will change how you read every latency graph you see. Google's papers on its Jupiter fabric, for the building. Meta's paper on running RDMA over Ethernet to train its models, for your own trade's machine room. And, should you wish to go back and take the course the boy never took, the standard textbook of the field, by Jim Kurose and Keith Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach — which, I am glad to say, starts at the top, with the applications you know, and works its way down, as we did.

That is the course, sir: five evenings, five questions. What a network is. Where a packet goes. Whether it arrived. Who is speaking, and who is listening. And why it is slow. If a colleague ever again tells you that a thing must be done because it has always been done, you will be able to tell them why — or, now and then, with great courtesy, that it need not be done at all.

And so, for the last time, the sentence — which you may now take as advice for a gentleman, though no longer for a protocol.

Be conservative in what you send, sir, and liberal in what you accept.
