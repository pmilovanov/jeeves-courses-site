Best Effort — A Remedial Course in Networking

The Sunday YouTube Went to Pakistan

Good day, sir.

In February of two thousand and eight, the government of Pakistan took exception to a video. Accounts differ as to which one — some say it repeated the Danish cartoons of the Prophet that had caused such grief two years before; some say it was connected with a Dutch politician's forthcoming film — but the objection was clear, and so was the remedy. The national telecommunications authority directed the country's internet providers to block YouTube. Within Pakistan, that is. Nobody in Islamabad proposed to inconvenience anybody else.

The largest of those providers, Pakistan Telecom, set about obeying, and it chose a method that was entirely reasonable on its face. Inside its own network, it would announce a route for YouTube's addresses that led nowhere — a dead end, a hole in the floor — so that any customer of Pakistan Telecom who tried to reach YouTube would have the packets quietly swallowed. Network engineers call such a route a black hole, and building one inside your own network is an ordinary thing to do.

At thirteen minutes to seven in the evening, Greenwich time, on Sunday the twenty-fourth of February, the announcement of that black hole escaped. It did not stay inside Pakistan Telecom's network. It went out to the company's provider, a large carrier in Hong Kong called PCCW Global, which ought to have checked whether its customer had any business announcing a route for YouTube's addresses, and did not. PCCW passed the announcement on to its neighbours, and they to theirs. Within minutes, routers all over the world — in London, in New York, in Toronto — had heard that the best way to reach YouTube was through Pakistan.

And they believed it. Every one of them. So when a teenager in Ohio, or a grandmother in London, or an engineer in Toronto, tried to watch a video that Sunday, the request set off for YouTube, was handed from router to router across the planet toward Pakistan, entered Pakistan Telecom's network, and fell into the hole. For the better part of two hours, most of the world could not reach YouTube at all.

YouTube's engineers noticed, as you may imagine, fairly quickly. And what they did next is the part of the story I find most instructive. They did not telephone Pakistan. They did not file a complaint with an international body, because there is no international body with the power to hear it. They fought back with the same weapon that had been used against them. About an hour and twenty minutes into the outage, YouTube began announcing the very same route Pakistan was announcing, so as to compete for the world's traffic on equal terms. Eleven minutes later, it went further, and announced routes that were narrower still — more specific, in the trade's language — than Pakistan's. Where those narrower routes reached, the world's traffic swung back to YouTube. And a little after nine o'clock, Greenwich time, PCCW at last cut off the announcements coming from its customer, and the episode was over: some two hours and a quarter, from start to finish.

Now, here is what I want you to notice. Nobody hacked anything. No password was stolen; no system was broken into; no malicious code was run. A provider in Pakistan made a configuration choice that was sensible inside its own walls, a provider in Hong Kong failed to apply a filter, and every other router on Earth did precisely, faithfully, and instantly what it was designed to do. The Internet was not attacked that Sunday. It worked exactly as built.

I should like you to hold on to that sentence, because it may be the most important in this evening. The telephone network of the nineteen-sixties could not have done such a thing. It was planned from the centre, by one company that knew where every line went, and a clerk in Islamabad could not have persuaded the exchanges of New York that Ohio's calls belonged in Pakistan. The Internet has no centre and no plan. Its routes are not designed by anybody. They are learned — by every network, from what its neighbours tell it — and passed on, and learned again: a planetary game of whispers in which some tens of thousands of independent organisations, most of which have never heard of one another, each decide for themselves which way to send every packet, on the strength of what they have been told. Most of the time, what they are told is true. That Sunday, one thing was not, and it travelled at very nearly the speed of light.

To understand how that can be — how a correctly functioning global network could hand YouTube's traffic to a black hole in Pakistan on the strength of one unchecked announcement — we need tonight's subject: how a packet finds its way. And the single rule at the bottom of it, the rule that every router on the planet obeys and that YouTube turned back to its own advantage, is the title of the evening. The longest match wins. By the end of tonight you will know exactly what that means, and you will be able to tell the story of that Sunday afternoon, step by step, better than most of the people who were awake for it.

Last week we asked what a network is, and the answer was a post that promises nothing: packets, each carrying its own address, forwarded hop by hop by routers that remember nothing, in envelopes within envelopes, at a speed the speed of light permits and no faster. Tonight's question is the second of our five: where does the packet go, and how does it find its way there? It is a question that has to be answered twice — once for the wire in your hall, where a packet need only reach the next machine, and once for the planet, where it must cross thousands of networks owned by strangers — and the two answers are very different, and the difference between them explains a great deal.

Our route, then, from the small to the large. We begin with the wire itself: Ethernet, its addresses, and the switches that sort its traffic, with an excursion into a poem. Then the puzzle of why every machine has two addresses, and the plain, trusting little protocol that joins them. Then the IP address properly examined — its two halves, the slash and the number after it that your cloud console keeps asking you for, and the special addresses that tell you something the moment you see them. Then the routing table and its single ruthless rule, with the time to live, the messages routers send home, the clever hack that turns them into a map, and the reason a connection can open perfectly and then hang forever. Then the matter of running out — four billion addresses for a planet that wanted many more — and the successor protocol that has been arriving, very slowly, for thirty years. And finally the network of networks: the treaties by which some seventy-odd thousand independent networks agree to carry one another's traffic, the protocol sketched on a lunch napkin that makes those treaties work, and the Sunday YouTube went to Pakistan, explained.

Ninety minutes, sir, from the hall to the sea floor. Let us begin with a memorandum written at Xerox, in the spring of nineteen seventy-three.

Part One — The Shared Wire

On Tuesday the twenty-second of May, nineteen seventy-three, a young engineer at Xerox's research centre in Palo Alto wrote a memorandum. His name was Bob Metcalfe, and the problem in front of him was a practical one. The centre was building a small personal computer called the Alto — a machine years ahead of its time, with a mouse and windows on its screen — and there were going to be a great many of them, in one building, and they would want to talk to one another and to the shared printers and file stores down the corridor. They needed a network, and it had to be cheap, simple, and fast.

Metcalfe had spent some time studying the ALOHAnet in Hawaii, which you may recall from last week: the radio network that sent packets between the islands. ALOHAnet's idea was disarmingly simple. There was one shared radio channel. Anybody who had a packet to send simply sent it. If two stations happened to send at the same moment, their transmissions garbled each other — a collision — and neither got through, so each waited a random interval and tried again. Metcalfe's memorandum proposed doing the same thing on a cable instead of the air. One long coaxial cable would snake through the building, and every computer would tap into it. Whatever any computer sent along the cable, every other computer would hear.

He called his network, at first, the Alto Aloha Network. In the course of the memorandum he renamed it, and his reasons are worth hearing, because they show an engineer thinking about layers before the word was common. The essential feature of the medium, he wrote, is that it carries transmissions and propagates bits to all stations; and it seemed wise to talk in terms of an ether, rather than "the cable", for as long as possible. The luminiferous ether was the invisible medium that nineteenth-century physicists believed filled all space and carried light — until experiment showed it did not exist. Metcalfe borrowed the name for a medium that did exist but whose physical nature he wished to keep out of the design. So the network became the Ether Network, and then, simply, Ethernet.

With a colleague, David Boggs, he built it. The first Ethernet ran at two point nine four megabits a second, a figure chosen for no better reason than that it fell conveniently out of the Alto's internal clock. In nineteen seventy-nine Metcalfe left Xerox and founded a company to sell Ethernet to everybody else; he called it Three-Com, for computers, communication, and compatibility, and wrote the three as a numeral, which is the sort of thing the period permitted. In nineteen eighty, three companies — Digital Equipment, Intel, and Xerox — published a joint specification for a ten-megabit version; in nineteen eighty-three the engineering profession's standards body, the IEEE, approved it as standard number eight-oh-two-dot-three, which is how the trade still refers to Ethernet in formal company. And in two thousand and twenty-three, half a century after the memorandum, Metcalfe received the Turing Award, computing's highest honour, for the invention, standardisation, and commercialisation of Ethernet. It was, by any standard, a good memorandum.

Let me describe what travels on the ether, because its shape has hardly changed since nineteen eighty, and it is in every packet your laptop sends.

An Ethernet frame — you will recall that frame is the link layer's word for its envelope — begins with two addresses: first the address of the machine it is going to, then the address of the machine it came from. Then a short field saying what kind of thing is inside, so the receiver knows which part of its software to hand it to: most often, the code meaning this is an IP packet. Then the payload itself — the packet. And at the very end, a checksum, called the frame check sequence, computed over the whole frame, so that the receiver can detect damage in transit and throw the frame away.

The addresses are the ones we met last week as hardware addresses. Their proper name is MAC address — MAC for medium access control, the part of the link layer that decides who may use the wire — and the trade says it as a word, mack. A MAC address is forty-eight bits long, written as six pairs of hexadecimal characters separated by colons. The first half of it identifies the manufacturer of the network card: every maker buys a block of addresses from the IEEE, and burns one into each card it builds. The second half is, in effect, a serial number. So in principle every network card in the world has a unique address, fixed at the factory, which it carries with it wherever it goes.

In principle. There is a bit in the address that marks it as locally administered — assigned by somebody other than the manufacturer — and modern telephones make enthusiastic use of it. Since about two thousand and nineteen and twenty, Android and the iPhone have by default shown each Wi-Fi network a different, made-up hardware address, for a reason that is obvious the moment it is said: if your telephone used the same hardware address everywhere, then every shop, cafe, and airport with a Wi-Fi network could recognise your telephone, and so you, every time you walked past. The address that was designed to identify a card turned out to identify a person, and the telephones now wear a different false moustache for every network they meet.

One MAC address is special: the one that is all ones — written as twelve f's. A frame sent to that address is a broadcast: it is meant for every machine on the network. Hold on to it. We shall need it in the next Part, at three o'clock in the morning.

There are two numbers you will meet about Ethernet frames, and both have stories.

The smallest frame Ethernet will carry is sixty-four bytes. The reason has to do with collisions on the old shared cable, and it is a pretty one. A sender could only detect that its frame had collided with somebody else's if it was still sending when the collision's echo came back along the cable. On a cable of the maximum permitted length, at ten megabits a second, that took up to about fifty microseconds — the time to send five hundred and twelve bits, which is sixty-four bytes. A frame shorter than that might finish before the news of its own collision arrived, and the sender would wrongly believe it had got through. So short frames are padded out to sixty-four bytes. The rule was set by the speed of light along a cable, and it has outlived the cable.

The largest payload is fifteen hundred bytes, the figure I promised you last week we should have cause to curse. Why fifteen hundred? There is, I regret to report, no single authoritative answer. The best accounts describe a compromise among the cost of memory on the early network cards, which had to hold whole frames; the fairness of the shared cable, since one station sending an enormous frame kept everybody else waiting; and the overhead of headers, which argued for frames not too small. You will hear it said that fifteen hundred is the most the checksum can protect, but that is folklore, and the arithmetic of the checksum says otherwise — it remains strong to several times that length, which is why the so-called jumbo frames of about nine thousand bytes, used inside many data centres, are perfectly safe. Fifteen hundred was a sensible number in nineteen eighty. It has since become the most expensive sensible number in the history of computing, because everything in the world has been built around it.

Now, the shared cable had a manners problem, which Metcalfe solved with an etiquette that you will recognise from any dinner party. Since everybody on the cable hears everybody else, the first rule is: listen before you speak. If somebody is already transmitting, wait until they have finished. That is called carrier sense. The second rule is: if you and somebody else begin at the same instant, both of you stop at once. That is collision detection. And the third rule, the clever one, is about what happens next.

Suppose two stations collide, both stop, and both wait exactly one microsecond before trying again. They will collide again. And again, forever, in perfect lockstep — like two polite gentlemen in a doorway, each stepping aside for the other in the same direction at the same moment. The way out is randomness. After a collision, each station waits a random interval before trying again. If they collide a second time, each doubles the range from which it draws its random wait, and tries again; and so on, doubling each time. Soon enough one of them goes first, and the other hears it and waits its turn. The scheme is called binary exponential backoff, and the whole discipline — listen, transmit, detect collisions, back off at random — went by the forbidding initials CSMA/CD: carrier sense, multiple access, collision detection.

And there is your first ritual of the evening, hiding in a nineteen-seventies cable. When a failed call is retried in your systems, you were told to back off exponentially and to add jitter — a random component to the wait. Now you know why the jitter matters. If a thousand clients all fail at the same moment, because a server hiccupped, and all retry after exactly one second, and then exactly two, and then exactly four, they arrive back at the server in a thousand-strong stampede each time, and knock it over again: polite gentlemen in a doorway, by the thousand. Randomness breaks the symmetry. Ethernet learned that in nineteen seventy-three; Amazon's engineers wrote it up again, with graphs, for the cloud, in two thousand and fifteen. It is the same lesson.

The shared cable is long gone. Its first successor was the hub: a box with many sockets, one cable to each machine, which simply repeated every frame it received out of every other socket — the shared cable folded up into a box. Then came the bridge, and its many-socketed descendant, the switch, which is cleverer, and whose cleverness is worth walking through, because it is a small marvel of learning by observation.

The cast: one switch with four sockets, which the trade calls ports. On port one, your laptop. On port two, a printer. On ports three and four, two other machines. The switch starts out knowing nothing at all.

First move: your laptop sends a frame addressed to the printer. It arrives at the switch on port one. The switch reads the source address on the frame — your laptop's — and makes a note: the laptop lives on port one. That is called learning.

Second move: the switch reads the destination address — the printer's — and looks in its notes. It has no idea where the printer lives. So it sends the frame out of every port except the one it came in on: ports two, three, and four. That is called flooding. The printer, on port two, receives it and is pleased. The machines on three and four receive it, see that it is not addressed to them, and ignore it.

Third move: the printer replies. Its frame arrives at the switch on port two. The switch learns: the printer lives on port two. And it looks up the destination — your laptop — and this time it knows the answer: port one. So it sends the reply out of port one and nowhere else. That is forwarding.

From then on, frames between your laptop and the printer travel only between ports one and two; the other machines never see them. And the fourth move happens quietly in the background: if the switch hears nothing from an address for a few minutes, it forgets it, in case the machine has been unplugged and moved. That is called aging, and it means the switch's notes are always reasonably fresh.

Learn, flood, forward, forget. With those four moves a switch turns a shared broadcast medium into something much better: a set of private conversations, each between two ports, carried on simultaneously. And because every cable now runs between exactly one machine and one port of a switch, and modern cables can carry traffic both ways at once, there is nothing left to collide with. The old etiquette of the shared cable — listen first, back off at random — is entirely unnecessary on a modern wired network. What survives from nineteen eighty is the frame: the same two addresses, the same type field, the same fifteen hundred bytes. The cable that started at two point nine four megabits a second now runs, in its latest standard, approved in two thousand and twenty-four, at eight hundred gigabits a second — nearly three hundred thousand times as fast — and the frame it carries would be perfectly recognisable to Bob Metcalfe.

Switches brought one new danger. Engineers like redundancy, so they connect switches to one another by more than one path, in case a cable is cut. But a network of switches with a loop in it is a catastrophe. Remember that a switch floods any frame whose destination it does not know, and always floods broadcasts. Send a broadcast into a loop of switches, and each switch floods it to the next, which floods it to the next, which floods it back to the first — which floods it again. The frame goes round forever, and multiplies at every switch with more than two ports, and there is nothing in an Ethernet frame to make it expire: no time to live, no counter. Within seconds the network is carrying nothing but copies of the same frame. Engineers call it a broadcast storm, and a single misplaced cable can bring down an entire building with one.

The cure was devised in nineteen eighty-five by an engineer at Digital Equipment named Radia Perlman, and it is called the spanning tree protocol. The idea is that the switches, by exchanging small messages among themselves, agree on a tree: a set of links that connects every switch to every other, with exactly one path between any two, and no loops. First they elect a single switch as the root of the tree — the one with the lowest identifier. Then each switch works out its cheapest path to the root. The links on those paths are used; every other link is switched off, held in reserve. If a link in the tree fails, the switches notice, and recompute, and bring a reserve link into service. Redundancy without loops.

Perlman, having designed the protocol, also wrote a poem about it, as a parody of Joyce Kilmer's Trees, and it is so exactly a summary of the algorithm that I shall read it to you whole. She called it Algorhyme.

I think that I shall never see A graph more lovely than a tree. A tree whose crucial property Is loop-free connectivity. A tree that must be sure to span So packets can reach every LAN. First, the root must be selected. By ID, it is elected. Least-cost paths from root are traced. In the tree, these paths are placed. A mesh is made by folks like me, Then bridges find a spanning tree.

I may add that Perlman has been called, in print, the mother of the Internet, and has said repeatedly that she wishes people would stop. The phrase was a journalist's, she says, and no single individual deserves credit for inventing the Internet; many people had large roles. It is a view this course has every reason to share.

Two more things, and the wire is done.

All the machines that receive one another's broadcasts — all the machines on one switched network, however many switches it spans — make up what is called a broadcast domain. Routers, which we shall come to, do not forward broadcasts; so a broadcast domain ends at the first router. In a large building, one would rather not have a single enormous broadcast domain, because every broadcast interrupts every machine in it. So a switch can be divided into several separate logical networks, each its own broadcast domain, by tagging every frame with a number saying which of them it belongs to. Those logical networks are called VLANs, virtual LANs, and the trade says vee-lan. When your company's network engineers speak of the printers' VLAN and the guests' VLAN, they mean exactly this: one set of physical switches, partitioned by a number in each frame.

And Wi-Fi. When the IEEE standardised wireless networking in nineteen ninety-seven, as standard eight-oh-two-dot-eleven, the shared medium came back, because the air is shared, inescapably, by everybody within range. But radio has a difficulty the cable did not: a radio cannot hear anybody else while it is transmitting, because its own signal drowns everything. So Wi-Fi cannot detect collisions; it must try to avoid them. A Wi-Fi station listens before sending, waits a random interval even when the air seems clear, sends, and then waits for an acknowledgement from the receiver — and if no acknowledgement comes, it assumes the frame was lost and sends it again. There is a further complication, with a fine name: the hidden node. Two laptops at opposite ends of a house may both be within range of the access point in the middle, yet out of range of each other, so neither can hear the other transmitting, and they collide at the access point without either knowing why.

Notice what Wi-Fi is doing with those acknowledgements. It is providing reliability at the link layer, frame by frame, retransmitting what the radio loses. That is precisely the performance enhancement that Saltzer, Reed, and Clark allowed for in the end-to-end argument last week: the radio loses so many frames that leaving every retry to the ends would be ruinously slow, so the link helps. But it is only help. The ends still check, because a link-layer acknowledgement says that one hop succeeded, and nothing about the rest of the journey.

So there is the wire. A frame carries two hardware addresses, a type, a payload of up to fifteen hundred bytes, and a checksum. On the old shared cable, stations listened, collided, and backed off at random; on a modern switched network, switches learn where every address lives by watching the traffic, flood what they do not know, forward what they do, and forget what they have not heard from lately. Loops are tamed by a spanning tree. Broadcasts reach everybody in the broadcast domain and stop at the first router. And Wi-Fi brings the shared medium back, with acknowledgements to make up for the air.

Part Two — Two Addresses

Here is a question that I suspect has crossed your mind at some point and been set aside for a quieter day. Every machine on a network has two addresses. It has a hardware address — the MAC address we have just met, burned in at the factory — and it has an IP address, of the dotted kind you type into configuration files. Why two? Surely one address per machine ought to be enough for anybody.

The answer is that the two addresses do entirely different jobs, and that neither can do the other's.

The hardware address is a name. It identifies a particular card, wherever that card happens to be. It is flat: it carries no information about location at all, beyond the identity of the company that made the card. A card made in a factory in Taiwan and installed in a laptop in Toronto has an address that says nothing about Toronto, and if you carry the laptop to London the address comes with it, unchanged. In that respect it is like a person's name, or the serial number on a watch.

The IP address is a location. It is assigned by the network you join, not by the factory, and it changes when you move: the laptop in Toronto has a Toronto address, and the same laptop in a hotel in London has a London one. And it is hierarchical: as we shall see in a moment, its leading part names a network and its trailing part names a machine within that network, the way a postal address runs from country to city to street to house.

Why must the address that does the travelling be a location rather than a name? Imagine trying to deliver letters addressed only by the recipient's name. Every sorting office in the world would need a ledger listing every person on Earth and which direction to send their post — billions of entries, changing every time anybody moved house. It would be impossible. A postal address works because it is aggregated: the sorting office in Vancouver needs to know only that everything for Ontario goes east, and need not know a single street in Toronto. The routers of the Internet work the same way, and so the address they read must be the kind that aggregates: an address of place, not of person. A flat hardware address is fine for picking one machine out of the few dozen on a single wire. It would be hopeless for picking one machine out of the billions on the planet.

So the division of labour is this. The IP address gets the packet to the right network, across as many routers as it takes. And on each link along the way, the hardware address gets the frame to the right machine — the next stop. The IP address is the destination on the letter; the hardware address is the label on the mailbag for the next sorting office.

This has a consequence that is worth saying very plainly, because it clears up a great deal. When your laptop sends a packet to a server in London, the IP header carries the server's IP address as its destination, and that address stays the same from one end of the journey to the other. But the frame your laptop sends is addressed, at the hardware level, to the box in the hall — the next hop — not to the server. The box in the hall unwraps it and sends a new frame, addressed to the hardware address of the next router. And so on. The IP destination never changes; the hardware destination changes at every hop. Your laptop never learns the server's hardware address. Across the Internet, it does not exist in any useful sense.

Let me walk that once with a small example, because it is the kind of thing one believes only after watching it. Take two machines in a data centre, on different networks, with one router between them: a web server, and the database server it wishes to query. The router has two network cards, one on each network, and each card has its own hardware address. So the cast is four hardware addresses — the web server's, the router's card on the web side, the router's card on the database side, and the database server's — and two IP addresses, the web server's and the database server's.

First hop. The web server builds a packet whose IP header says from the web server, to the database server. It knows the database server is not on its own network, so it wraps the packet in a frame addressed from its own hardware address to the router's web-side card. The frame crosses the first network and arrives at the router.

Second hop. The router strips off the frame and throws it away. It reads the IP header — to the database server — consults its table, and finds that the database server is on the network attached to its other card. It wraps the very same packet in a brand new frame: from its database-side card's hardware address, to the database server's hardware address. The frame crosses the second network and arrives.

Now compare. The IP header was not touched, apart from the time-to-live counter going down by one: from the web server, to the database server, on both hops. The frame was entirely different on each hop, with not one hardware address in common between them. That is the division of labour made visible: the IP addresses describe the journey, and the hardware addresses describe the leg.

That leaves one practical problem, and its solution is one of the oldest and plainest protocols in the stack. Your laptop knows the IP address of the box in the hall — it was told, when it joined the network, that one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot one is the gateway. But to send it a frame, the laptop needs the box's hardware address. How does it find out?

It asks. Out loud. Everybody on the network hears.

The protocol is called the Address Resolution Protocol — ARP, said as a word — and it was written down in November nineteen eighty-two, in an RFC by David Plummer of MIT, under a title of the period's magnificent length: An Ethernet Address Resolution Protocol — or — Converting Network Protocol Addresses to forty-eight bit Ethernet Address for Transmission on Ethernet Hardware. It is a short document, and a lively one; at one point in its description of the algorithm, the author breaks off, in a parenthesis, to tell the implementer to now look at the opcode — the now in capitals, and two exclamation marks after it — which is not a thing one often finds in a standard. Let me walk it, with the cast announced. On the household network are your laptop, at one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty; the box in the hall, at one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot one; and a printer and a television, which will overhear everything and say nothing.

One: the laptop broadcasts a frame — addressed to the hardware broadcast address, so that every machine on the network receives it — carrying a question. The packet-capture tools of the trade print it in a form that is almost a sentence, and it is worth hearing in that form: who has one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot one? Tell one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty. The question includes the laptop's own IP and hardware addresses, so that the answer can be sent back directly.

Two: every machine on the network receives the frame and reads the question. The printer, finding that the address asked for is not its own, ignores it. So does the television.

Three: the box in the hall, finding that the address asked for is its own, replies — not by broadcast, but directly, to the laptop's hardware address: one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot one is at — and then its hardware address.

Four: the laptop writes the answer into a small table, the ARP cache, and keeps it for a few minutes, so that it need not ask again for every packet. And now it can send its frame.

I am reminded, sir, that there is in your own household a much older implementation of the same protocol. At three in the morning, a request is broadcast to every station in the house. Its content, freely translated, is: who has milk? Tell Ian. Every station receives it. All but one, by long-standing convention, ignore it. The station that holds the resource replies directly to the requester, and then, I understand, retains a very clear record of the requester's location for the remainder of the night. Master Ian, I may say, implements the broadcast with an enthusiasm the protocol's author never envisaged.

Now, three things follow from ARP's design, and each matters in practice.

The first: the laptop must decide, before it asks anything, whether the destination is on its own network at all. If you print to the printer at one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot thirty, the laptop asks who has one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot thirty, and the printer answers, and the frame goes straight to the printer. But if you fetch a page from a server in London, the laptop does not ask who has the server's address — nobody on the household network would answer. It asks for the gateway's hardware address instead, and sends the packet there. How the laptop tells the two cases apart is the business of the next two Parts.

The second: ARP has no security whatever. Anybody on the network may answer any question, and the laptop believes the answers it receives — it will even accept answers to questions it did not ask. The seed of that trust is in Plummer's original algorithm, which tells every machine receiving an ARP packet to update any entry it already holds for the sender — the who in tell — before it has even checked whether the packet was meant for it. It was a sensible economy in nineteen eighty-two, among colleagues: why waste an announcement? It is the reason that, ever since, a liar on the local network has found ARP so very accommodating. So a malicious machine on the same network — on the Wi-Fi of a cafe, say — can announce one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot one is at its own hardware address, and the laptop will cheerfully send all its traffic for the outside world to the impostor, who can read it and pass it on. That is ARP spoofing, and it is the classic way of putting oneself in the middle of other people's conversations on a local network. The defence is not to fix ARP, which would be very hard, but to make the impostor's position worthless: if the traffic is encrypted end to end, the man in the middle holds only sealed envelopes. That is the end-to-end argument of the first evening, applied to security, and we shall see it again in the fourth.

The third: the same lack of ceremony is turned to good use. A machine may broadcast an ARP announcement about itself without being asked — a so-called gratuitous ARP, which a later RFC defined, with admirable plainness, as an ARP packet sent by a node in order to spontaneously cause other nodes to update an entry in their ARP cache: one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot fifty is at this hardware address, for anybody who cares. Why would it? Because when a service fails over from one machine to another and the standby takes over the service's address, every neighbour's ARP cache still points to the old machine. One gratuitous announcement, and every cache on the network is corrected at once. If you have ever run a pair of load balancers or database servers that share a virtual IP, and watched the address move from one to the other within a second of the primary's death, this is the mechanism. The new holder of the address simply tells the room.

I should add, for completeness, two notes on where ARP does and does not live. In the newer version of IP, which we shall meet before the evening is out, ARP is replaced by a similar mechanism with a different name, called neighbour discovery, which uses a more discreet kind of announcement than a shout to the whole house. And in the cloud, there is very often no real ARP on the wire at all. The virtual network is an illusion maintained by the hypervisor, which knows perfectly well where every virtual machine is, and which answers your machine's ARP questions itself, on the spot, without troubling anybody else. Your virtual machine believes it is shouting into a room. In fact it is whispering to a butler who has the guest list by heart.

So: two addresses, because there are two jobs. The hardware address is a name, flat and fixed, good for picking out one machine on one wire. The IP address is a location, hierarchical and assigned, good for finding one network among millions. The IP destination stays the same from end to end; the hardware destination is rewritten at every hop, to name the next stop. And ARP joins the two, by the simple expedient of asking the whole room, who has this address?, and believing whoever answers.

Part Three — The Address and the Prefix

Now let us look properly at the second address — the one that does the travelling.

An address in IP version four, which is the version that still carries most of the world's traffic and the one you mean when you say "an IP address" without qualification, is a number thirty-two bits long. Thirty-two little switches, each on or off. Thirty-two bits can count to a little under four point three billion, and that is the number of possible addresses: about four point three billion, for a planet that now has rather more people than that and a great many more devices. Hold on to that figure; it is the plot of the fifth Part.

Nobody reads thirty-two bits comfortably, so the convention is to cut the address into four groups of eight bits — each group is called an octet, eight being the number — and to write each octet as an ordinary number. Eight bits can count from zero to two hundred and fifty-five, so each of the four numbers lies in that range, and they are written with dots between them. That is the familiar dotted quad. The box in the hall of a great many houses has the address one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot one; your laptop, on the same network, might be one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty. You will notice I say them as the trade does — one ninety-two, not one hundred and ninety-two — which is a small thing, and a useful one, since it is how you will hear them said in every war room you ever sit in.

Now, the essential fact about an IP address — the fact that makes it different in kind from a hardware address — is that it has two parts. The left-hand part names a network. The right-hand part names a particular machine, a host, on that network. It is exactly like a telephone number with an area code: the area code tells the telephone system which region to send the call to, and the rest of the number picks out one telephone within it. Or like a postal code and a house number, if you prefer letters to telephones, which in this course I generally do. Routers far away care only about the network part. Only the last router, the one attached to the destination network, cares about the host part.

The interesting question is: where, in the thirty-two bits, does the network part end and the host part begin? The answer to that question has changed once, in a hurry, and the change is the reason your cloud console asks you to type a slash and a number.

In the original design, written down in nineteen eighty-one, the boundary was fixed by the first few bits of the address itself, into what were called classes. A class A address gave the first eight bits to the network and the remaining twenty-four to hosts: a network of a class A could hold some sixteen million machines. A class B address gave sixteen bits to each: sixty-five thousand-odd hosts per network. A class C gave twenty-four bits to the network and eight to hosts: two hundred and fifty-six addresses, a few of which were reserved. Three sizes, like a tailor who stocks small, medium, and enormous.

The class A networks were handed out in the early days to whoever asked first and seemed important — universities, government departments, a handful of great companies — so that there are to this day organisations that hold sixteen million public addresses apiece, a fact that the rest of the Internet regards much as tenants regard a landlord with fourteen spare bedrooms. But the trouble that brought the system down was in the middle. A class C, at two hundred and fifty-six addresses, was too small for any organisation of size. A class B, at sixty-five thousand, was far too large for almost all of them. So an organisation with two thousand machines got a class B, and used three percent of it, and the rest sat idle. By the early nineteen-nineties the class B networks were visibly running out, and the tables in the Internet's core routers were swelling alarmingly, since every network needed its own entry. A memorandum of November nineteen ninety-two estimated that the unassigned class B networks would be gone in less than two years, and noted that the most powerful routers of the day could hold something on the order of sixteen thousand routes. The core routers of today hold a little over a million. The Internet's engineers formed a working group on the problem — they called it ROAD, for routing and addressing, which is the kind of joke engineers permit themselves in committee — and something had to give, and in September nineteen ninety-three, it did.

The replacement is called Classless Inter-Domain Routing — CIDR, which the trade pronounces cider. The RFC that introduced it diagnosed the old system in one sentence that any tailor would recognise: a class C, with a maximum of two hundred and fifty-four host addresses, is too small, while a class B, which allows up to sixty-five thousand five hundred and thirty-four addresses, is too large for most organisations. Its idea is almost embarrassingly simple: abolish the classes, and let the boundary between network and host fall at any bit. And since the boundary can no longer be deduced from the address, write it down beside the address: the address of the network, then a slash, then the number of bits that belong to the network part. That number is called the prefix length, and the whole notation is called, naturally, slash notation.

So one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot zero, slash twenty-four, means: the first twenty-four bits are the network, and the remaining eight bits are free for hosts. Eight free bits make two hundred and fifty-six addresses — one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot zero up to one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot two fifty-five. That is what an engineer means by "a slash twenty-four". The RFC, I should add, was modest about its own prospects: the plan, it said, should be viable for at least three years. That was in nineteen ninety-three. It is still the plan. And the arithmetic of all the others follows from one rule, which I shall give you and then walk: every bit you move from the host side to the network side halves the block; every bit you move back doubles it.

Walk it with me. A slash twenty-four: two hundred and fifty-six addresses. A slash twenty-five: one more bit to the network, half as many hosts: a hundred and twenty-eight. A slash twenty-six: sixty-four. A slash twenty-eight: sixteen. A slash thirty: four. A slash thirty-two: every bit is network, no bits left for hosts, so exactly one address — which is how you write a single machine in this notation. Now go the other way. A slash twenty-three: five hundred and twelve. A slash twenty-two: a thousand and twenty-four. A slash twenty: four thousand and ninety-six. A slash sixteen: sixty-five thousand five hundred and thirty-six — the old class B. A slash eight: about sixteen million — the old class A. And a slash zero: no bits of network at all, which means every address there is — the whole Internet. Remember the slash zero. It turns up in the next Part as the most important line in every routing table you will ever see.

There is an older way of writing the same information, which you will still meet in configuration files: the netmask. It is a thirty-two-bit number with a one in every network position and a zero in every host position, written as a dotted quad. A slash twenty-four, as a netmask, is two fifty-five dot two fifty-five dot two fifty-five dot zero: three octets of all ones, then an octet of zeros. A slash sixteen is two fifty-five dot two fifty-five dot zero dot zero. Same information, older dress. The machine uses the mask in the obvious way: to find the network an address belongs to, it keeps the bits where the mask has ones and zeroes the bits where it has zeros.

Why did abolishing classes help with the swelling of the routing tables? Because CIDR permits aggregation, and aggregation is the single most important trick in making a planetary network possible. Suppose your internet provider holds the block ten dot twenty dot zero dot zero slash sixteen — sixty-five thousand addresses — and hands out slash twenty-fours from it to two hundred and fifty-six customers. The rest of the Internet does not need two hundred and fifty-six entries, one for each customer. It needs one: everything in the slash sixteen goes to that provider. The provider sorts out the rest internally. A hierarchy of blocks within blocks lets the core of the Internet carry one line for an entire provider, instead of a line for every network in it. It is the postal code again: a sorting office in Vancouver need not know every street in Toronto, only that everything beginning with M goes east.

Two addresses in every block are traditionally set aside. The first, with all the host bits zero, names the network itself — one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot zero is the name of the slash twenty-four, not a machine on it. The last, with all the host bits one, is the block's broadcast address — a packet sent to one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot two fifty-five is meant for every host on the network. So a slash twenty-four gives you two hundred and fifty-four usable addresses, not two hundred and fifty-six, and the cloud providers typically reserve a few more in every subnet for their own machinery. That is why the number of addresses your console says are available is always a little smaller than the arithmetic says it should be; the console is not cheating you, only keeping some of the bedrooms for the staff.

Now: some addresses in the space are special, and three families of them will save you time.

The first family is the private ranges. In February nineteen ninety-six, an RFC numbered nineteen eighteen set aside three blocks that anyone may use inside their own network, and that are never routed on the public Internet: ten dot zero dot zero dot zero, slash eight; one seventy-two dot sixteen dot zero dot zero, slash twelve; and one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot zero dot zero, slash sixteen. That is why every home network in the world seems to be one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot something, and why every cloud network you have ever built seems to be ten dot something. The addresses are reused by millions of networks at once, which is perfectly safe so long as none of those networks tries to talk to another directly — a thing they accomplish through a device we shall dissect in the third evening.

And here is your first ritual of the evening. When you created a virtual network in the cloud, you were asked for a block — and someone told you to choose a slash sixteen in ten dot something, and to make sure it did not overlap with the other teams' blocks. Now you can see why on both counts. A slash sixteen gives you sixty-five thousand addresses to carve into subnets — slash twenty-fours for this tier, slash twenty-fours for that zone — with room to grow, and running out of addresses in a network that is already full of running machines is one of the more tedious emergencies in the trade. And the blocks must not overlap because, the day somebody wants to connect your network to theirs, an address must mean exactly one place. If both networks contain ten dot zero dot one dot five, a router joining them cannot know which one a packet is for, and the connection simply cannot be made without renumbering one side. That conversation, should you ever have to hold it, is one of the less agreeable meetings in an engineer's life, and the dropdown in the console was trying to spare you it.

The second family is loopback. The whole block one twenty-seven, slash eight, is reserved for a machine talking to itself, and one twenty-seven dot zero dot zero dot one — localhost — is the address every machine uses for the purpose. A packet sent to it never touches a wire. It goes down through the layers and straight back up again, inside the same machine, a letter posted through one's own letterbox.

And here is your second ritual, a more treacherous one. When you start a server, you tell it which address to listen on, and two choices are common: one twenty-seven dot zero dot zero dot one, and zero dot zero dot zero dot zero. They look like cousins. They are opposites. Listening on one twenty-seven dot zero dot zero dot one means: accept connections only from programs on this same machine. Listening on zero dot zero dot zero dot zero, in this context, means: accept connections arriving at any of this machine's addresses, from anywhere. The first is private; the second is open to whoever can reach the machine. The classic misadventure runs as follows: a developer's server works perfectly on a laptop, where it listens on localhost; the same server is put into a container, still listening on localhost; and nothing outside the container can reach it, because the container's localhost is the container's own, and the request from outside arrives at a different address altogether. Half an afternoon is lost, and the remedy, once found, is to listen on zero dot zero dot zero dot zero. The opposite misadventure — a database listening on zero dot zero dot zero dot zero on a machine with a public address, open to the entire Internet — is how a remarkable number of companies have made the newspapers.

The third family is link-local: one sixty-nine dot two fifty-four dot zero dot zero, slash sixteen. A machine that asks for an address and gets no answer will, as a last resort, pick one for itself from this range, good only for talking to its immediate neighbours. So if you ever look at a machine's address and see one sixty-nine dot two fifty-four dot something, you have learned something at once: it asked for an address and nobody answered. The fault lies with whatever was supposed to answer.

One address in that range has a second life you will certainly have met. Every major cloud runs a small service at one sixty-nine dot two fifty-four dot one sixty-nine dot two fifty-four, reachable only from inside the virtual machine itself, where the machine can ask about its own identity, its configuration, and the temporary credentials it has been granted. It is enormously convenient. It is also, therefore, a target: a program that can be tricked into fetching a web address of an attacker's choosing can be made to fetch that one, and hand over the credentials. That precise trick was at the heart of a famous breach at a large American bank in two thousand and nineteen, which exposed the records of about a hundred million people in the United States and some six million in Canada — the attacker tricked a misconfigured web firewall into fetching the metadata address, received the machine's temporary credentials in reply, and used them to copy the bank's stored files. Which is why the clouds have since added versions of their metadata services that demand a little more ceremony — a session token, obtained by a separate request — before they answer.

Last in this Part: how a machine gets its address in the first place. You will recall from the first evening that when your laptop joins the household network it asks for one, by a protocol called DHCP — the Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol. Let me walk the exchange, because it is a small marvel of bootstrapping, and it has four messages with a mnemonic you will not forget.

The cast: the laptop, which has no address yet, and the DHCP server, which is usually the box in the hall. One: the laptop, having no address, cannot address anybody; so it broadcasts, from the address zero dot zero dot zero dot zero to the address two fifty-five dot two fifty-five dot two fifty-five dot two fifty-five — everybody — a message that amounts to is there a DHCP server here? That is the Discover. Two: the server replies with an Offer: here is an address you may have, with the netmask, the gateway's address, and a resolver's address. Three: the laptop broadcasts a Request: I should like to take the address you offered. It broadcasts rather than replying privately because there may be more than one server on the network making offers, and this way they all learn which offer was taken. Four: the server sends an Acknowledgement: it is yours, for a while. Discover, Offer, Request, Acknowledge: the trade calls it DORA, and it is the reason your laptop has an address within a second or two of seeing the network.

That "for a while" is the lease: the address is lent, not given, for a period of hours or days, and the laptop must renew it before it expires. That is why your laptop's address sometimes changes when you come back from a holiday — the lease ran out in your absence and the address went to somebody else — and why, in a data centre, machines that must keep their addresses are given reservations rather than leases.

So: an IP address is thirty-two bits, written as four numbers with dots; it has a network part and a host part, and since nineteen ninety-three the boundary between them can fall at any bit, written as a slash and a prefix length. Every bit added to the prefix halves the block; a slash twenty-four is two hundred and fifty-six addresses, a slash thirty-two is one, a slash zero is everything. Blocks nest inside blocks, and that nesting lets the core of the Internet carry one entry for an entire provider. And the special addresses — private, loopback, link-local — each tell you something the moment you see them.

Part Four — The Routing Table

We have addresses. Now let us send something, and watch the decisions that get it where it is going. There are surprisingly few of them, and every one is local.

The first decision is made by the sender itself, before any router is involved, and it is a simple question: is the destination on my own network? Your laptop knows its own address — one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty — and it knows its prefix, slash twenty-four. So it knows its network: one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one, everything from zero to two fifty-five. If you print a document to the printer at one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot thirty, the laptop applies its mask to that address, finds the same network, and concludes that the printer is a neighbour. It asks, by ARP, for the printer's hardware address, and sends the frame straight there. No router is consulted at all.

But if you fetch a page from a server whose address is, say, two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot ten — a block, as it happens, that the Internet's rules reserve for examples in documentation, so that nobody's real server is inconvenienced by a lecture — the laptop applies its mask and finds a different network altogether. The destination is not a neighbour. So the laptop does the only thing it can: it hands the packet to the gateway — the box in the hall — by sending a frame to the box's hardware address with the server's IP address still written inside. Not mine to deliver; you deal with it.

That decision, written down, is the laptop's routing table, and you may be surprised how short it is. On a typical laptop it has, in essence, two lines. The first says: one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot zero, slash twenty-four: directly connected — deliver it yourself, on the Wi-Fi. The second says: zero dot zero dot zero dot zero, slash zero: send it to one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot one. And there is the slash zero I asked you to remember. Slash zero means every address there is. So the second line matches everything, and it is called the default route: the rule for every destination that no more particular rule covers. It is the "all other enquiries" window at the post office, and on a laptop it handles nearly everything you do.

Now, you will have spotted a puzzle. The destination one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot thirty — the printer — matches both lines. It is inside the slash twenty-four, and it is inside the slash zero, because everything is inside the slash zero. So which line wins? The answer is the rule on which the entire Internet's forwarding rests, and the title of this evening. When several entries in a routing table match a destination, the router chooses the one with the longest prefix — the most specific match. This is called longest prefix match, and the slash twenty-four, being longer than the slash zero, wins. The printer is delivered directly. Everything that matches nothing else falls through to the default.

Let me walk the rule on a slightly larger table, because you should see it work more than once before you trust it. Imagine a router in a company's network with four entries. I shall read you the table first, then send four packets through it.

Entry one: zero dot zero dot zero dot zero, slash zero — send to the internet provider. Entry two: ten dot zero dot zero dot zero, slash eight — send to the company's core network. Entry three: ten dot twenty dot zero dot zero, slash sixteen — send to the Toronto office. Entry four: ten dot twenty dot thirty dot zero, slash twenty-four — send to the third floor in Toronto.

First packet: destination ten dot twenty dot thirty dot forty. Which entries contain it? The slash zero, certainly. The slash eight — it begins with ten. The slash sixteen — it begins with ten dot twenty. And the slash twenty-four — it begins with ten dot twenty dot thirty. All four match. The longest is the slash twenty-four. The packet goes to the third floor.

Second packet: ten dot twenty dot ninety-nine dot one. The slash zero matches; the slash eight matches; the slash sixteen matches, since it begins ten dot twenty; but the slash twenty-four does not, because its third number is thirty and ours is ninety-nine. Longest of the three: the slash sixteen. The packet goes to the Toronto office, and the Toronto office's own router, with its own table, will decide where within Toronto.

Third packet: ten dot ninety-nine dot one dot one. Only the slash zero and the slash eight match. The packet goes to the company's core.

Fourth packet: eight dot eight dot eight dot eight — which happens to be a famous public resolver. Only the slash zero matches. Out to the internet provider it goes.

So: the most specific route wins, and the general routes catch everything the specific ones do not. It is exactly how a sorting office works, if the sorting office is sensibly run. All of Canada goes in the Canada bag — except Toronto, which has its own bag — except the King Street branch, which has its own bag again. A letter for King Street matches all three rules and goes in the King Street bag, because that is the most particular rule that applies. Specific exceptions to general rules: it is how law works, too, and how good butlers work, and it is how every router on Earth works, millions of times a second, in hardware built to perform exactly this lookup and almost nothing else.

Now the second great principle of this Part, which follows from the first and which surprises most people when they first take it in. No router plans a route. There is no map of the journey anywhere. Each router, receiving a packet, consults its own table, picks the next hop, and sends the packet one step further — and then forgets it. The next router does the same with its table, which it built for itself. The route a packet takes from your laptop to a server in London is not a decision anybody made; it is simply the sum of twenty local decisions made by twenty routers that have never heard of one another. This is called hop-by-hop forwarding, and it is the store-and-forward principle of the first evening, made concrete. The routers are like the citizens of a town where everybody gives directions only to the next corner, and somehow, if every one of them gives good directions, every visitor arrives.

If every one of them gives good directions. That is the catch, and it leads to a mechanism you have certainly seen without understanding. Suppose two routers have inconsistent tables — suppose router A believes the way to some network is through router B, and router B believes the way is through router A. This happens, briefly, whenever the network changes and the news has reached some routers and not others. A packet for that network will be passed from A to B, and from B to A, and back again, forever — a letter bounced between two sorting offices that each believe the other is responsible for it. Forever, that is, unless something stops it.

The something is a small field in the IP header called the time to live, or TTL. The sender sets it to some modest number — sixty-four is common, or a hundred and twenty-eight — and every router that forwards the packet reduces it by one. If a router reduces it to zero, the router discards the packet. So no packet can circulate for more than a few dozen hops; a loop costs a little wasted work and then expires. The original specification, in nineteen eighty-one, defined the field in seconds — the time, it said, is measured in units of seconds, but since every module that processes a datagram must decrease the time to live by at least one even if it processes the datagram in less than a second, the field must be thought of only as an upper bound. Elsewhere the same document calls it, rather splendidly, a self destruct time limit. In practice nobody ever held a packet for a whole second, and the field has always worked as a count of hops; the newer version of IP admits as much, and calls the field the hop limit. The old name is a fossil of the original intention, which is the usual fate of names in this trade.

When a router discards a packet because its time to live ran out, it does one more thing: it sends a short message back to the sender, saying so. That message belongs to a small protocol that rides inside IP and exists for exactly this sort of report: the Internet Control Message Protocol, ICMP. ICMP is the network's way of sending you a note. Your packet's time to live expired here. I cannot reach that destination. Your packet was too big for my next link. And, most famously: are you there? — yes, I am here. That last exchange, an echo request answered by an echo reply, is what the program called ping sends and receives. It was written in December nineteen eighty-three by Mike Muuss at the United States Army's Ballistic Research Laboratory, and he described it afterwards, with justice, as a little thousand-line hack that he wrote in an evening and that practically everyone seems to know about. He named it, he said, after the sound a sonar makes: you send out a pulse and listen for the echo. It is not, he was at pains to add, an acronym for anything, whatever you may have been told. And it has acquired a literary connection. There is a children's picture book of nineteen thirty-three called The Story About Ping, concerning a duck lost on the Yangtze, and network administrators took to reviewing it on a large bookseller's website as though it were a manual for the program. Muuss preserved one such review on his own page, from a reader who gave an address in Upper Volta, Uzbekistan, which praised the book's deft allegory as an insightful explanation of one of Unix's most venerable networking utilities. Muuss died in a car accident in two thousand; his evening's work runs on every computer you own.

ICMP's specification, written in nineteen eighty-one, contains one rule I have always admired for its philosophy: to avoid the infinite regress of messages about messages, no ICMP messages are sent about ICMP messages. If the note goes astray, nobody writes a note about the note.

Now put the time to live and ICMP together, and you get one of the cleverest small hacks in the history of the field: traceroute. It was written by Van Jacobson at the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory in December nineteen eighty-eight — we shall meet him again next week, in a much larger role — and it uses the time to live for a purpose its designers never intended: to make every router along a path identify itself. The comments he left at the top of the program are a small masterpiece of the genre. He stole the idea, he wrote, from Steve Deering. And: do not use this as a coding example; he was trying to find a routing problem, and the code sort of popped out after forty-eight hours without sleep, and he was amazed it ever compiled, much less ran. It has run, since, on more machines than almost any program ever written.

Let me walk it, one probe at a time, because it is a procedure and deserves to be watched as one. The cast: your laptop, the destination far away, and a chain of routers in between whose identities you do not know.

First probe: your laptop sends a packet toward the destination with a time to live of one. The first router, the box in the hall, reduces it to zero, discards it, and sends back an ICMP message: time to live exceeded. That message comes from the router's own address — so now you know the first hop, and how long the round trip to it took.

Second probe: a packet with a time to live of two. The first router reduces it to one and forwards it. The second router — your provider's — reduces it to zero, discards it, and sends back its own time exceeded. Now you know the second hop.

Third probe: time to live three, and the third router reveals itself. And so on, one more each time, until at last a probe has enough time to live to reach the destination itself, which replies in a different way — traditionally, with a message saying that the probe was addressed to a port on which nothing was listening, which is traceroute's way of knowing it has arrived.

The result is a list of the routers along the path, each with its round-trip time: a map of the journey that no router ever drew, assembled by deliberately killing one packet at each step and reading the obituary. And now you can read traceroute's output better than most. When a line shows three asterisks instead of a time, it does not necessarily mean that something is broken: it means that router did not send back a time exceeded message, perhaps because it is configured not to, or because it rations such messages when busy. If later hops reply, the path through the silent router is evidently fine. And when the times jump suddenly from ten milliseconds to eighty between one hop and the next, you have very likely just watched your packet cross an ocean; the jump is the speed of light in glass, five microseconds a kilometre, made visible. One caution: the path your packets take to a destination need not be the path the replies take back. Every router chooses only the next hop, and the routers on the return journey have their own tables and their own opinions. Routes on the Internet are frequently asymmetric, and traceroute shows you only the outbound half.

One more thing a router must check before it forwards a packet, and it is the source of a ritual you may have performed in some exasperation. Every link has a largest packet it will carry, called its maximum transmission unit, MTU, which the trade says as three letters. For Ethernet, and for most of the links you will meet, it is fifteen hundred bytes — the figure I promised you last week we should have cause to curse.

What happens when a packet is too big for the next link? In the original design, a router could cut it into pieces, called fragments, each small enough to fit, to be reassembled by the destination. Fragmentation turned out to be a poor idea, for reasons set out in a well-known paper of nineteen eighty-seven by two engineers at Digital Equipment Corporation, under the title — the trade loves this formula — Fragmentation Considered Harmful. Lose any one fragment and the whole packet is lost, so the loss rate multiplies; the destination must hold the pieces in memory while it waits for the rest; and boxes in the middle that want to look at port numbers cannot find them in any fragment but the first. Fragments were also, for a while, a weapon. In the mid-nineteen-nineties it was discovered that a great many operating systems would crash outright on receiving a ping whose fragments, reassembled, added up to more than the sixty-five thousand five hundred and thirty-five bytes that an IP packet is permitted to be. The attack was called, with the period's fondness for melodrama, the ping of death, and it needed nothing more sophisticated than the program Mike Muuss wrote in an evening. So modern senders set a bit in the IP header that says don't fragment, and rely instead on path MTU discovery: if a packet is too big for some link along the way, the router there discards it and sends back an ICMP message saying, in effect, too big; my link carries only so many bytes. The sender notes the figure and sends smaller packets thereafter. It is an elegant arrangement, and it has one fatal weakness.

The weakness is that some firewalls, configured by people who had heard that ICMP is dangerous, block all ICMP messages, including the one that says too big. Now consider what happens. The connection opens normally, because the handshake's packets are tiny. A small request goes through. Then the server begins to send its response in full-sized packets, the first link with a smaller MTU discards them and sends back too big, the firewall swallows the message, and the server, hearing nothing, sends the same full-sized packets again, and again. The connection does not fail. It hangs — open, polite, and perfectly silent — after the first few hundred bytes. Engineers call this a black hole, and the symptom is so distinctive that you can diagnose it from across a room: ping works, the connection opens, small things load, large things never arrive.

And now the ritual. Where do links with an MTU smaller than fifteen hundred bytes come from? Very often from encapsulation — the envelopes within envelopes of the first evening. A VPN, or a tunnel, or an overlay network in a cloud, wraps each of your packets in an extra envelope of its own before sending it across the real network. The extra envelope costs some tens of bytes. So the real network's fifteen hundred bytes now hold your packet plus the tunnel's envelope, and your packet must be correspondingly smaller. If nothing tells the sender, the sender keeps sending fifteen-hundred-byte packets into a tunnel that can carry only fourteen hundred and something, and if the too big messages are lost, you have a black hole. Hence the instructions you will have followed at some point: lower the MTU on the VPN interface; or enable the setting with the alarming name of MSS clamping, which has the box at the tunnel's mouth quietly rewrite the handshake so that the two ends agree from the start to send segments small enough to fit. You were not performing magic. You were making room in the envelope.

So: the sender's first decision is whether the destination is a neighbour; if not, the default route hands it to the gateway. Every router after that consults its own table, chooses the most specific matching route — the longest match wins — and forwards the packet one hop, knowing nothing of the rest of the journey. The time to live keeps loops from running forever, and when it expires a router sends back an ICMP note — which traceroute turns into a map. And the MTU limits the size of what any link will carry, with path MTU discovery to learn it and a black hole waiting for anybody who blocks the messages that teach it.

Part Five — Running Out

Four point three billion addresses. When the address was fixed at thirty-two bits, in the specification of nineteen eighty-one, the Internet consisted of a few hundred computers, nearly all of them in universities and research laboratories, and four billion must have looked like a number one need never think about again. Vint Cerf has said, more than once, that he thought of the whole thing as an experiment, and that four point three billion seemed enough for an experiment. It was. The trouble is that the experiment escaped.

The waste began at once, with the classes. Sixteen million addresses to an organisation that needed fifty thousand; sixty-five thousand to an organisation that needed two thousand. CIDR, in nineteen ninety-three, stopped the bleeding, by letting blocks be cut to fit. The private ranges, in nineteen ninety-six, helped more, by letting every organisation number its internal machines from the same reusable stock. But then came the web, and then broadband in every house, and then a telephone in every pocket, each wanting an address, and the arithmetic became unanswerable.

The addresses are handed out through a hierarchy, which you will by now find unsurprising. At the top sits IANA — the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority, a function that for many years was, in practice, Jon Postel and a small staff at a university institute in California — which hands out large blocks, each a slash eight of sixteen million addresses, to five regional registries: one for North America, one for Europe and the Middle East, one for Asia and the Pacific, one for Latin America, and one for Africa. The trade knows them by their initials, and you will meet the initials in the news: ARIN for North America; RIPE NCC, said ripe, for Europe; APNIC for Asia and the Pacific; LACNIC for Latin America; and AFRINIC for Africa. The regional registries hand smaller blocks to internet providers and large organisations, who hand smaller blocks still to their customers. Blocks within blocks, all the way down.

On Thursday the third of February, two thousand and eleven, at a small ceremony in Miami, IANA handed out its last five slash eights: one to each of the five regional registries. The central pool of IP version four addresses was empty. The registries ran dry one after another in the years that followed. The Asian and Pacific registry reached its final block within ten weeks, that April. The North American registry emptied its free pool in September two thousand and fifteen. And the European registry, which had been rationing its last block in tiny portions for seven years, announced at twenty-five minutes to four on the afternoon of the twenty-fifth of November, two thousand and nineteen, that it had made its final allocation, and added, with a directness one rather admires in a bureaucracy, that it had now run out of IP version four addresses. Full stop.

What happens when a thing that was free runs out? It acquires a price. Weeks after the central pool emptied, in March two thousand and eleven, Microsoft paid seven and a half million dollars for six hundred and sixty-six thousand addresses that had belonged to Nortel — the bankrupt Canadian telephone-equipment maker, which, like many old companies, had been sitting on a large block it had been given for nothing decades before. That came to about eleven dollars an address. Prices rose for a decade after that, peaking, by most accounts, at several times that figure a few years ago, before easing again. And if you have looked at a cloud bill lately, you will have seen the shortage arrive in your own accounts: since February two thousand and twenty-four, Amazon has charged for every public IP version four address its customers hold, by the hour. That line item, sir, is the price of a decision taken in nineteen eighty-one by people who thought they were running an experiment.

How, then, does the Internet keep working with fewer addresses than devices? Mostly by sharing them. The box in your hall has exactly one public address, given to it by your provider. Behind it, your laptop, your telephone, Mrs Milovanov's tablet, the television, and very likely the baby monitor all have private addresses from the one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight range — which, you will recall, are never routed on the public Internet. When any of them talks to the outside world, the box in the hall rewrites the packet so that it appears to come from the box's own public address, and keeps a note so that it can direct the replies back to the right machine inside. That sleight of hand is called network address translation — NAT, said as a word — and it is the device I promised in the first evening. It is how hundreds of millions of homes and offices manage on a single public address apiece. Exactly how the box knows which reply belongs to which machine — the note it keeps, and what it writes in it — requires the port numbers of the transport layer, which are next week's business, so I shall leave the mechanism there for now with a promise. What I will say tonight is that NAT was introduced, in nineteen ninety-four, explicitly as a short-term measure to buy time. It has now bought thirty years, and it has bent the architecture of the Internet out of shape in the process, in ways we shall come to.

The proper cure was always a bigger address. Work began in the early nineteen-nineties, and the result was published in December nineteen ninety-five, chiefly by Steve Deering — the same Deering whose idea Van Jacobson stole for traceroute, and who drew the hourglass — and Bob Hinden. It is called IP version six, and you may wonder, as everybody does, what became of version five. The answer is that the number five had already been given to an experimental protocol for streaming data, and six was the number assigned to the proposal of Deering's that grew into the new IP. Version numbers in this trade are like house numbers: they record the order in which plots were claimed, not the order in which anybody built anything worth living in.

IP version six addresses are a hundred and twenty-eight bits long — four times the length of the old ones, which makes them not four times as numerous but unimaginably more. Two to the hundred and twenty-eighth is about three hundred and forty billion billion billion billion. There is no useful way to picture that number, but the usual attempts are entertaining: enough to give an address to every grain of sand on Earth, and then to every grain of sand on a great many other Earths. The point is simply that the shortage is over, permanently, for any purpose anybody can foresee. A single household is now customarily given a slash fifty-six or a slash forty-eight of its own — more addresses for one house than the whole of the old Internet had.

They are written differently, too, which is the first thing everybody notices. Instead of four decimal numbers with dots, an IP version six address is eight groups of four hexadecimal characters, separated by colons. That is long, so there are two abbreviations: leading zeros in a group may be dropped, and any single run of groups that are entirely zero may be replaced by a double colon. So the loopback address, the machine talking to itself, which in version four is one twenty-seven dot zero dot zero dot one, is in version six written simply as colon colon one: seven groups of zeros swallowed by the double colon, and a one at the end. If you see addresses in a log full of colons, beginning with the characters f, e, eight, zero, they are the version six equivalent of the link-local addresses we met earlier — good only on the local wire.

Version six took the opportunity to tidy several of the old protocol's habits. There is no broadcast; a machine that wants to reach its neighbours uses a more discreet kind of group message called multicast, so that a question is heard only by machines that might want to answer it. ARP is replaced by a protocol called neighbour discovery, doing the same job in that more discreet way. Routers never fragment packets; a sender must discover the path's limit and fit within it, and every link must carry at least twelve hundred and eighty bytes. And a machine can give itself an address without asking anybody: the router on the local network announces the network's prefix — on this network, addresses begin thus — and each machine appends an identifier of its own to make a complete address. That is called stateless address autoconfiguration, which the trade shortens to SLAAC and says slack, and it is a small demonstration of the hierarchy at work: the network supplies the left half of the address, the machine the right.

So why, thirty years after it was published, does IP version six carry only part of the world's traffic? It is a fair question, and the answer is the narrow waist of the hourglass, which I warned you last week was very hard to change.

First, version six is not compatible with version four. A machine that speaks only version four cannot talk to a machine that speaks only version six, any more than a telephone could ring a telegraph office. So the transition cannot be made by one network at a time; for decades, nearly everything must speak both — what is called dual stack — which is expensive, and doubles everything that can go wrong.

Second, there could be no flag day. The first evening's flag day, in nineteen eighty-three, moved a few hundred hosts, all funded by one agency, onto a new protocol on a single date. There is nobody who can order the whole Internet to change on a date, and there never will be.

Third, NAT took away the pain. The shortage of addresses was supposed to force the move; but the box in the hall, sharing one address among a household, made the shortage bearable for most people, most of the time.

And fourth, the benefit accrues only when nearly everybody has moved, while the cost falls on each mover now. It is a coordination problem of the purest kind: everybody is better off if everybody switches, and nobody is much better off for switching first.

Meanwhile, the software at the edges has learned to hedge. When your browser looks up a name and finds that the server has both a version four and a version six address, it does not choose one and hope; it tries both, a fraction of a second apart, and uses whichever connection answers first. The technique was written down in two thousand and twelve under the faintly embarrassing name of Happy Eyeballs — the eyeballs being yours, and the happiness consisting in not having to stare at a page that is waiting on a broken version six path. It is a small instance of a large idea we shall meet again in the fifth evening: when you cannot tell in advance which of two paths will be fast, ask both, and take the first answer.

And yet it has been happening, slowly, the way glaciers happen. On the sixth of June, two thousand and twelve, the large web companies and many internet providers switched version six on permanently, in what was billed as its world launch. Mobile networks, which needed vast numbers of addresses and had no old equipment to protect, moved fastest. And this year — in the spring of two thousand and twenty-six — Google reported that about half of its users now reach it over IP version six. Half, after thirty years. I do not say this with any contempt, sir. It is a change of address for an entire planet, with no forwarding service, and no authority to order it. That it has got half way is, by the standards of such undertakings, remarkable.

So: thirty-two bits was enough for an experiment and not for a world. The central pool ran dry in February two thousand and eleven; addresses now have a market price, which appears on your cloud bill. NAT stretched the old addresses by sharing them among whole households and companies, at a cost to the architecture we shall examine next week. And the successor, with a hundred and twenty-eight bits and a great deal of tidying, has been arriving for thirty years and has reached about half the users of the largest web company on Earth.

Part Six — The Network of Networks

We have followed a packet from your laptop to the box in the hall, and from the box to your provider, and watched each router consult its table and choose the next hop. But I have been begging a question all evening, and it is time to stop. Where do the routing tables come from? Your laptop's table has two lines and was handed to it by DHCP. A router in the middle of the Internet has a table of a little over a million lines, one for every block of addresses announced anywhere in the world. Nobody types those in. They are learned — and how they are learned is the most consequential protocol in this course, and the one least understood by the people whose businesses depend on it.

First, a unit. The Internet is a network of networks, and the networks in question have a proper name. An autonomous system is a network, or a group of networks, run by one organisation under one routing policy, and presenting itself to the rest of the world as a single entity. Your internet provider is an autonomous system. So is Google, and Amazon, and Pakistan Telecom, and PCCW Global, and YouTube was one in two thousand and eight. So, very likely, is your own employer. Each autonomous system is identified by a number, its AS number, issued by the same regional registries that issue addresses — they were sixteen bits long until the supply ran short, and are now thirty-two, a story with a familiar shape. There are something like seventy-odd thousand of them active in the world today. The Internet is the sum of their agreements.

Routing is therefore done at two levels, with two very different philosophies, and it is worth seeing both.

Inside an autonomous system, the routers belong to one owner who can see everything and trusts everybody, and so they cooperate fully. They use what are called interior routing protocols — the two you will hear of are OSPF, for open shortest path first, and IS-IS — and the method is called link-state routing. Every router tells every other router, by flooding small messages through the network, exactly which links it has and what each costs. Each router thereby assembles a complete map of its whole network. And then each router, separately, computes the shortest path from itself to every destination on the map, and fills in its table accordingly.

The computation it uses has a pleasant history. In nineteen fifty-six a young Dutch mathematician named Edsger Dijkstra wanted a problem with which to show off a new computer, and settled on finding the shortest route between two Dutch cities. Late in life he described how the solution came to him, and I shall let him tell it: One morning I was shopping in Amsterdam with my young fiancee, and tired, we sat down on the cafe terrace to drink a cup of coffee and I was just thinking about whether I could do this, and I then designed the algorithm for the shortest path. As I said, it was a twenty-minute invention. He designed it, he added, without pencil and paper, and he had learned since that one of the advantages of designing without pencil and paper is that you are almost forced to avoid all avoidable complexities. That twenty-minute coffee is now running, continuously, inside a large fraction of the routers on Earth. It is called Dijkstra's algorithm, and if any single idea deserves to be called the heartbeat of the network inside your company's data centres, it is that one.

Between autonomous systems, everything is different. The networks belong to different owners, who are competitors as often as partners, and who have no intention of showing one another their internal maps or their costs. They do not want the shortest path. They want the path that suits their business. And so between autonomous systems there is exactly one protocol, used by every network on Earth that connects to more than one other, and it is called the Border Gateway Protocol: BGP.

BGP has an origin story worthy of it. In January nineteen eighty-nine, at an IETF meeting in Austin, Texas, two engineers — Kirk Lougheed of a young company called Cisco, and Yakov Rekhter of IBM — sat down to lunch and sketched the design of a new exterior routing protocol on napkins. The trade has called it ever since the two-napkin protocol, though one account I have seen counts three, and describes them as stained with ketchup; the historian in me must report both. The first specification appeared as an RFC that June. The fourth version, which added CIDR's slash notation and is the version still in use, was published in the mid-nineteen-nineties. A protocol designed over lunch now carries the routing of the entire planet, and has done for over thirty years.

Here is how it works. I shall walk it with a small cast, announced first. At one end, YouTube's network, which holds a block of addresses. Next to it, YouTube's provider — a large network that YouTube pays to carry its traffic to the rest of the world. Beyond that, one of the great global backbone networks. And at the far end, your provider in Toronto.

First: two neighbouring networks that wish to exchange routes set up a BGP session between a router on each side — a long-lived conversation, over TCP, which is the subject of next week. Within that session, each side tells the other which blocks of addresses it can reach. Such a message is called an announcement.

Second: YouTube announces its own block to its provider. The announcement says, in effect: I can deliver traffic for these addresses, and the path is: me. And the path is written as a list of AS numbers — at this point, a list of one.

Third: the provider accepts the announcement, installs a route in its own tables — for YouTube's block, send to YouTube — and passes the announcement on to its own neighbours, the backbone among them, with one change: it adds its own AS number to the front of the path. The path is now two long: the provider, then YouTube.

Fourth: the backbone does the same, prepending its own number, and passes it on. And your provider in Toronto receives an announcement for YouTube's block whose path reads: the backbone, then the provider, then YouTube. It installs the route, and now packets from Toronto to YouTube flow back along the chain the announcement came down.

That list of AS numbers is called the AS path, and BGP is accordingly known as a path-vector protocol. The path serves two purposes. It gives each network a crude sense of distance — other things being equal, a shorter path is preferred. And it prevents loops in the simplest possible way: a network that receives an announcement with its own number already in the path knows the announcement has gone round in a circle, and throws it away.

Now, the important part. A network typically hears announcements for the same block from several neighbours, by several paths. Which does it choose? This is where BGP is utterly unlike the shortest-path routing inside a network. The choice is governed by policy — the network's own preferences, configured by its operators — and policy is, at bottom, about money.

Between two autonomous systems there are, broadly, two kinds of relationship. In the first, one network pays the other to carry its traffic to and from the rest of the Internet. The payer is the customer; the payee is the provider; and the service is called transit. In the second, two networks of comparable size agree to exchange traffic directly with each other, without payment, because each benefits about equally from reaching the other's customers. They are peers, and the arrangement is called peering.

Follow the money and the policies write themselves. A network prefers to send traffic through a customer, because the customer pays it for the traffic. Failing that, through a peer, because peering is free. Only failing both, through its own provider, because that costs money. That ordering — customer, then peer, then provider — is the common practice of the trade. And there is a matching rule about whom to tell. A network announces its customers' routes to everybody: it wants traffic for its customers, since they pay. But it announces routes learned from peers and providers only to its own customers — never from one peer to another, nor from a provider to a peer — because otherwise it would be offering to carry traffic between two parties neither of whom pays it a penny. The economists of the Internet call the resulting pattern valley-free, and two researchers showed, around the turn of the century, that if networks follow rules of roughly this shape — above all, preferring routes through their customers — the global routing system is guaranteed to settle down rather than oscillate forever. It is one of the more reassuring results in the field: greed, properly structured, converges.

At the top of this hierarchy sit a dozen or so very large networks that pay nobody for transit, because they peer with all of one another and can reach the whole Internet that way. They are called tier one networks, and their names — the great backbone carriers of North America, Europe, and Asia — are mostly unknown to the public. The people who run them, I am told, find the term faintly absurd; one operator I have read called the terminology ridiculous, and asked not to be got started on it. But it describes a real thing: the top of a pyramid of payment, through which every corner of the Internet can reach every other.

Where do networks actually meet? Physically, in buildings. An internet exchange point is a place — usually a floor of a data centre — where hundreds of networks run their cables into a shared switch, so that any two of them can peer without each laying a line to the other. The largest, in Frankfurt and Amsterdam, carry traffic peaks of many terabits a second. Toronto has its own, and if you wish to visit the physical Internet in your own city, sir, it is concentrated to a remarkable degree in one building on Front Street West, a few minutes' walk from Union Station, where a great many of Canada's networks come to meet one another. And between continents, the networks meet by cable along the sea floor: several hundred of them, well over a million kilometres in all, carrying the overwhelming majority of the traffic between continents — with, I may add, only a few dozen ships in the world equipped to mend them.

One more use of BGP before we return to Pakistan, because you rely on it daily. Nothing in BGP requires that a block of addresses be announced from only one place. If a company announces the same block from fifty sites around the world, each network will simply choose, by its usual policies, the nearest of the fifty in BGP's sense, and a user in Toronto will be sent to the copy in Toronto while a user in Tokyo is sent to the copy in Tokyo, both using the same address. That is called anycast. The Internet's root name servers, which we shall meet in the fourth evening, are only thirteen in name but now about two thousand in fact, by this trick; the famous public resolvers at one dot one dot one dot one and eight dot eight dot eight dot eight are anycast; and so are most of the content delivery networks that put a copy of your site near your customers.

And now we may go back to that Sunday afternoon, with everything we need.

There is one property of BGP I have so far left out, and it is the whole of the story. BGP believes what it is told. When a neighbour announces a route, the receiving network installs it, unless its operators have configured a filter to say otherwise. There is, in the protocol as designed, no check that the network announcing a block of addresses has any right to it. The system rests on the assumption that networks announce only what is theirs, and on the hope that each provider filters its customers' announcements to make sure of it.

The first great demonstration of what happens when that hope fails came in April nineteen ninety-seven, from a small provider in Florida whose autonomous system number, seven thousand and seven, has lived in infamy ever since. One of its routers received a full table of the Internet's routes from a customer, and — in the words of the apology its engineer posted to the operators' mailing list the next day, which I quote because it is a classic of the genre — in an as yet unexplained twist of bits, the router began cutting those routes up into small, specific pieces and announcing them all to the world as its own. Being more specific than the real routes, they were preferred everywhere — the longest match wins — and much of the Internet's traffic was drawn into one small network in Florida, which could not possibly carry it, and was lost. Not having a filter imposed on the inbound side, the apology admitted, was our error. The trade learned from it, and providers began limiting how many routes a customer may announce. It has gone on learning from it ever since.

Now, Pakistan. Let me tell it again, slowly, with the cast announced and the rule in hand.

The cast: YouTube, whose network announced to the world a block of addresses that we may call a slash twenty-two — a thousand and twenty-four addresses, containing among others the addresses of YouTube's web servers. Pakistan Telecom, which was ordered to block YouTube. PCCW Global, Pakistan Telecom's provider in Hong Kong. And everybody else: every router on Earth carrying a full table.

First move. To block YouTube for its own customers, Pakistan Telecom created, inside its own network, a route for a slash twenty-four — two hundred and fifty-six addresses, a quarter of YouTube's block, and the quarter that contained YouTube's web servers — pointing into a black hole.

Second move. That announcement leaked out of Pakistan Telecom to PCCW. PCCW did not filter it; a filter would have asked whether Pakistan Telecom had any business announcing addresses that belonged to YouTube, and found that it had not. PCCW installed the route and passed it on to its neighbours, who passed it on to theirs.

Third move. Now every router in the world held two routes covering YouTube's web servers: YouTube's own slash twenty-two, and Pakistan Telecom's slash twenty-four. And each router, consulting its table for a packet bound for YouTube, applied the one rule it never breaks. Both routes matched. The slash twenty-four was longer. The longest match wins. And so, router by router, across the whole Internet, packets for YouTube were sent toward Pakistan, and into the hole.

Fourth move: YouTube's counter-attack, and you can now see exactly why it took the form it did. First, YouTube announced the same slash twenty-four itself. That put the two announcements on equal terms for length, so that routers fell back on BGP's other preferences — the AS path, the business policies — and some of the world's traffic came back to YouTube, though not all of it. Then, eleven minutes later, YouTube announced two slash twenty-fives — the same addresses, cut into halves of a hundred and twenty-eight each, each half more specific than Pakistan's slash twenty-four. And wherever those reached, the rule that had stolen YouTube's traffic gave it back. Longest match, answered with a longer match. Not everywhere, mark you: many networks refuse, as a matter of hygiene, to accept any route more specific than a slash twenty-four, so the slash twenty-fives did not reach the whole world. But they reached a great deal of it.

Fifth move. A little after nine o'clock, Greenwich time, PCCW cut off the announcements coming from Pakistan Telecom, the false route was withdrawn throughout the world, and the episode was over. Some two hours and a quarter, from start to finish.

That is the whole of it. A sensible local decision, an unapplied filter, a protocol that believes what it is told, and a forwarding rule — the longest match wins — that every router on Earth obeys without question, because it is the right rule, and is the right rule nearly always.

The incidents have continued, because the architecture has not changed in its essentials. In two thousand and ten a Chinese provider briefly announced some thirty-seven thousand blocks that were not its own, for about eighteen minutes; you may have read that it diverted fifteen percent of the world's traffic, which it did not — the fifteen percent was a share of routes, not of traffic, and the actual diversion was much smaller. In two thousand and eighteen, thieves announced routes for part of Amazon's DNS service, sent the visitors of a cryptocurrency wallet website to a forgery, and walked off with something like a hundred and fifty thousand dollars. In two thousand and nineteen, a small provider in Pittsburgh ran a piece of software that was supposed to optimise its routes, which it did by inventing thousands of over-specific ones; a steel company that was its customer passed them on to a large American carrier, which passed them to the world; and for a while a large share of the traffic of several of the Internet's biggest companies was trying to squeeze through a steel company in Pennsylvania. One of those companies reckoned it lost about fifteen percent of its global traffic at the worst moment.

The repair is under way, slowly, in the manner of every repair to the narrow waist. It is called RPKI, the resource public key infrastructure, and the idea is simple. The holder of a block of addresses publishes a cryptographically signed statement, called a route origin authorisation, saying in effect: this block may be announced by this autonomous system, and by no other, and no more specifically than so. Networks then check every announcement they receive against those statements, and discard the ones that contradict them — a practice called route origin validation. Had it been in place in two thousand and eight, Pakistan Telecom's slash twenty-four would have been marked invalid and dropped by every validating network, and the black hole would have stayed in Pakistan. In May two thousand and twenty-four, for the first time, more than half of the IP version four routes in the global table were covered by such statements, and the great backbone networks now nearly all discard routes that fail the check.

I should not like you to think the cure is complete. RPKI checks who may originate a route, not whether the path the route took is honest; a cleverer liar can still lie. And every security mechanism brings its own failure. In January two thousand and twenty-four, somebody logged into a large Spanish provider's account at the European registry with a password found in a public leak of stolen credentials — the password, I regret to tell you, was ripeadmin — and altered the provider's RPKI statements, so that the world's validating networks dutifully rejected the provider's own perfectly legitimate routes. The lock had been fitted to the door, and the key left under the mat.

So there is the network of networks. Inside each autonomous system, routers share a complete map and compute shortest paths with a Dutchman's twenty-minute algorithm. Between autonomous systems, BGP carries announcements from neighbour to neighbour, each adding its number to the path; each network chooses among the routes it hears by policy, and policy follows the money — customers first, then peers, then providers. The whole planet's routing rests on networks announcing only what is theirs, and on their neighbours checking; and when one fails to check, every router on Earth will faithfully deliver the world's traffic into a hole, because the longest match wins.

What the Boy Should Have Been Told

So, sir: the second lecture, as the boy should have heard it.

Every machine has two addresses because there are two jobs. The hardware address — the MAC address — is a name: flat, fixed at the factory, good for picking one machine out of the few on a wire, and useless for finding anything farther away. The IP address is a location: hierarchical, assigned by the network you join, changing when you move, and built so that the world's routers can find a network among millions without knowing a single machine in it. The IP destination stays the same from one end of a journey to the other; the hardware destination is rewritten at every hop, to name only the next stop. And ARP joins the two, by shouting who has to the whole room and believing whoever answers — a trusting little protocol that liars on coffee-shop networks have been abusing ever since.

On the wire itself, Ethernet frames carry two hardware addresses, a type, up to fifteen hundred bytes of payload, and a checksum. The old shared cable taught the trade an etiquette — listen before speaking, stop on a collision, and back off at random, doubling the range each time — and the switches that replaced it learn where every address lives simply by watching the traffic: learn, flood, forward, forget. A spanning tree keeps the switches from building loops that would drown the building in its own broadcasts; and Radia Perlman, who designed it, wrote the only routing poem worth learning by heart.

An IP address is thirty-two bits, written as four numbers with dots, with a network part on the left and a host part on the right. Since nineteen ninety-three the boundary between them can fall at any bit, written as a slash and a prefix length, and every bit added to the prefix halves the block: a slash twenty-four is two hundred and fifty-six addresses, a slash thirty-two is one, a slash zero is everything. Blocks nest inside blocks, which lets the core of the Internet carry one line for a whole provider. Ten, one seventy-two dot sixteen, and one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight are private; one twenty-seven is the machine talking to itself; one sixty-nine dot two fifty-four means that somebody asked for an address and nobody answered — except at one sixty-nine dot two fifty-four dot one sixty-nine dot two fifty-four, where the cloud answers questions it would be wiser to guard.

Every routing decision is local. A sender checks whether the destination is a neighbour, and if not hands it to the default route. Every router after that consults its own table and chooses the most specific matching route — the longest match wins — and sends the packet one hop further, knowing nothing of the rest of the journey. A time-to-live counter keeps loops from running forever, and ICMP carries the network's little notes home, which traceroute turns, by killing one packet at each hop, into a map nobody drew. And every link has a largest packet it will carry, which senders learn from ICMP's too big — unless a firewall eats the message, and a connection opens perfectly and hangs forever.

The addresses ran out in February two thousand and eleven. NAT stretched them, by sharing one public address among a household; IP version six, with a hundred and twenty-eight bits, has been arriving for thirty years and has now reached about half of the users of the largest web company on Earth. The narrow waist of the hourglass, it turns out, is very hard to let out.

And between the seventy-odd thousand autonomous systems of the world, routes are not designed but learned. BGP carries announcements from neighbour to neighbour, each adding its number to the path; every network chooses among the routes it hears by policy, and policy follows the money — customers first, then peers, then providers. The whole arrangement rests on networks announcing only what is theirs and on their neighbours checking. When nobody checked, one Sunday in two thousand and eight, every router on Earth obeyed the longest-match rule and carried the world's YouTube traffic into a hole in Pakistan; and YouTube took it back with the same rule, by announcing something longer still. Signed route statements are now slowly teaching the routers to doubt.

Your rituals, this evening, were these. You add random jitter to your retries because two polite stations backing off by identical intervals will collide forever, as Ethernet learned in nineteen seventy-three. You choose a slash sixteen in ten dot something for a cloud network because you will want room for subnets, and you keep it from overlapping your colleagues' because one day somebody will want to join the two, and an address must mean one place. A server in a container listens on zero dot zero dot zero dot zero because its localhost is its own and nobody else's. A machine with a one sixty-nine dot two fifty-four address has a broken DHCP server somewhere. You lower the MTU on a VPN, or clamp the segment size, because the tunnel's extra envelope costs bytes and somebody's firewall is eating the messages that would have said so. Traceroute's asterisks mean a router declined to write home, not necessarily that it is broken. A virtual IP leaps from a dead machine to its standby in a second because the standby announces itself to the room. And the line item for public IP version four addresses on your cloud bill is the price of a reasonable decision taken in nineteen eighty-one.

For reading: Larry Peterson and Bruce Davie's Computer Networks: A Systems Approach, a fine textbook whose authors have made it free to read online, for the routing and the addressing done properly; and, for pleasure, Andrew Blum's Tubes, in which a journalist sets out to visit the physical Internet — the exchange points, the cable landings, the buildings where the networks of the world meet — and finds it, as you now know he would, in rather fewer places than one might expect.

I said that NAT would have to wait for the port numbers of the transport layer, and the transport layer is next week. It begins in October nineteen eighty-six, in Berkeley, California, between a laboratory on a hill and the university campus below it — two sites four hundred yards apart, joined by a link that on an ordinary day carried thirty-two thousand bits a second. That October, the rate fell to forty. Nothing was broken. No cable had been cut, and no machine had failed. Every machine on the network was doing exactly what the rules of the day told it to do, and those rules, obeyed faithfully by everybody at once, had brought the Internet very nearly to a standstill. How that happened, and the cure — a matter of manners, voluntarily observed to this day by billions of machines that have never met — is the third evening, and to my mind the most important of the five.
