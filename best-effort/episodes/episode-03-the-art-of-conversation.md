# Episode Three -- The Art of Conversation

*Best Effort -- A Remedial Course in Networking*

## Four Hundred Yards

Good day, sir.

In October of nineteen eighty-six, something went badly wrong with the Internet, and the first people to measure it were in Berkeley, California.

The Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory sits on a hill above the campus of the University of California. The two were connected by the network, of course, and the distance between the laboratory's machines and the university's was about four hundred yards -- a pleasant walk, a few minutes downhill. On a normal day, data flowed between them at about thirty-two thousand bits a second, which by the standards of nineteen eighty-six was perfectly respectable.

That October, the flow fell to forty bits a second.

Not forty thousand. Forty. A short message that should have crossed in a quarter of a second now took more than three minutes. Somebody could have walked the four hundred yards with the message written on a card, had a cup of tea at the far end, and walked back, in less time than the network took to carry it. The fall was a factor of eight hundred, and it was not a single bad afternoon. It was, as one of the men who investigated it wrote two years later, the first of what became a series of *congestion collapses*, and it was happening all over the Internet.

Here is the strangest part. The wires were not cut. The machines were not broken. If you had looked at the network during one of these collapses, you would have found it extremely busy -- links full, routers working flat out, packets everywhere. It was carrying an enormous amount of traffic. Almost none of it was useful. The network had filled itself with copies.

The man who wrote that account was Van Jacobson, then a researcher at the laboratory on the hill, and with a colleague at Berkeley, Mike Karels, he set out to understand what had happened. Their paper begins with the numbers I have just given you, and then says that they were fascinated by this sudden drop, and embarked on an investigation of why things had gotten so bad. In particular, they wondered whether the Berkeley version of TCP was misbehaving, or whether it could be tuned to work better under abysmal network conditions. *The answer to both of these questions*, they wrote, *was yes.*

What they found was a failure of manners.

Every computer on the Internet was running a protocol for conversation called TCP, and TCP had a sensible rule: if you send something and hear nothing back, assume it was lost, and send it again. Now picture a crowded room. A few people cannot hear one another over the noise, so they repeat themselves -- a little louder. That adds to the noise. So more people cannot hear, and they repeat themselves, and so on, until the whole room is shouting and nobody can hear anything at all. That is what happened to the Internet. The routers' queues filled, and packets were dropped. Every sender whose packet was dropped sent it again. The resent packets filled the queues further, so more packets were dropped, so more were resent. The network did not stop. It spent all its effort carrying second and third and fourth copies of letters, most of which would themselves be lost. The engineers had a name for this condition already, coined two years earlier by an engineer named John Nagle, and Nagle had added a sentence that still chills anyone who runs a network. *This condition*, he wrote, *is stable.* A network in collapse does not recover by itself. It stays collapsed.

Jacobson and Karels's cure was not a new machine or a faster wire. It was a code of conduct, written into TCP itself. A sender must not put a new packet into the network until an old one has left it. A sender must begin each conversation slowly, and feel its way. And a sender that hears of a loss must take it as a sign of a crowded room, and must lower its voice -- sharply, at once, and voluntarily. They put these rules into Berkeley's version of Unix in nineteen eighty-eight, and every other TCP in the world followed. Some of the most important changes were remarkably small; one of them, they noted, was one new variable and three lines of code.

The Internet has run on those manners ever since. Hold on to this, sir, because it is the most surprising fact of the evening, and I shall come back to it twice more before we are done: *every TCP connection you have ever opened has voluntarily slowed itself down whenever the network grew crowded.* Nobody polices it. No router enforces it. The network does not check. The whole Internet depends on billions of computers being polite at once, and on the whole they are.

That is tonight's subject. It is the third of our five questions: *did it arrive?* The network, you will remember from the first evening, promises nothing. It loses letters, delivers some twice, delivers them out of order, and says nothing about any of it. And yet, when your program writes bytes into a connection, the program at the other end reads exactly those bytes, in exactly that order -- or is told, eventually, that something has gone wrong. That miracle is manufactured entirely at the two ends, by TCP.

And the best way to understand TCP, I have come to think, is not as a piece of engineering but as a code of manners: a strict etiquette for a conversation between two strangers, conducted through a post that loses letters. It has five parts, and I shall give you them now, so that you can see the shape of the evening.

There are *introductions*, conducted with some formality, in which each party announces how it will number its remarks.

There is *acknowledgement*, so that each side always knows exactly what the other has heard, and can say again whatever was lost.

There is *turn-taking*, so that neither party speaks faster than the other can listen.

There is the rule against *shouting in a crowded room* -- the rule that saved the Internet in nineteen eighty-six.

And there is a proper way of saying *goodbye*, with a curious habit of lingering at the door afterwards, which you have certainly met, and very likely cursed, under the name of TIME-WAIT.

Introductions, acknowledgement, turn-taking, restraint, goodbye. Five manners. Hold them in mind, and the evening will hang on them.

Our route. We begin with doors and rooms: how one machine holds many conversations at once, the port numbers that make it possible, and the bare post that does nothing more, called UDP. Then the machinery of saying everything exactly once -- numbering, acknowledgement, timers, and the window -- with a revelation, at the end of that Part, about the sentence with which I close these evenings. Then introductions and farewells: the handshake, and the lingering at the door. Then the rules against speaking too fast -- to the listener, and to the network -- which is where we shall return to the hill above Berkeley and see exactly how the cure works. Then the box in your hall once more, which, I warned you last week, has been quietly rewriting your conversations, and which occasionally forgets them altogether. And finally, the silences: what each of the error messages you have ever seen from a network connection actually means, and the two clever ideas whose combination has cost the world a great many forty-millisecond pauses.

Ninety minutes, sir. Let us begin with the doors.

## Part One -- Doors and Rooms

Last week's whole effort was to get a packet to the right *machine*. The IP address on the envelope names a machine -- strictly, one of its network interfaces -- and with the routing table, the longest match, and the treaties between networks, the packet arrives there. But a machine is not a single listener. Your laptop at this moment is running a browser with a dozen tabs, a mail client, a chat program, something synchronising files, and very likely several programs you have never heard of, all talking to the network at once. When a packet arrives at the laptop, which of them is it for?

That is the transport layer's first job, before anything to do with reliability: to let one machine hold many conversations at once, and to deliver each arriving packet to the right one. The trade calls this *multiplexing*, which is a grand word for sorting the post within a household. And the device it uses is the **port number**.

A port number is a sixteen-bit number -- so it runs from zero to sixty-five thousand five hundred and thirty-five -- carried in the transport header, the first of the inner envelopes. If the IP address is the address of a building, the port number is the number of a flat within it. A program that wishes to receive conversations asks the operating system for a particular port, and from then on the operating system delivers to that program every packet that arrives for that port. Every transport header carries two port numbers, a source and a destination, just as the IP header carries two addresses: one saying which flat the letter is for, and one saying which flat the reply should go to.

Some ports are famous, by long convention. A web server listens on port eighty for plain HTTP and on port four forty-three for the encrypted kind -- so that when you type a web address, your browser knows which door to knock on without being told. Mail servers accept mail on port twenty-five. The Domain Name System answers questions on port fifty-three. The routers of last week, exchanging their routes by BGP, hold a TCP conversation with one another on port one seventy-nine -- a single conversation, between two neighbouring networks, that may stay open for months. And the remote shell you use to log in to servers listens on port twenty-two. The registry of these conventions is kept by IANA -- the same Internet Assigned Numbers Authority that kept the address registry -- which divides the range in three: the low ports, from zero to one thousand and twenty-three, called *system ports*, which on most operating systems only a privileged program may open; a middle range of *registered* ports, up to forty-nine thousand one hundred and fifty-one, which anybody may ask IANA to record for a protocol; and the top of the range, which is never assigned to anybody and is left free for temporary use.

That first rule -- that only a privileged program may open a port below one thousand and twenty-four -- explains one of your rituals, and it is older than most of your colleagues. On Unix systems, opening a low port requires the powers of the superuser. In the nineteen-eighties that was treated as a kind of credential: if a connection arrived *from* a low port, it must have been opened by a privileged program on the far machine, and so -- the reasoning went -- could be trusted a little more. It was a weak credential, and the trade long ago stopped relying on it; the fourth evening will show you what replaced it. Today the rule survives mainly as a nuisance. A program that wishes to listen on port eighty must either run with privileges it should not need, or be granted a special exemption. And so, by an overwhelming convention, the services you write and run in containers listen on port eight thousand and eighty, or three thousand, or eight thousand, and something in front of them -- a load balancer, a proxy, the container platform -- listens on the low, official port and passes the traffic along. You did not choose eight thousand and eighty for its beauty. You chose it, or somebody chose it for you, to avoid asking for the keys to the house in order to open the front door.

Port twenty-two has a story, and it is a small portrait of how the Internet used to be run. In the spring of nineteen ninety-five a researcher at the Helsinki University of Technology named Tatu Ylonen, alarmed by a password-sniffing attack on his university's network, wrote the first version of SSH, the *secure shell*, to replace the old remote-login programs, which sent passwords across the network unencrypted. He meant SSH to replace two older programs in particular: telnet, which used port twenty-three, and the file-transfer program, which used port twenty-one. Port twenty-two, between them, happened to be free. He thought, he wrote later, that having that number might be one of those small things that would give some aura of credibility. So he wrote to IANA to ask for it. IANA was, in practice, Jon Postel, whom Ylonen described as outright scary, having authored all the main Internet RFCs. The reply came the next day, from Postel's colleague Joyce Reynolds, dated the tenth of July, nineteen ninety-five, and I give it to you whole, because it is short: *Tatu, We have assigned port number twenty-two to ssh, with you as the point of contact. Joyce.* Every server you have ever logged into has been listening on the result.

Now, what about the other end? When your browser connects to a web server on port four forty-three, the server's end of the conversation is port four forty-three. But the browser's end needs a port too, so that the replies can be delivered to the right tab of the right program. That port is chosen by the operating system on the browser's behalf, automatically and temporarily, from a range set aside for the purpose. It is called an **ephemeral port**, because it lasts only as long as the conversation. On Linux the range runs, by default, from thirty-two thousand seven hundred and sixty-eight to sixty thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine -- about twenty-eight thousand ports -- a number I ask you to remember, because later this evening it is going to run out.

The program's handle on all of this is called a **socket**: the object a program holds that represents one end of a conversation, through which it reads and writes. A server's program creates a socket, binds it to its well-known port, and *listens* -- announces to the operating system that it will accept conversations arriving there. A client's program creates a socket and *connects* it to the server's address and port, and the operating system picks the ephemeral port and does the rest.

There is a subtlety on the server's side that is worth a moment, because it explains a good deal about how servers are built. The listening socket never itself carries a conversation. It is a receptionist. When a client's connection has been opened -- we shall see how in a little while -- the server's program asks the operating system to *accept* it, and is handed back a brand new socket for that one conversation, while the listening socket goes on listening at the door. A server with ten thousand clients has ten thousand and one sockets: one receptionist, and one for each guest. Between the moment a connection is opened and the moment the program gets round to accepting it, the connection waits in a queue belonging to the listening socket -- the *backlog* -- and that queue, like every queue in this course, has a length, and can fill.

And here is the question that puzzles everybody exactly once. A busy web server listens on a single port, four forty-three, and holds a hundred thousand conversations on it at the same moment. How does it keep them apart, if they are all arriving at the same door?

The answer is that a TCP conversation is identified not by one port but by five things together: the protocol, the source address, the source port, the destination address, and the destination port. The trade calls that the **five-tuple**, and every conversation in the world has a different one. A hundred thousand clients connecting to the same server on port four forty-three differ in their own addresses, or, if they share an address, in their ephemeral ports. So the operating system, receiving a packet, looks at all five values together and finds the one conversation they name. Two conversations from your laptop to the same server differ only in your ephemeral port; that is enough. The door is shared. The conversations behind it are not.

Let me make that concrete, with your own laptop as the cast. Your laptop, at one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty, opens two tabs to the same web server, at two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot ten, port four forty-three. For the first tab, the operating system picks an ephemeral port -- say, fifty-one thousand and one. For the second, fifty-one thousand and two. So the first conversation is: TCP, from one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty port fifty-one thousand and one, to two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot ten port four forty-three. And the second is the same in every respect but one: the port is fifty-one thousand and two. When a reply arrives addressed to port fifty-one thousand and two, the laptop knows it belongs to the second tab. When a request arrives at the server from port fifty-one thousand and one, the server knows it belongs to the first conversation, and hands it to the socket it accepted for that conversation. One differing number is all it takes.

You will meet the five-tuple again and again, because nearly everything in the middle of a network that remembers anything remembers it by five-tuple: the box in your hall that shares one address among your household; the firewall that lets replies in but not strangers; the load balancer that keeps each conversation on the same server. When an engineer says that two packets belong to the same *flow*, the five-tuple is what is meant.

Before we build TCP's elaborate machinery, let us look at the transport protocol that has almost none, because it is the control in the experiment, and because you depend on it more than you might think.

It is called the **User Datagram Protocol**, UDP, and its specification is RFC seven sixty-eight, written by Jon Postel and dated the twenty-eighth of August, nineteen eighty. It runs to three pages. That is not a figure of speech; the document's last page is numbered three. UDP adds to IP exactly four small things, in a header of eight bytes: a source port and a destination port, so that the datagram reaches the right program; a length; and a checksum, so that damage can be detected -- a checksum which, with a casualness the period permitted, the sender was allowed to leave out, signalling as much by sending zeros. And that is all. The specification says so plainly: the protocol is transaction oriented, and delivery and duplicate protection are not guaranteed.

So UDP is IP's own best effort, with flat numbers added. A program sends a datagram; it arrives, or it does not; it arrives once, or twice; it arrives before or after the one sent a moment later. Nothing is numbered, nothing is acknowledged, nothing is retransmitted, and nobody is told.

Who would want such a thing? More people than you might think, and for three different reasons.

The first reason is that some exchanges are so small that a conversation would cost more than the content. When your laptop asks a resolver for the address of a name, the question fits in one packet and the answer fits in another. Opening a TCP connection first would cost a full round trip of introductions before the question could even be asked. With UDP, the laptop simply sends the question, and if no answer comes within a second or so, it sends the question again. For one question and one answer, a retry is cheaper than a handshake. That is why the Domain Name System has run over UDP since its birth.

The second reason is the one Danny Cohen made in the nineteen-seventies, which split TCP from IP in the first place: some data is worth more late than never, and some is worth nothing late at all. A video call, a voice call, a game reporting where the other players are standing -- for all of these, a lost packet is better skipped than resent, because by the time the resent copy arrived the moment would be gone. Such programs use UDP, and deal with loss in their own way: by smoothing over it, by sending a little redundancy, by simply carrying on.

And the third reason is the most interesting: UDP is a blank page on which a program may write its own transport. Because UDP does almost nothing, a program that wants different manners from TCP's can build them on top, at the ends, without asking the operating system or the network for anything. The most important modern example is a protocol called QUIC -- spelled Q-U-I-C, and pronounced *quick* -- designed at Google and now standardised, which builds its own reliability, ordering, congestion control, and encryption on top of UDP, and which now carries a large share of the web's traffic. Why anybody would rebuild TCP from scratch, and what was wrong with the old one, is a question we shall be able to answer properly in the fifth evening, once we have seen tonight what TCP does and where it pinches.

So: an IP address names a machine, and a port names a program on it; a socket is a program's handle on one end of a conversation; and a conversation is known to everything that tracks it by its five-tuple. UDP adds ports and a checksum to IP and nothing else -- a post that delivers letters to the right flat, and makes no other promise. Everything TCP promises, it must manufacture at the two ends from that bare service. Let us watch it do so.

## Part Two -- Saying Everything Once

Here is what TCP promises a program. You may write bytes into your end of a connection, as many as you like, in pieces of whatever size you please. The program at the other end will read exactly those bytes, every one, in exactly the order you wrote them, each exactly once. Or, if that becomes impossible -- if the far machine dies, or the network is cut for long enough -- you will be told, eventually, that the connection has failed. That is the whole contract: a **reliable byte stream**. No lost bytes, no doubled bytes, no shuffled bytes.

Notice what it does not say. It says nothing about packets. Your program writes a stream of bytes, and TCP decides for itself how to cut the stream into segments for the network; the program at the other end reads a stream of bytes, and has no idea where the segment boundaries fell. If you write a hundred bytes and then another hundred, the far end may read them as two hundred at once, or as fifty and a hundred and fifty. The stream has no seams. A good many bugs in a good many network programs have come from forgetting that.

It is also the reason for a habit of every protocol you use, which you may never have noticed as a habit. Because TCP delivers a seamless stream, every protocol built on it must mark for itself where one message ends and the next begins. The old protocols of the Internet end each line with a line break, and each message with a blank line. HTTP announces, in its headers, how many bytes of body are to follow, so that the receiver knows when to stop reading. The remote-procedure-call frameworks you use between services put a small length in front of every message. Each of them is drawing, by hand, the seams that TCP refuses to keep. The trade calls this *framing*, and if you ever write a protocol of your own, it will be the first thing you have to decide.

And the network beneath, remember, loses packets, duplicates them, reorders them, and occasionally damages them. So TCP must build its promise out of exactly the materials the network denies it. It does so with a small set of tools, each simple, each answering one of the network's failings. I shall hand you them one at a time, in the order you would invent them if you sat down to the problem yourself.

The first tool answers *reordering* and *duplication*: number everything. TCP gives every byte in the stream a number -- not every packet, mark you, every *byte* -- called its **sequence number**. Each segment carries, in its header, the sequence number of the first byte it contains. So if a segment arrives saying *these are bytes four thousand and one to five thousand*, the receiver knows exactly where they belong in the stream, regardless of when they arrived or what arrived before them. A segment that arrives early is held until the gap before it is filled. A segment that arrives twice is recognised by its numbers and the second copy thrown away. Numbering alone defeats reordering and duplication completely.

The numbering does not start at zero, incidentally. Each side chooses its own starting number at the beginning of the conversation -- the **initial sequence number** -- and announces it as part of the introductions, which are the subject of the next Part.

The second tool answers *loss*, or rather lets the sender discover it: the receiver acknowledges what it has received. Every segment the receiver sends back carries an **acknowledgement number**, and the acknowledgement number has a particular and very clever meaning. It is not *I have received the segment you just sent*. It is *I have received every byte before this number, and this is the number of the next byte I expect.* An acknowledgement of five thousand and one means: *everything up to and including byte five thousand has arrived, in order, without gaps.* That is called a **cumulative acknowledgement**, and its cleverness lies in being robust to its own loss. If one acknowledgement goes missing, the next one says everything it said and more. The receiver never needs to repeat itself, because each statement it makes contains all the previous ones.

The third tool answers loss directly: keep a copy, and send it again if nobody acknowledges it. The sender holds on to every byte it has sent until those bytes are acknowledged. And it keeps a timer. If an acknowledgement does not arrive within a certain time, the sender assumes the segment was lost and sends it again. That is **retransmission**, and the time it waits is called the **retransmission timeout**.

How long should that be? Here, in a small question, is a large difficulty. Wait too long, and every loss costs you a long silence. Wait too little, and you resend segments that were not lost at all, only slow -- adding traffic to a network that may already be struggling, which, as we shall see in Part Four, is how the Internet once nearly died. And the right answer differs from one conversation to another by a factor of a thousand: a connection within a data centre has a round trip of tens of microseconds, and one to Perth has a round trip of two hundred milliseconds or more. No fixed timeout can be right.

So TCP *measures*. It times how long acknowledgements actually take to come back, conversation by conversation, and keeps a running estimate of both the typical round trip and how much it varies; and it sets its timeout at the typical figure plus a generous multiple of the variation. That refinement -- paying attention to the variation, not merely the average -- was one of Van Jacobson's contributions in nineteen eighty-eight, and we shall meet him properly shortly. There are two further refinements worth knowing. When a segment has been sent twice, and an acknowledgement then arrives, the sender cannot tell which copy is being acknowledged, so it ignores that measurement altogether; that rule bears the name of the engineer who pointed it out, Phil Karn. And when a retransmission is itself not acknowledged, the sender doubles its timeout before trying again, and doubles it again after that: the exponential backoff you met on the Ethernet cable last week, here applied to a conversation. Before a connection has measured anything at all, the standard says to begin with a timeout of one second -- it used to be three -- and Linux, which likes to be brisk, will let a measured timeout go as low as a fifth of a second.

The fourth tool answers the speed of light. If the sender sent one segment and then waited for its acknowledgement before sending the next, every segment would cost a full round trip, and a conversation with London would crawl along at one segment every ninety milliseconds -- the long, wide pipe of the first evening, almost entirely empty. So the sender is allowed to have a certain amount of data *in flight* at once -- sent but not yet acknowledged. That amount is called the **window**. As acknowledgements arrive for the oldest bytes, the window slides forward over the stream, and the sender may send that much more new data. The arrangement is called a **sliding window**, and the picture to hold is just that: a frame laid over the stream of bytes, marking the portion currently in the air. Behind the frame, bytes sent and acknowledged; within it, bytes sent and awaiting acknowledgement, or permitted to be sent; ahead of it, bytes not yet allowed out. How big the window should be -- and who gets to decide -- is the subject of Part Four, and the most consequential question in the protocol.

Now let me walk the four tools working together, through a loss, because the dance is a pretty one and it has a final step that will surprise you.

The cast: a sender and a receiver, and six segments, each of a thousand bytes, which I shall call one to six. The sender's window is large enough to send all six without waiting.

First: the sender sends segments one through six, back to back.

Second: segment one arrives. The receiver acknowledges: *next, please, byte one thousand and one*.

Third: segment two is lost. A queue somewhere was full.

Fourth: segment three arrives. The receiver holds it -- it belongs after a gap -- and acknowledges again. But what can it say? Its acknowledgement can only state the next byte it expects in order, and that is still one thousand and one. So it says, once more: *next, please, byte one thousand and one*. That is called a **duplicate acknowledgement**.

Fifth: segments four, five, and six arrive, and each provokes the same reply: *one thousand and one*. The sender has now received four identical acknowledgements -- the original and three duplicates.

Now the final step. The sender could simply wait for its retransmission timer to fire. But it can reason faster than that. A duplicate acknowledgement means that *something* arrived at the receiver, but something after a gap. Three of them in a row mean that three later segments have got through, while the one at byte one thousand and one has not. A segment merely delayed would have turned up by now, so the sender concludes that segment two was lost, and resends it at once, without waiting for the timer. That is called **fast retransmit**, and the rule of three duplicates is one of the small constants on which the Internet runs.

Sixth: the resent segment two arrives. The gap is filled. The receiver now holds, in order, everything from byte one to byte six thousand, and its next acknowledgement says so in a single statement: *next, please, byte six thousand and one*. The cumulative acknowledgement swallows the whole episode in one number.

A practical note, for the day you open a packet capture and try to follow this dance yourself. Because each side starts its numbering at an arbitrary point, the real sequence numbers are enormous and meaningless -- nine or ten digits apiece. So the usual capture tools quietly subtract each side's starting number and show you *relative* sequence numbers, beginning from zero or one, so that you can read the conversation as I have just described it: one thousand and one, two thousand and one, and so on. If you ever see a capture with ten-digit sequence numbers in it, the tool has merely been told to show you the truth, and the truth is less readable.

There is a later refinement, which you will see named in packet captures: **selective acknowledgement**, SACK, said as a word. With it, the receiver can say not only *I am still waiting for byte one thousand and one*, but also *and, for your information, I already hold bytes two thousand and one to six thousand*. The sender then knows precisely which pieces are missing and resends only those, which matters a great deal when several segments are lost from one window. It was standardised in October nineteen ninety-six and is now used nearly everywhere.

I am told, sir, that the same protocol is in force in your house at bedtime. Miss Xenia, who turns five on the fourteenth of this month, as nobody in the household is permitted to forget, is read a story. She is a receiver of the most exacting kind. Attempt to turn two pages at once, in the hope of reaching the end a little sooner, and she will notice the gap at once -- *you missed the bit where the bear finds the honey* -- and will decline to acknowledge anything further until the missing segment has been retransmitted. Her acknowledgements, I understand, are cumulative: she knows exactly how far the story has got, and states it with authority. The only respect in which she departs from the specification is that TCP does not require the sender to read the whole story again from the beginning afterwards, by way of penalty.

There is one more failing of the network that TCP must handle: *damage*. A packet's bits may be corrupted on the way. Every TCP segment carries a checksum -- a small number computed from the contents, which the receiver recomputes and compares; if they disagree, the segment is thrown away and, in due course, retransmitted. But TCP's checksum is a modest thing, sixteen bits of simple arithmetic designed in an era when processors were slow, and I promised you in the first evening some figures that would make you thoughtful. Here they are. In two thousand, two researchers, Jonathan Stone and Craig Partridge, examined hundreds of thousands of damaged packets captured on real networks. They found that between one packet in eleven hundred and one in thirty-two thousand failed the TCP checksum -- on links whose own error-checking should have caught all but one error in four billion. The damage, in other words, was being done not on the wires but inside the machines: in memory, in buggy drivers, in the routers themselves, between one checksum and the next, exactly as the end-to-end argument predicted. And they estimated that the TCP checksum itself would fail to catch damage in something like one packet in sixteen million to one in ten billion. One in ten billion sounds like never, until you remember how many packets a busy service sends in a day. Their advice was the end-to-end argument in one sentence: some applications should use checksums of their own. When your storage service insists on a hash of your object, now you know why.

So there are the tools: number every byte; acknowledge cumulatively; keep a copy and resend on a timer that learns the round trip; keep a window of data in flight; resend early on three duplicate acknowledgements; check every segment for damage. From those, at the ends, TCP builds a perfect byte stream out of an imperfect post.

And now, sir, I can pay a debt. I have closed each of these evenings with a borrowed sentence, and promised to tell you whose it was when we reached the specification it was written into. This is that specification.

TCP is defined in a document of September nineteen eighty-one, RFC seven ninety-three. Its editor was Jon Postel -- the young man we met in the first evening at UCLA, editing the Requests for Comments, who went on editing them for twenty-eight years, and who also, from a modest office at a university institute in Marina del Rey, on the coast just south of Santa Monica, kept the Internet's registry of numbers, its ports and its addresses: the man Tatu Ylonen found outright scary. Section two point ten of that document is headed *Robustness Principle*, and it reads, in full: *TCP implementations will follow a general principle of robustness: be conservative in what you do, be liberal in what you accept from others.*

That is the sentence. Postel had written it, in slightly different words, twice before, in drafts of IP and TCP from January nineteen eighty, and he put it into the final IP specification too. Later engineers turned it round into the form I have been using -- *be liberal in what you accept, and conservative in what you send* -- and it came to be known simply as *Postel's law*. Its meaning, for a protocol, is this. When you *send*, follow the specification to the letter: never emit anything that another implementation might misunderstand. When you *receive*, be forgiving: if another implementation sends you something slightly odd, but whose meaning is clear, accept it and carry on rather than refusing it. In nineteen eighty-one, with dozens of independent TCP implementations written from a prose specification by people at different institutions who had never met, that forgiveness was what made them able to talk to one another at all. It is a large part of why the Internet worked.

Postel died in October nineteen ninety-eight, at the age of fifty-five, after heart surgery. Vint Cerf wrote his memorial as an RFC -- it is number twenty-four sixty-eight, *I Remember IANA* -- and it contains the best description of him I know: *He could have outwaited the Sphinx in a staring contest, I think.* Cerf also remembered him at UCLA, *bearded and sandaled*, as their *resident hippie-patriarch*. The year before, The Economist had written that if the Net had a god, he was probably Jon Postel, and Postel had answered, in a magazine interview, that of course there isn't any God of the Internet; the Internet works because a lot of people cooperate to do things together. I can think of no better gloss on the robustness principle than that answer. Cerf's memorial, being an RFC, has a section on security considerations, as every RFC must. It reads: *Security issues are not relevant to this Remembrance.*

Whether the principle was right -- whether liberality in what you accept is a virtue in a protocol, or a slow poison -- is a question on which the trade has changed its mind since Postel's death, and on which I shall give you my own verdict at the end of the course. For tonight, it is enough that you know whose voice it is at the end of each evening, and that it was written into the heart of TCP.

## Part Three -- How Do You Do, and Goodbye

Every TCP conversation begins with a formal introduction, and the introduction has three movements. It is called the **three-way handshake**, and your machine performs it every single time it opens a connection to anything. Let me walk it slowly, with the cast announced: a *client*, which wishes to talk, and a *server*, which is listening.

First movement. The client sends a segment with a flag set in its header called *SYN* -- short for *synchronise*, and said *sin*. With it goes the client's chosen starting number for its side of the conversation. Call that number X. In plain words: *may I speak with you? My remarks will be numbered from X.*

Second movement. The server, if it is willing, replies with a segment that carries two flags at once. A SYN of its own, announcing the server's starting number -- call it Y. And an ACK, acknowledging the client's number: the acknowledgement says X plus one, meaning *I have heard you up to X; I expect X plus one next*. In plain words: *you may. I heard that your numbering begins at X. Mine begins at Y.* The trade calls this segment the *SYN-ACK*.

Third movement. The client replies with an ACK of Y plus one. In plain words: *I heard that yours begins at Y.* And now the connection is open, on both sides, and data may flow.

SYN; SYN-ACK; ACK. *May I?* -- *you may, and may I?* -- *you may.* That is the handshake, and it is worth saying once more in the trade's own words, because you will hear them in every packet capture you ever read: the client sends a SYN, the server answers with a SYN-ACK, the client completes it with an ACK.

Why three movements, and not two? Because each side must announce its own starting number *and* hear the other side confirm it. After two movements, the client knows that the server heard its number. But the server does not yet know that the client heard the server's. The third movement closes that loop. Three is the smallest number of messages in which two parties can each announce something and each learn that the other has heard it -- a piece of etiquette any diplomat would recognise.

And why choose a fresh starting number for each conversation, rather than simply counting from zero every time? Housekeeping, chiefly. The network, you will remember, can delay a packet for a surprisingly long time. Suppose a conversation between two programs ends, and a moment later a new one begins between the very same two addresses and the very same two ports. A straggling packet from the old conversation might arrive during the new one. If both conversations numbered their bytes from zero, the straggler's numbers might fit neatly into the new conversation, and be accepted as part of it -- a letter from last week filed in this week's correspondence. Starting each conversation at a fresh, unpredictable point in the numbering makes that kind of accident vanishingly unlikely. Modern operating systems derive the starting number from a secret value mixed with the conversation's addresses and ports, as the standards now require.

Now, notice what the handshake *costs*: one full round trip before the client may send a single byte of its request. Ninety milliseconds to London. Nearly two hundred to Perth. And paid again for every new connection. That is the first of the round trips we counted together in the first evening, and it is the reason for a ritual you perform every day, probably without thinking of it: *re-using connections*. A connection pool in your service, keep-alive in your HTTP client -- these exist, first of all, so that the introductions are made once and not a thousand times. I shall give you the second reason, which is even better, in the next Part. For now, the plain fact, which bears repeating: every new TCP connection costs a round trip before anything useful happens.

Let me show you how that compounds, because in a system of many services it compounds quickly. Suppose a request to your front door is handled by one service, which calls a second, which calls a third, and suppose -- as careless code often does -- each of them opens a fresh connection for every call. Then a single request pays for three handshakes, one at each hop, before the third service has heard a word of the question. Inside one data centre, where a round trip takes a fraction of a millisecond, that is a tax you might never notice. Put one of those services in another region, a continent away, and each handshake to it costs tens of milliseconds; add the encryption negotiation of the fourth evening, which costs another round trip on top, and a careless chain can spend a quarter of a second on introductions alone. Connection pools exist so that each of those introductions is made once, at start-up, rather than once per request.

Let me now take you behind the server's counter, because there is a queue there, and queues, as you know by now, are where trouble lives.

When a client's SYN arrives, the server's operating system answers it with the SYN-ACK and makes a note of the half-finished introduction. When the client's final ACK arrives, the introduction is complete, and the connection is placed in a queue, waiting for the server's program to come and accept it -- the receptionist of the first Part, handing each new guest a socket of its own. That waiting line is the **backlog**: the queue of connections that are fully open but not yet accepted by the program.

If the program is slow to accept -- because it is busy, or blocked, or simply has too few threads -- the backlog fills. And when it is full, new connections are refused or ignored until there is room. The symptom is distinctive: under a burst of new connections, some clients connect instantly and others wait a second or more, or fail, while the server itself looks only moderately busy. For many years the maximum length of that queue on Linux was a hundred and twenty-eight, a figure fixed in the nineteen-nineties. When it was finally raised to four thousand and ninety-six, in two thousand and nineteen, the engineer who made the change, at Google, remarked in passing that Google had been using a thousand and twenty-four internally for at least fifteen years. So if you have ever been told to *raise the backlog*, or to increase a setting with the name *somaxconn*, and wondered what you were raising: it was the length of the line of guests waiting in the hall for a receptionist who could not keep up.

So much for introductions. Now farewells.

A TCP conversation has two directions, and each direction is closed separately. When one side has finished speaking, it sends a segment with a flag called **FIN** -- *finish*, said *fin*. It means: *I have said all I have to say.* The other side acknowledges it. But the other side may still have things to say, and may go on saying them; a connection with one direction closed and the other still open is called *half-closed*, and it is perfectly proper. When the second side has finished in its turn, it sends its own FIN, and the first side acknowledges that. So a complete farewell takes four messages: *I have finished* -- *noted* -- *so have I* -- *noted*. FIN, ACK, FIN, ACK.

And now the lingering at the door.

The side that sent the *first* FIN -- the side that began the farewell -- does not simply forget the conversation when the farewells are complete. It waits. It keeps a small record of the conversation for a fixed period, and during that period it will not allow a new conversation with the very same five-tuple -- the same two addresses and the same two ports. That waiting state is called **TIME-WAIT**, and the original specification set its length at twice the longest time a packet might plausibly survive in the network. It defined that longest time, with a candour I have always admired, as *arbitrarily defined to be two minutes*. So TIME-WAIT was four minutes. Linux, being practical, fixes it at sixty seconds, and does not let you change it.

Why linger at all? For two reasons, and they are both the post's fault.

The first reason: the very last message of the farewell -- the final *noted* -- might be lost. If it is, the other side will never hear that its own FIN was received, and will send the FIN again. The lingering side must still be there to answer it politely. If it had already forgotten the conversation, it would answer the repeated FIN with bafflement -- a reset, the protocol's equivalent of slamming the door -- and the other side would record an error for a conversation that had in fact ended perfectly well.

The second reason: stragglers. A packet from this conversation might still be wandering in the network. If a brand new conversation began at once between the same two addresses and ports, the straggler might arrive and be mistaken for part of it. By waiting until any straggler must have expired, the lingering side makes sure the old conversation is truly dead before its name is reused.

So TIME-WAIT is the polite guest who, having said goodbye at the door, lingers on the step for a minute -- in case the host calls something after them, and so that nobody confuses that departure with the next arrival.

The courtesy has a cost, and you have very likely paid it. Here is the ritual. Every connection in TIME-WAIT holds on to its five-tuple, and in particular to the *client's* ephemeral port, for the full sixty seconds. You will remember that Linux has about twenty-eight thousand ephemeral ports. Now suppose a client -- one of your services, say -- opens a fresh connection to the same server for every single request, and closes it afterwards. Each closed connection leaves a port lingering at the door for a minute. Twenty-eight thousand ports, each held for sixty seconds, means that the client can open at most about four hundred and seventy new connections a second to that one server, and no more. Exceed it, and the operating system simply runs out of ports: the next attempt to connect fails at once, with an error that reads *cannot assign requested address*. One Linux developer, testing exactly this, recorded a test program failing after thirty-two thousand-odd connections in twenty-four seconds.

The cure that people reach for first is a setting with the name *TCP time-wait reuse* -- written in lower case, with underscores between the words -- which permits a new outgoing connection to take over a lingering one's name when the protocol can tell it is safe to do so. It is a reasonable setting. Its notorious sibling, *TCP time-wait recycle*, which was far more aggressive, is a cautionary tale: it misbehaved badly for clients that shared an address behind a NAT -- letting only one machine in an office connect at a time -- and was removed from Linux altogether in two thousand and seventeen. If you find it in an old runbook, you may take pleasure in deleting it.

But the real cure is the ritual you already perform: *re-use your connections*. A client that keeps a pool of open connections and sends many requests down each one never runs out of ports, because it hardly ever closes anything. That is the second time this Part has sent you to the connection pool, and it will not be the last: the handshake's round trip, and the ports lingering at the door, are two separate reasons for the same habit.

There is a cousin of this ritual on the server's side. You stop a server and start it again at once, and it refuses to start, complaining that its *address is already in use*. Nothing else is listening; you can see that nothing else is listening. What is holding the port is the old server's conversations, lingering in TIME-WAIT. The remedy, which nearly every server framework now applies for you, is a socket option called *reuse-address* -- written with the letters S and O and an underscore in front -- which tells the operating system that the new listener may take the port despite the lingerers on the step.

And there is one more way for a conversation to end, which is not a farewell at all. A segment with a flag called **reset** -- written RST, and spoken simply as *reset* -- means: *this conversation does not exist, as far as I am concerned. Stop.* A machine sends a reset when a segment arrives for a conversation it knows nothing about: a request to a port where nothing is listening, or a packet for a conversation it has already forgotten. A program can also abort a conversation deliberately with a reset, instead of the four-message farewell. Where a FIN is a polite goodbye, a reset is the door slammed. You will meet resets again before the evening is out, in the last Part, because a great many of the error messages you have seen are simply reports that one arrived.

So, the manners of beginning and ending. Introductions take three movements -- SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK -- in which each side announces the number its remarks will begin from and hears the other confirm it; and they cost a full round trip before any work is done. Connections that are open but not yet accepted wait in the backlog, which can fill. Farewells take four movements, one FIN and one acknowledgement in each direction; the side that said goodbye first lingers in TIME-WAIT for a minute, holding its port; and a reset ends a conversation without ceremony. And the two costs at the beginning and the end -- the round trip of the handshake, and the port held at the door -- both send you to the same remedy: keep your connections, and use them again.

## Part Four -- Not So Fast

There are two ways for a sender to talk too fast, and TCP has a separate manner for each. It can talk faster than the *listener* can take in -- that is turn-taking. And it can talk faster than the *room* can carry -- that is restraint, and that is the one that nearly ended the Internet. Let us take the listener first, because it is simpler, and then go back up the hill to Berkeley.

The listener's problem is plain. The receiving program reads data from its connection at whatever pace suits it, and until it reads, the operating system must hold the arrived data in a buffer. Buffers are finite. If the sender keeps sending while the receiving program is busy elsewhere, the buffer fills, and anything further would have to be thrown away. So the receiver tells the sender, in every acknowledgement it sends, how much room it has left. That figure is called the **receive window**. *I have room for so many more bytes.* The sender promises never to have more unacknowledged data in flight than the receiver last said it had room for. If the receiver says *no room at all*, the sender stops, and waits, and every so often sends a tiny probe to ask whether room has appeared. That is **flow control**, and it is the manners of a good conversationalist: you do not keep talking at someone who has stopped listening.

Now, the receive window was given a field in the TCP header sixteen bits wide, which means it could announce at most sixty-five thousand five hundred and thirty-five bytes of room -- sixty-four kilobytes, near enough. You may recall from the first evening what that does to a long, wide pipe. A gigabit link to London, with a round trip of eighty milliseconds, needs ten megabytes in flight to stay full; a sender allowed only sixty-four kilobytes in flight fills less than one percent of the pipe at a time, waits for the acknowledgements to come back, and achieves about six and a half megabits a second on a link that could carry a thousand. In nineteen eighty-one nobody had a gigabit link to London. By the early nineteen-nineties the problem was visible, and in May nineteen ninety-two Van Jacobson and two colleagues published the fix: an extension, agreed during the handshake, by which both sides may multiply the window by a power of two -- up to about sixteen thousand times -- giving windows of up to a gigabyte. Their document called such a path a *long, fat pipe*, and a network containing one a *long, fat network* -- abbreviated, with a gravity I can only applaud, to L-F-N, *pronounced elephant*. It is called **window scaling**, and every modern TCP uses it.

That is your first ritual of this Part. When a colleague tells you that a transfer across an ocean is slow and the cure is to *raise the socket buffer sizes*, this is what is being raised: the room the receiver can advertise, and the amount the sender is willing to keep in flight. On a long, fat pipe, the buffers must be at least as large as the bandwidth multiplied by the round trip -- the bandwidth-delay product of the first evening -- or the pipe cannot be kept full however fast the link. Turn-taking, in short: the receive window tells the sender how fast the listener can listen; window scaling lets that window be large enough for a long pipe; and the buffers behind it must be large enough to match.

Now the room.

Flow control protects the receiver. It does nothing whatever to protect the network in between. A receiver with an enormous buffer will happily advertise an enormous window, and a sender honouring it will pour data into the network as fast as its own link allows -- regardless of whether some router in the middle, on a slower link shared with thousands of other conversations, can possibly carry it. And that, in nineteen eighty-six, is exactly what happened.

Let me tell the collapse again, now that you have the machinery. A router somewhere in the middle has more packets arriving for a link than the link can send, so its queue fills, and it drops packets. Each sender whose packet was dropped waits for its retransmission timer, and sends the packet again -- and in nineteen eighty-six, the timers were crude, and often fired too soon, so they also resent packets that had not been lost at all, merely delayed in the long queue. Every resent packet adds to the load on the router that was already overloaded. So more packets are dropped. So more are resent. The network stays busier than ever while delivering less and less, until four hundred yards takes three minutes. Everybody in the room, unable to hear, has started shouting, and the shouting is why nobody can hear.

Jacobson and Karels's insight was to ask what a well-behaved conversation looks like when everything is working. Imagine a conversation that has been running for a while, with a full window of data flowing. Each time a packet reaches the far end, the receiver sends back an acknowledgement. Those acknowledgements come back to the sender spaced out exactly as the packets were spaced out when they squeezed through the slowest link on the path -- the *bottleneck*. So the stream of returning acknowledgements is a clock, ticking at precisely the rate the network can deliver. The principle they drew from it they called the **conservation of packets**, and they put it in one sentence: *a new packet isn't put into the network until an old packet leaves.* The sender waits for an acknowledgement -- proof that a packet has left the network at the far end -- before it sends a new one. The acknowledgements pace the sender, and the network itself sets the pace. Engineers call it the *ack clock*, and it is the heartbeat of every TCP connection you have.

The conservation principle tells a sender how to behave once it is running at the right rate. Two further rules tell it how to *find* the right rate, and what to do when it has overshot.

To hold its estimate, the sender keeps a second window of its own, alongside the receiver's. It is called the **congestion window**, and it is the sender's private guess at how much data the network between them can bear in flight without overflowing. The sender then obeys whichever window is smaller: the receiver's, which says how much the listener can take; and its own congestion window, which says how much the room can take. Two windows: one for the listener, one for the room.

The first rule is how a conversation *begins*. The sender cannot know the network's capacity in advance, so it starts with a small congestion window, and probes. That starting window is now, by a standard of two thousand and thirteen, ten segments -- about fourteen kilobytes. For every acknowledgement that comes back, the sender enlarges the window by one segment. Work out what that means: if ten segments go out and ten acknowledgements come back, the window becomes twenty; the twenty bring back twenty acknowledgements, and the window becomes forty. The window *doubles* every round trip. This is called **slow start**, which is one of the great misnomers of the trade, since doubling every round trip is anything but slow. It is slow only by comparison with what came before it, which was to start at full speed and hope.

And now I can give you the second reason for re-using connections, which I promised you in the last Part. Let us count. A fresh connection to London, eighty milliseconds away, sends about fourteen kilobytes in its first round trip; twenty-nine in its second; fifty-eight in its third; and so on, doubling. To deliver one megabyte from a standing start takes about seven round trips -- more than half a second -- on top of the round trip for the handshake. A connection that has been in use for a while has already opened its window wide, and can send the same megabyte in a round trip or two. That is why the first request on a new connection is so often slow, and the tenth on the same connection fast. Introductions cost a round trip; slow start costs several more. A warm connection has paid both already.

There is a wrinkle in that, and it is a ritual you may have seen in a tuning guide without an explanation. A connection that has been idle for a while does not stay warm. TCP reasons, sensibly enough, that if it has said nothing for some time, the state of the network may have changed while it was silent, and its old congestion window may no longer be safe. So, by default, Linux shrinks the window of a connection that has been idle for more than about one round-trip timeout back toward its starting size, and the next burst of data must go through slow start again. For a long-lived connection that carries traffic in bursts -- a stream of messages, say, between two services in different regions -- that can mean paying the slow-start tax over and over. Hence the setting, with a long name about *slow start after idle*, that the tuning guides tell you to switch off for exactly such connections. You are telling TCP that the room has not changed while you were quiet, and that it may resume at the volume it had reached.

The second rule is what to do in the middle of a conversation, and it is the one that saved the Internet. Once a sender has grown its window to a sensible size, it carries on growing it, but gently -- by one segment for each round trip, rather than doubling. And the moment it detects a loss, it takes the loss as a sign of a crowded room, and *halves* its window at once. Add a little, steadily; cut by half, suddenly. The trade calls it **additive increase, multiplicative decrease**, shortened to AIMD, and if you draw the size of the window over time you get a sawtooth: a long gentle slope up, a sudden drop by half, a long gentle slope up again. Every TCP conversation on Earth is drawing that sawtooth, or something like it, at this moment.

Why halve, and why add only a little? Because of a beautiful small result, published in nineteen eighty-nine by two researchers at Digital Equipment, Dah-Ming Chiu and Raj Jain. They showed that among all the simple rules a sender might follow, additive increase with multiplicative decrease is the one that drives competing senders not only to use the network fully, but to share it *fairly* -- whatever state they start in. Let me give you the reason in words, because it is lovely. Imagine two conversations sharing one link, one with a large window and one with a small. While the link has room, each adds one segment per round trip: they grow by the same amount, so the *difference* between them stays the same. When the link overflows, each halves its window: and halving both windows halves the *difference* between them too. So with every cycle of growth and loss, the gap between them shrinks -- unchanged on the way up, halved on the way down -- until the two are sharing the link equally. Fairness, arrived at by arithmetic, with no referee and no conversation between the two senders at all.

So here is the shape of restraint, and it is the most important mechanism of the evening, so let me say it plainly once more. A TCP sender starts small and doubles its window every round trip to find the network's capacity; then grows it gently; and when it hears of a loss, it halves its window, voluntarily, at once. Billions of conversations do this, each knowing nothing of the others, and the result is a network that stays full without collapsing, and shares itself roughly fairly among everybody using it.

The restraint has a consequence that you will have met as a ritual, perhaps without knowing it. Because TCP treats every loss as a sign of congestion, and halves its window each time, its throughput over a long path depends heavily on how often packets are lost. There is a well-known formula for the classic algorithm, worked out by Matt Mathis and colleagues in nineteen ninety-seven, and I shall give it to you in words rather than symbols: the throughput is roughly the segment size divided by the round-trip time, divided again by the square root of the loss rate. Put numbers to it for a connection from Toronto to London, eighty milliseconds away. Lose one packet in a hundred, and the classic algorithm can manage under two megabits a second, however fast the link. Lose one in ten thousand, and it rises to about eighteen. Lose one in a million, and it reaches about a hundred and eighty -- still a fraction of a gigabit link. Long round trips and a little loss make a slow TCP connection, and no amount of bandwidth helps.

And there is the ritual: your cloud provider's copying tools, when they move large files between regions or to a bucket on another continent, open not one connection but many -- dozens, sometimes -- and send pieces of the file down each in parallel. You may have been told to turn up the parallelism to make the copy faster, and it worked. Now you know why. Each connection's throughput is capped by its round trip and the path's loss rate; twenty connections, each with its own congestion window, can carry nearly twenty times as much. It is a slightly impolite trick -- twenty voices in the room instead of one -- and it is universal.

The algorithm has been refined many times since nineteen eighty-eight, and two of the refinements are worth naming, because you will see them in configuration files. The first is called **CUBIC**, devised by researchers at North Carolina State University in the middle of the two-thousands, the default congestion control in Linux since two thousand and six, and now the default in Windows and in Apple's systems too. Rather than adding one segment per round trip, CUBIC grows its window along a cubic curve, measured in time since its last loss: quickly when it is far below the size at which it last ran into trouble, cautiously as it approaches that size, and then quickly again if it passes it without incident. It is AIMD with better judgement about how fast to climb.

The second is a more radical departure. Loss is a poor signal of congestion in some circumstances: on a radio link, packets are lost to interference rather than to full queues, and a sender that halves its window for every such loss will crawl. Worse, as we shall see in a moment, by the time a loss occurs the queues may have been full for a long while. So in two thousand and sixteen a team at Google that included Van Jacobson himself -- thirty years after Berkeley -- published an algorithm called **BBR**, which does not wait for loss at all. It continually *measures* the path: the bottleneck's bandwidth, from how fast acknowledgements return, and the path's minimum round trip, from the fastest it has seen; and it paces its sending to match. On Google's own private network between data centres, they reported that BBR achieved between two and twenty-five times the throughput of CUBIC; on YouTube, worldwide, it cut the median round trip by more than half, and in the developing world by more than four-fifths. Restraint, in BBR, is no longer a reflex after the damage is done. It is a measurement taken before.

One more refinement deserves a sentence of its own, because it changes the nature of the signal. Since nineteen ninety-four engineers had proposed, and in two thousand and one standardised, a way for a router to warn senders of a crowded room *without* throwing anything away. It is called **explicit congestion notification**, ECN. A router whose queue is growing, instead of dropping a packet, sets a mark on it; the receiver notices the mark and passes word back in its acknowledgements; and the sender lowers its voice exactly as if a packet had been lost -- but nothing has been lost, and nothing need be resent. It is restraint by tap on the shoulder rather than by broken crockery. It took many years to be widely switched on, because some old boxes in the middle of the Internet mishandled the marks, which is a theme you will hear again before the course is out.

Which brings me to the last pathology of the Part, and it is one you have certainly suffered at home. In two thousand and ten an engineer named Jim Gettys, working from his house, noticed that whenever he uploaded a large file, everything else on his network became unusable -- web pages crawled and video calls fell apart -- and that the round trip to anywhere at all rose from a few tens of milliseconds to more than a second. He traced it, and gave it a name that has stuck: **bufferbloat**. The cause was that manufacturers of home routers and modems, memory being cheap, had given their devices enormous queues, on the reasonable-sounding theory that a dropped packet is a bad thing and more buffer means fewer drops. But TCP's restraint is triggered by loss. With an enormous queue in the way, a sender keeps growing its window and filling the queue, and no loss comes, so it never slows down -- until the queue holds several seconds' worth of packets, and everything else that must pass through that queue, your video call included, waits behind them. The cure, published in two thousand and twelve by Kathleen Nichols and, once again, Van Jacobson, was to manage queues by the *time* packets spend in them rather than by their length, and to drop or mark packets early when that time grows -- deliberately sending the restraint signal before the room is full. Their method and its descendants are now the default on a great many Linux systems. If you have ever turned on a setting called *smart queue management* on a home router, and found that the video calls survived the backups at last, you were installing Van Jacobson's manners in your hall.

So: turn-taking and restraint. The receiver advertises its window, and the sender never overruns it; window scaling lets that window be large enough for a long pipe. The sender also keeps a congestion window of its own, its estimate of what the room can bear, and obeys whichever window is smaller. It begins with slow start, doubling every round trip; grows gently thereafter; and halves at the first sign of loss -- additive increase, multiplicative decrease, which shares a link fairly among strangers by arithmetic alone. That restraint, voluntary and unenforced, is what has kept the Internet from collapsing since nineteen eighty-eight. And its costs explain three of your rituals: why fresh connections are slow and warm ones fast; why long-distance copies open many connections at once; and why a home router with a queue too large for its own good ruins a video call.

## Part Five -- The Box in the Hall, Again

Last week I left you with a promise. The box in your hall, I said, shares one public address among your whole household, by rewriting every packet that leaves the house so that it appears to come from the box itself, and keeping a note so that the replies can be sent back to the right machine inside. And I said that the note required port numbers, which we did not yet have. We have them now. Let me pay the debt, because the mechanism explains two of the most maddening failures in your working life.

A reminder of the arrangement, so that you need not reach back a week for it. Inside your house, every machine has a private address from the range one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight, which is never routed on the public Internet. The box in the hall has, on its outside, exactly one public address, given to it by your provider. Everything your household sends to the world must leave from that one address. The trick that makes this possible is called **network address translation** -- NAT, said as a word -- and the particular form of it that every home router performs, which rewrites ports as well as addresses, is called *port address translation*. In practice, everybody just says NAT.

Let me walk it, with the cast announced. Your laptop, inside, at one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty. The box in the hall, whose public address -- I shall use one reserved for examples -- is one ninety-eight dot fifty-one dot one hundred dot seven. And a server out in the world, at two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot ten, listening on port four forty-three.

First. Your browser opens a connection to the server. The laptop picks an ephemeral port -- say, fifty-one thousand and one -- and sends the first packet of the handshake: *from one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty, port fifty-one thousand and one, to two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot ten, port four forty-three.*

Second. The packet reaches the box in the hall on its way out. The box cannot send it to the world as it stands, because the source address is private, and no reply addressed to it could ever find its way back. So the box rewrites the source. It replaces the laptop's private address with its own public one, and the laptop's port with a port of the box's own choosing -- say, forty thousand. And in a table it keeps for the purpose, it writes a line: *my port forty thousand belongs to the laptop at one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot twenty, port fifty-one thousand and one, talking to two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot ten, port four forty-three.* Then it sends the packet on. As far as the server can tell, the box itself is the client.

Third. The server replies -- naturally, to the address and port it saw: *to one ninety-eight dot fifty-one dot one hundred dot seven, port forty thousand.*

Fourth. The reply reaches the box. The box looks up port forty thousand in its table, finds the line it wrote, rewrites the destination back to the laptop's private address and port fifty-one thousand and one, and passes the packet inside. The laptop receives a reply to exactly the conversation it started, and never knows anything happened.

Now suppose that at the same moment a tablet in the next room, at one ninety-two dot one sixty-eight dot one dot thirty, opens a connection to the same server -- and by coincidence its own operating system also picks port fifty-one thousand and one. No matter. The box gives that conversation a different port of its own -- forty thousand and one -- and writes a second line in its table. The two conversations leave the house from the same public address, distinguished by the box's ports; and the replies are sorted back to the laptop and the tablet by those ports. That is why the port numbers were essential: the box can share one address among a whole household only because it has sixty-five thousand ports to tell their conversations apart. The table it keeps is called, on Linux and in the trade generally, the **connection-tracking table** -- *conntrack*, for short -- and it is keyed, of course, by the five-tuple.

So there is the mechanism. Outgoing, the box rewrites the private address and port into its public address and a port of its own, and writes the pairing in its table. Incoming, it looks up the port, rewrites back, and delivers. Everything else about NAT follows from that table.

NAT was proposed in May nineteen ninety-four, in an RFC whose authors were refreshingly frank about what they were offering. It was, they wrote, *another short-term solution*, to stretch the address supply while the long-term one was built. And in their conclusion they observed that NAT *has several negative characteristics that make it inappropriate as a long term solution, and may make it inappropriate even as a short term solution.* That was thirty-two years ago. It is now in every house in the world, and a great many data centres, and the long-term solution, as you heard last week, has reached about half the users of the largest web company on Earth. There is nothing so permanent, the saying goes, as a temporary arrangement.

Nor has it stopped at your house. As the supply of addresses ran out, many providers -- nearly every mobile network, and a growing number of home providers -- stopped giving each customer a public address at all. They give the box in your hall a *private* address, from a block set aside for exactly this purpose in two thousand and twelve, one hundred dot sixty-four dot zero dot zero, slash ten; and then they translate it again, in a very large box of their own, into one of a small pool of public addresses shared among many customers at once. It is called **carrier-grade NAT**, and it means that your packets may be translated twice before they reach the open Internet: once in your hall, and once in your provider's building. Two consequences are worth knowing. First, if you are behind such a box, nothing you do in your own hall can make a machine of yours reachable from outside, because the provider's table is not yours to write in. And second, when a web site blocks or slows an address because of misbehaviour, it may be blocking a whole neighbourhood, since the whole neighbourhood is behind that address. If a web site has ever asked you to prove that you are not a robot for no reason you could see, it may be that a stranger sharing your public address earned you the question.

And this, at bottom, is why the long-term solution matters. In IP version six, every machine can have a public address of its own, and the box in the hall need translate nothing at all -- though it will still, as a rule, and quite properly, refuse conversations that strangers try to start.

Let me give you the negative characteristics, because you live with all three.

The first is that conversations can only be started from inside. A packet that arrives at the box from the outside world, uninvited -- not a reply to anything in the table -- has nowhere to go. The box has no line for it, so it does not know which machine inside it is for, and it throws it away. That is a mild blessing for security, since it means strangers cannot open connections to the machines in your house. But it is a great nuisance for anything that wants to *accept* connections at home -- a game, a video call between two houses, any program that wants two people's machines to talk directly. Such programs go to considerable lengths, with the help of servers outside both houses, to coax two boxes in two halls into letting a conversation through. When a video call connects instantly, a good deal of quiet diplomacy has been conducted on your behalf.

There is a way round it, and you may have used it. You can write a line in the box's table by hand, in advance: *anything that arrives at my port twenty-five thousand, send to the games console, at its port twenty-five thousand.* That is called **port forwarding**, and it is how anybody who has ever run a server at home has made it reachable from outside. Games consoles, and some other programs, ask the box to write such a line for them, automatically, by a protocol with the unlovely name of *Universal Plug and Play*. And if you once started a little server on your laptop at home, gave a colleague your address, and found that they could not reach it, this was why: they knocked at the box in your hall, and the box had no line in its table to tell it which machine inside the knock was for.

The second negative characteristic is philosophical, and it is the reason the purists have always disliked NAT. You will remember fate-sharing, from the first evening: the principle that the state of a conversation should live only at its two ends, so that it is lost only if one of the ends is lost. NAT breaks the principle. The box in the middle now holds a piece of every conversation's state -- the line in its table -- and if the box forgets that line, the conversation dies, though both ends are alive and well and believe the conversation is in perfect health. The box has become a third party to every conversation in the house, and a forgetful one.

And that is the third characteristic, and the one that has cost you hours. The box *does* forget, deliberately. It cannot keep a line in its table for every conversation forever, or the table would fill. So it forgets conversations that have been idle for a while. How long a while depends on the box. And the answers, for the boxes in the clouds you use, are short.

Here are some, as the providers document them. Amazon's NAT gateway forgets a connection that has been idle for three hundred and fifty seconds -- just under six minutes -- and if a packet then arrives for the forgotten conversation, it answers with a reset. Amazon's load balancers, by default, give up on an idle connection after sixty seconds. Microsoft's load balancer, by default, forgets after four minutes, and does so silently, so that a packet for a forgotten conversation simply vanishes. Google's NAT keeps an established conversation for twenty minutes of silence. And against all of those stands TCP's own remedy for idle connections: a feature called **keepalive**, in which a side that has heard nothing for a while sends a tiny probe to make sure the other end is still there -- and the default interval before the first probe, on Linux and nearly everywhere else, is *two hours*. The standards actually require that the default be no less than two hours, on the grounds, reasonable in nineteen eighty-nine, that idle connections cost nothing and probes are a waste.

Do you see the trap? Your service keeps a pool of connections to a database, or to another service, through a NAT gateway or a load balancer. Some of those connections sit idle for a few minutes in a quiet period -- at night, say, or on a Sunday. The box in the middle forgets them, at six minutes, or four, or one. Neither end knows. Neither end has been told anything. Both still hold the connection open, and believe it to be in perfect health, and TCP's own keepalive will not check for another hour and fifty-odd minutes. Then traffic picks up, and your service takes one of those connections from the pool and sends a request down it. And one of two things happens. If the box answers forgotten conversations with a reset, your request fails at once with an error saying that the connection was *reset by peer* -- although the peer, the database, has done nothing at all; the reset came from the box. If the box drops forgotten conversations silently, your request simply disappears, and your service waits, and waits, until its own timeout fires, if it has one. Either way, the first requests after every quiet period fail, mysteriously, and the next ones succeed, because by then the pool has noticed and opened fresh connections. The pattern -- *errors on Monday morning, or just after a quiet spell, and then fine* -- is the signature of a box in the middle that forgot your conversations.

And now the rituals explain themselves. You were told to set *keepalives* on your connections, and to set them to something like every minute or two rather than the default -- because a probe sent more often than the box's idle timeout keeps the line in the box's table fresh, and the box never forgets. You were told to set a *maximum idle time* on your connection pool, shorter than the load balancer's timeout -- because a pool that discards its own idle connections before the box can forget them never hands you a dead one. You were told to make sure your load balancer's idle timeout is longer than your service's, or shorter, depending on which side of it you sit -- because whichever party forgets first is the one that causes the trouble. There is a particularly common version of this trap that sits between a load balancer and the servers behind it, and it produces a symptom you may well have chased: a small, steady trickle of errors from the load balancer -- the kind that say *bad gateway* -- at no particular time, under no particular load. Here is the cast. A load balancer, which keeps a pool of connections open to each of your servers and re-uses them for many requests; and a server, which also has an idle timeout of its own, after which it closes a quiet connection. Suppose the server's idle timeout is *shorter* than the load balancer's -- a few seconds, say, as many web frameworks set by default, against the load balancer's sixty. Then, now and again, the server decides a connection has been idle long enough and closes it, at the very instant the load balancer decides to send a new request down it. The request arrives at a connection that has just been closed; it is answered with a reset; and the load balancer, which has no way to know whether the request was processed, reports a bad gateway to your user. The remedy, which the cloud providers' own documentation states, is that the server must be the *more patient* of the two: its idle timeout must be longer than the load balancer's, so that it is always the load balancer that closes an idle connection, and never the server out from under it. Whoever forgets first causes the trouble; arrange for the right party to forget first.

And your database driver's habit of *validating* a connection before use, with a trivial query, is the same remedy in another coat: ask the room whether it still remembers you before you say anything important. All five rituals answer one fact, which deserves repeating because it will cost you an afternoon otherwise: *a box in the middle will forget an idle conversation long before TCP thinks to check on it.*

One last note, which we shall need in the fifth evening. The connection-tracking table is not only in the box in your hall. Every Linux machine that does any address translation keeps one -- and that includes, as it happens, every node in a Kubernetes cluster, which translates addresses for every Service you have ever defined. Those tables have a maximum size, and when one fills, the machine starts dropping new connections and writes a message to its log saying, in effect, *table full, dropping packet*. I shall come back to it when we reach the machine room.

So: NAT lets a whole household share one public address by rewriting each outgoing packet's address and port and remembering the pairing in a table, keyed by the five-tuple, and translating the replies back. It was meant to be temporary, and is now universal. It means conversations can be started only from inside; it breaks the principle that a conversation's state lives only at its ends; and the boxes that do it forget idle conversations after minutes, while TCP's own keepalive waits two hours. Hence the keepalives, the idle limits on pools, and the validation queries: every one of them a way of reminding a forgetful box that you are still talking.

## Part Six -- Reading the Silence

When a conversation fails, your program is not told much. It does not receive a report. It receives, from the operating system, a short error -- a single phrase -- and on the strength of that phrase you, or the engineer on call at three in the morning, must decide what has gone wrong and where. The good news is that each of those phrases is precise. Each one tells you, if you know the manners, exactly which part of the conversation failed. Let me take them one at a time, and give each its meaning in the terms of this evening.

*Connection refused.* This one is quick: it arrives at once, within a round trip. It means that your first message, the SYN, reached the far machine, and the far machine answered it -- with a reset. The machine is there. It is switched on. It is reachable. But nothing is listening at the port you asked for. The program is not running, or it is listening on a different port, or it is listening only on its own localhost, which you will remember from last week. A house, with somebody at home, but nobody in that flat. *Refused* is good news of a kind: the network is fine; your problem is the program.

*Connection timed out*, while connecting. This one is slow, and it means the opposite. Your SYN went out, and nothing came back at all -- no SYN-ACK, no reset, nothing. The operating system tried again, and again, and before long it was waiting longer and longer between attempts, as you now know it must. On Linux, by default, it keeps trying for about two minutes before it gives up and tells your program. Silence of that kind means the packets are being lost or discarded somewhere along the way: the far machine is switched off, or the address is wrong, or -- most often, in the clouds you use -- a firewall or a security rule is quietly dropping them without a word. *Timed out* means: look at the network, and at the rules on it, not at the program.

And here is a ritual. You were told to set a *connect timeout* on every client, of a few seconds, rather than leaving the default. Now you see what the default is: two minutes of patient knocking at a door that is not going to open, while your user waits, and the threads in your service pile up behind the knocking. A connect timeout of a few seconds turns two minutes of silence into a quick, clear failure that something can act on.

*No route to host*, or *network is unreachable*. This means that a router somewhere along the way sent back one of the little ICMP notes we met last week: *I cannot get there from here.* The destination's network is not in the router's table at all. Check the address, and the routes -- and remember that many networks do not send such notes, so their absence proves nothing.

*Connection reset by peer.* This one arrives in the middle of a conversation. It means that a reset arrived for a conversation you believed was open: the other side -- or something pretending to be it -- has announced that, as far as it is concerned, this conversation does not exist. The far program may have crashed, or been restarted, or closed the connection abruptly. Or, as you heard in the last Part, a box in the middle may have forgotten the conversation after an idle spell and reset it on the far end's behalf. If the resets come after quiet periods, suspect the box. If they come when the far service is deployed or restarted, suspect the far service.

*Broken pipe.* This means you wrote to a connection that the other side had already closed or reset. Typically it is the second write after a reset: the first write provoked the reset, and the second is told about it. It is the same failure as a reset, discovered from the other direction.

*Cannot assign requested address.* That is the one from Part Three: you have run out of ephemeral ports, very likely because a great many short-lived connections are lingering at the door in TIME-WAIT. The cure is to re-use connections.

And finally, the timeout that you set yourself -- the *read* timeout, or *request* timeout -- which fires when a connection is open but no answer has come back in time. That one, on its own, does not tell you whether the far service is slow or the conversation died silently in the middle. That ambiguity is the reason the box-in-the-hall rituals of the last Part exist: so that a dead conversation is discovered by a keepalive before it is discovered by your user.

Let me give you the catalogue once more, in one breath, because it is the most useful thing in this Part and you may want it at three in the morning. *Refused*: the machine is there, nobody is listening on that door. *Timed out* while connecting: silence, so look at the network and the firewall rules. *No route*: a router says it cannot get there. *Reset by peer*: the other side, or a box in between, has forgotten or abandoned the conversation. *Broken pipe*: you wrote after a reset. *Cannot assign requested address*: you have run out of ports. Each error names the stage at which the conversation failed.

Let me work three cases with you, the way one does at three in the morning, so that the catalogue becomes a habit rather than a list.

First case. Every Monday at about nine, a service logs a short burst of *connection reset by peer* when talking to its database, and then nothing for the rest of the week. The database's own logs show no errors at all. What do you suspect? Not the database: a reset arriving after a long quiet spell, with no trouble at the far end, is the signature of a box in the middle that forgot idle conversations over the weekend -- a NAT gateway or a load balancer -- and of a connection pool that handed out connections the box had forgotten. The remedy is keepalives, or a shorter idle limit on the pool.

Second case. After a colleague changes the firewall rules in your cloud network, one service can no longer reach another, and every attempt fails with *connection timed out* -- after, you notice, a very long wait. What do you suspect? Silence, and a long wait, mean that the packets are being dropped without reply, which is exactly what a security rule does to traffic it does not permit. Had the far service merely been down, you would more likely have seen *refused*, at once. Look at the rule.

Third case. During a deployment, for a few seconds, clients see *connection refused* from some of the new servers. What do you suspect? The machines are up and answering -- a refusal is an answer -- but the program has not yet begun listening on its port. The load balancer is sending traffic to servers before they are ready, and the cure is a health check that waits until the program is listening and willing.

Reset after quiet: a forgetful box. Timed out after a rule change: a firewall dropping silently. Refused during a deploy: a door not yet opened. The phrase tells you the stage, and the stage tells you where to look.

Two tools will let you see all of this for yourself, rather than inferring it from an error. The first is a command on every Linux machine called *ss* -- two letters, generally read as *socket statistics* -- which lists every conversation the machine is part of, each with its five-tuple and its state: the listening doors, the established conversations, the guests lingering in TIME-WAIT. If you suspect you are running out of ports, count the TIME-WAITs. If you suspect the receptionist is overwhelmed, look at the queue beside the listening door. The second is a packet capture -- *tcpdump*, on the command line, or *Wireshark*, which shows the same conversation in colour -- which records the segments themselves as they pass, so that you can watch the handshake, the acknowledgements, the retransmissions, and the resets, and see with your own eyes which side said what, and when. Wireshark will even plot a connection's sequence numbers against time, so that the doubling of slow start, and the stall after a loss, appear as a picture. The error tells you the stage; the capture tells you the whole story.

I should add, in fairness to the operating system, that the network does not always make this easy. Alexey Kuznetsov, who wrote a good deal of Linux's network code in the nineteen-nineties, left a comment in it in nineteen ninety-eight, complaining that in the modern Internet, routing is unreliable, and in each dark corner broken firewalls sit, sending random errors ordered by their masters. One knows the feeling.

Now let me tell you about two good ideas that, together, make one very bad one, because it is the most common cause of mysterious latency in the world, and because it carries the name I promised you in the first evening.

The first idea is John Nagle's. In January nineteen eighty-four, Nagle -- an engineer at Ford Aerospace, the same man who named congestion collapse -- wrote about the problem of small packets -- *tinygrams*, as he has called them since. A program like a remote terminal sends each keystroke the moment it is typed: one byte of data, in a packet with forty bytes of headers. One byte of letter in forty of envelope, as I put it in the first evening. Nagle observed, drily, that this four-thousand-percent overhead was annoying but tolerable on lightly loaded networks -- and that on loaded ones, it helped push them over the edge. His remedy was simple. If a connection has sent some data that has not yet been acknowledged, and the program offers another small piece, *hold it*; gather the small pieces together; and send them all in one packet when the acknowledgement for the earlier data comes back. It was, he noted, one or two lines inside a TCP program. It is called **Nagle's algorithm**, and it has been on by default in nearly every TCP in the world for forty years.

The second idea was set down by David Clark at MIT in nineteen eighty-two, and built into Berkeley's version of Unix soon after. When a receiver gets a segment, it need not acknowledge it instantly. If it waits a moment, the program on its side may write a reply, and then the acknowledgement can ride along on the reply, saving a packet. So receivers *delay* their acknowledgements, briefly, in the hope of company. The standard says the delay must be less than half a second; Linux uses a minimum of forty milliseconds, and some systems use two hundred. That is called a **delayed acknowledgement**, and it too is a perfectly sensible economy on its own.

Now put them together. Let me walk it with the cast announced: a client, and a server, and a request that the client writes in two pieces -- first the headers, then the body -- which is a very common way for a program to write a request.

First. The client writes the headers. Nothing is unacknowledged yet, so Nagle's rule lets them go at once. They reach the server.

Second. The client writes the body. But now there *is* unacknowledged data in flight -- the headers. So Nagle's rule holds the body back, waiting for the headers to be acknowledged.

Third. The server has received the headers, and has no reply to send yet, because it is waiting for the body before it can answer. So, following the delayed-acknowledgement rule, it does not acknowledge the headers at once. It waits, hoping a reply will come along to carry the acknowledgement.

And there they stand. The client will not send the body until the headers are acknowledged. The server will not acknowledge the headers until it has a reply to send, and it cannot reply without the body. Two courteous people in a doorway, each waiting for the other to go first. The deadlock is broken only when the server's delayed-acknowledgement timer gives up and sends the acknowledgement on its own -- after forty milliseconds, or two hundred -- at which point the body is released, and everything proceeds as if nothing had happened. Except that forty or two hundred milliseconds have been added to the request, every time, for no reason at all.

Nagle himself has said, many years later, in a public forum, that this still irks him. The real problem, he wrote, is not tinygram prevention; it is ACK delays, and that stupid fixed timer. He did tinygram prevention, and Berkeley did delayed acknowledgements, both in the early nineteen-eighties, and the combination of the two is awful.

The stall turns up everywhere, and the stories of finding it all end the same way. An engineer named Julia Evans wrote of a service that took forty milliseconds to talk to another program *on the same machine* -- a request that should have taken a fraction of a millisecond -- until she switched off Nagle's algorithm, and all of the forty-millisecond delays vanished at once, and she felt, she said, like a wizard. A payments company found two hundred milliseconds added to every single request of one kind, because its HTTP library wrote the headers and the body of each request separately, and the proxy in front of its servers delayed its acknowledgements. A senior engineer at Amazon wrote a short essay in two thousand and twenty-four whose title, in its entirety, was a sigh: *it's always TCP no-delay; every damn time.*

And there is the ritual, and the option in your code that I promised you in the first evening. The socket option that switches off Nagle's algorithm is called **TCP no-delay** -- written in capitals, as TCP, an underscore, and NODELAY, all one word. Setting it tells TCP to send small writes at once, without waiting. Most modern frameworks for calls between services set it for you; many database drivers do too. If you have ever found it set in your code, by a colleague who could not say why, now you know: somebody, once, found the forty-millisecond pause, and swore.

One more consequence of TCP's manners, and then we are done, because it sets up a large part of the fifth evening. TCP promises to deliver bytes *in order*. That is a fine promise for one conversation. But suppose a single TCP connection carries many independent requests at once, interleaved -- as the newer versions of the web's protocol do, to save on connections. If one segment is lost, TCP will not deliver *anything* that came after it, until the lost segment has been resent and has arrived -- even bytes that belong to entirely different requests, which arrived perfectly well and are sitting in the receiver's buffer, waiting. One lost packet holds up every request behind it. The trade calls this **head-of-line blocking**, after the customer at the front of a queue who holds everybody up, and it is one of the main reasons engineers at Google built QUIC on top of UDP: so that each request could have its own stream, and a loss in one would not stall the others. That story belongs to the fifth evening.

Last, a word on the rituals that sit above all of this, because they are the rituals in which the evening's lessons combine. Every call your service makes across a network should have a timeout -- for connecting, and for the whole request -- because otherwise a silent failure, of any of the kinds we have just described, becomes a thread waiting forever. A failed call may be retried, but with exponential backoff and random jitter, as Ethernet taught us last week, so that a thousand clients do not retry in lockstep. A call that is retried must be idempotent, as the first evening taught us, because you cannot know whether the first attempt was done. And retries must be *limited*, because a service that retries every failure several times will, when its dependency is struggling, multiply the load on it several times over -- and you will have rebuilt the congestion collapse of nineteen eighty-six one floor up, in your own code, with the same cure: restraint. Back off, and lower your voice, when the room is crowded.

So: the errors are precise. *Refused* is a closed door; *timed out* is silence, and the network's fault; *reset by peer* is a conversation forgotten or abandoned, often by a box in between; *broken pipe* is writing after a reset; *cannot assign requested address* is running out of ports. Nagle's algorithm and delayed acknowledgements, sensible apart, combine into a stall of forty or two hundred milliseconds, cured by TCP no-delay. In-order delivery means one lost packet can hold up everything behind it. And above it all, your own manners: timeouts on every call, retries with backoff and jitter, idempotent operations, and limits on retrying -- restraint, once again.

## What the Boy Should Have Been Told

So, sir: the third lecture, as the boy should have heard it, and as I hope you will carry it, whether you are at your desk, or on the run, or walking the hall at an hour when nobody ought to be awake.

The network loses letters, doubles them, shuffles them, and says nothing. TCP turns that into a perfect conversation, entirely at the two ends, by a handful of manners. It numbers every byte. It acknowledges cumulatively -- *I have everything up to here; send me the next.* It keeps a copy of everything it sends until that copy is acknowledged, and sends it again if the acknowledgement does not come in time, with a timer that learns each conversation's round trip. It keeps a window of data in flight, so that a long pipe is not left empty. And it resends at once when three duplicate acknowledgements say that something after a gap has arrived. Number, acknowledge, keep a copy, resend: that is how a reliable byte stream is made out of an unreliable post. You have heard it three times now, and that is how often it deserves.

Every conversation opens with three movements -- SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK -- in which each side announces where its numbering begins and hears the other confirm it. Those introductions cost a full round trip before any work is done. Every conversation ends with four -- FIN, acknowledged, in each direction -- and the side that said goodbye first lingers at the door in TIME-WAIT for a minute, holding its port. A reset slams the door instead. And the two costs at either end -- the round trip of the handshake, and the port held on the step -- send you, both of them, to the same habit: keep your connections, and use them again.

There is a third reason for the same habit, which belongs to the most important idea of the evening. Every TCP sender keeps a congestion window -- its own estimate of how much the network can bear -- and obeys it as well as the receiver's. It begins small and doubles every round trip; that is slow start, and it is why a fresh connection is sluggish and a warm one quick. It then grows gently, and at the first sign of loss it halves its window, at once, of its own accord. Additive increase, multiplicative decrease: a sawtooth, drawn by every conversation on Earth, which shares a crowded link fairly among strangers by arithmetic alone. That voluntary restraint, put into TCP by Van Jacobson and Mike Karels after the Internet collapsed in nineteen eighty-six, is what has kept it standing ever since. No router enforces it. Billions of machines are polite at once. That is the third time I have said it, and I shall not apologise for it: it is the most surprising fact in networking.

The box in your hall shares one public address among your household by rewriting the address and port of every packet that leaves, and remembering the pairing in a table so that it can translate the replies. That table forgets idle conversations -- after six minutes, or four, or one, depending on whose box it is -- while TCP's own keepalive waits two hours before it checks. So a pooled connection that sat quiet over a weekend may be dead without either end knowing it, and the first request on Monday morning fails with a reset that the far end never sent.

And when conversations fail, the error says where. *Connection refused*: the machine is there, and nobody is listening at that door. *Timed out*: silence, and so the network, or a firewall dropping packets without reply. *Reset by peer*: the other side, or a box in between, has forgotten or abandoned the conversation. *Broken pipe*: you wrote after a reset. *Cannot assign requested address*: you have run out of ports. Read the phrase, and it tells you the stage at which the conversation failed, and the stage tells you where to look.

Your rituals this evening were many, and every one of them is now a mechanism. You pool connections and keep them alive because a handshake costs a round trip, slow start costs several, and every closed connection holds a port at the door for a minute. You set keepalives to a few minutes, cap the idle time of your pools, and make your servers more patient than the load balancer in front of them, because boxes in the middle forget, and whoever forgets first causes the trouble. You set a connect timeout of seconds because the default is two minutes of knocking. You set TCP no-delay because Nagle's algorithm and delayed acknowledgements, each sensible alone, together make two courteous strangers wait forty milliseconds in a doorway. You raise socket buffers for long, fat pipes, and you let your copying tools open many connections at once, because one connection's throughput is bounded by its round trip and its losses. You raise the backlog when a burst of new connections finds the receptionist overwhelmed. You may enable time-wait reuse, but never its reckless sibling, which is in any case gone. You retry with backoff, jitter, and a budget, because retries without restraint rebuild nineteen eighty-six in your own code. And you switched on smart queue management at home, and the video calls survived the backups, because a queue that is too large defeats TCP's restraint until it is seconds deep.

And you now know whose sentence it is that closes these evenings. It was written into TCP's own specification in September nineteen eighty-one by Jon Postel, who edited the Internet's documents for twenty-eight years and kept its registry of numbers from a modest office in Marina del Rey, and who answered a magazine's suggestion that he was the god of the Internet by saying that there is no god of the Internet, only a lot of people cooperating. Whether his principle was right, I shall tell you in the last evening.

For reading: Ilya Grigorik's *High Performance Browser Networking*, which its author and publisher have made free to read online, and whose chapters on TCP and on latency say in fewer pages much of what I have said tonight, with diagrams. And Van Jacobson and Michael Karels's paper of nineteen eighty-eight, *Congestion Avoidance and Control* -- if only for its first page, which tells the story of the four hundred yards in the authors' own words, and which I think the most consequential page in this field. For the brave, there is a two-volume classic by the late Richard Stevens, *TCP/IP Illustrated*, which follows real packets through real machines, byte by byte, and from which a generation of engineers learned what you have heard tonight.

Next week, the fourth question: *who* is speaking, and who is listening? Every conversation we have discussed tonight has been between two addresses. But you do not type addresses; you type names. And when you reach the bank, you take it on trust that the machine answering is the bank, and that nobody between you is listening. Both of those -- the name and the trust -- rest on two great systems of delegated authority, each rooted at the very top in a handful of keys. And it so happens that on the eleventh of October this year, at the root of the Internet's directory, one of those keys is being replaced, by a ceremony of a formality that would not disgrace a coronation: locked rooms, safes within safes, smart cards held by trusted people from around the world, every step scripted and witnessed and filmed. We shall begin there, in the locked room, and work our way down to the padlock in your browser.

Be conservative in what you send, sir, and liberal in what you accept.
