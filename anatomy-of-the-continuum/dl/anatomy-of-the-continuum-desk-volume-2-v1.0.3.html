1.2 The Contract

Definition 1.1 (The contract for a length).

A candidate notion of length is an assignment $\mu$ of a value in $[0, \infty]$ to sets of reals (all sets, or a declared tribe of them), demanded to satisfy:

  1. (i)

    $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$;

  2. (ii)

    $\mu(I) = b - a$ for every interval $I$ with endpoints $a \le b$, open, closed, or half-open;

  3. (iii)

    (translation invariance) $\mu(A + t) = \mu(A)$ for every $t \in \R$, where $A + t = \{ a + t : a \in A \}$;

  4. (iv)

    (countable additivity) for every sequence $A_1, A_2, \dots$ of pairwise disjoint sets in the domain whose union is in the domain, $\mu\bigl( \bigcup_k A_k \bigr) = \sum_k \mu(A_k)$.

Finite additivity is the weakening of (iv) to finitely many pieces; it follows from (iv) by padding with empty sets, clause (i) absorbing the padding.

Remark 1.2 (Additivity across arbitrary families is not on the table).

$[0,1]$ is the disjoint union of its singletons. Each singleton has length zero under any candidate honouring (ii) and monotone common sense: $\{x\} \subseteq (x - \varepsilon, x + \varepsilon)$ for every $\varepsilon$. An additivity clause surviving uncountable disassembly would price $[0,1]$ as a “sum” of uncountably many zeros; no convention for such sums yields one (any reasonable definition — say, the supremum of finite subsums — yields zero). Clause (iv) stops at countable by necessity, not modesty.

Proposition 1.3 (Continuity of measure — the engine’s crankshaft).

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a sigma-algebra and $\mu$ a countably additive assignment on $\mathcal{A}$ with values in $[0,\infty]$ and $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$. (This proposition is stated for the tribes to come; from Chapter 2 onward it is used constantly.)

  1. (i)

    (Swelling chains) If $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots$, all in $\mathcal{A}$, then $\mu\bigl( \bigcup_n A_n \bigr) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$.

  2. (ii)

    (Shrinking chains) If $A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots$, all in $\mathcal{A}$, and $\mu(A_1) < \infty$, then $\mu\bigl( \bigcap_n A_n \bigr) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$. The finiteness hypothesis is not removable: $A_n = [n, \infty)$ shrinks to the empty set with every $\mu(A_n)$ infinite (once Chapter 2 delivers the measure, this is a lawful exhibit; the Archimedean property of Volume One, Theorem 2.12, empties the intersection).

Proof.

(i) Disjointify into rings of growth: $B_1 = A_1$ and $B_k = A_k \setminus A_{k-1}$ for $k \ge 2$. Each $B_k$ lies in $\mathcal{A}$ (complement and intersection, via De Morgan); the $B_k$ are pairwise disjoint; $\bigcup_{k \le n} B_k = A_n$; and $\bigcup_k B_k = \bigcup_n A_n$. Countable additivity prices the union as $\sum_k \mu(B_k)$, whose partial sums are, by finite additivity, $\mu(A_n)$ — the telescope, Volume One’s oldest cancellation. A series of nonnegative terms is the limit of its partial sums: $\mu(\bigcup A_n) = \lim \mu(A_n)$.

(ii) Pass to complements within $A_1$: the sets $A_1 \setminus A_n$ swell to $A_1 \setminus \bigcap_n A_n$. Apply (i), then subtract from $\mu(A_1)$ — lawful precisely because $\mu(A_1) < \infty$ keeps every quantity finite and the prohibited $\infty - \infty$ off the page (finite additivity gives $\mu(A_1) = \mu(A_n) + \mu(A_1 \setminus A_n)$, so $\mu(A_1 \setminus A_n) = \mu(A_1) - \mu(A_n)$, and likewise for the intersection). The identity rearranges to the claim. The converse direction — (i) plus finite additivity recovers countable additivity — is Problem II.2, solved in full: the engine and the clause are one mechanism.

1.3 Jordan’s Content

Definition 1.4 (Inner and outer content).

For bounded $A \subseteq \R$: the inner content $\underline{c}(A)$ is the supremum of $\sum_{j=1}^{N} \lvert I_j \rvert$ over all finite families of pairwise disjoint intervals with $\bigcup_j I_j \subseteq A$; the outer content $\overline{c}(A)$ is the infimum of $\sum_{j=1}^{N} \lvert I_j \rvert$ over all finite families with $A \subseteq \bigcup_j I_j$. Always $\underline{c}(A) \le \overline{c}(A)$; when equal, $A$ is Jordan measurable with content the common value.

Proposition 1.5 (The kinship: contents are Darboux ledgers).

Let $A \subseteq [a,b]$ and let $\mathbf{1}_A$ be its indicator. Then

\[ \underline{c}(A) = \underline{\int_a^b} \mathbf{1}_A \qquad\text{and}\qquad \overline{c}(A) = \overline{\int_a^b} \mathbf{1}_A . \]

In particular $A$ is Jordan measurable exactly when $\mathbf{1}_A$ is Riemann integrable, with content equal to integral. The rationals’ failure below and the static’s defeat (Volume One, Proposition 6.25 and Chapter 8) are one failure.

Proof.

Lower ledgers. A partition $P$ of $[a,b]$ has $L(\mathbf{1}_A, P) = \sum' \Delta x_i$, the primed sum over slices lying entirely inside $A$ (the floor is one exactly there) — a finite disjoint family of intervals inside $A$, so $L(\mathbf{1}_A,P) \le \underline{c}(A)$. Conversely a finite disjoint packing of $A$ by intervals of total length $\ell$ yields, after listing all endpoints as a partition and shaving $\varepsilon$ at the joins (closed slices must sit inside $A$; open packing intervals lose two points’ worth, which cost nothing after shaving), a lower sum exceeding $\ell - \varepsilon$. Suprema agree. The upper ledgers mirror: slices meeting $A$ versus finite covers, with the same endpoint bookkeeping. The desk leaves the mirrored half as drill within Problem I.4’s ambit.

□
Proposition 1.6 (Jordan’s verdict on the rationals).

For $A = \Q \cap [0,1]$: $\underline{c}(A) = 0$ and $\overline{c}(A) = 1$. The set is not Jordan measurable.

Proof.

Inner: an interval of positive length inside $A$ would consist of rationals only, impossible — the irrationals are dense (Volume One, Corollary 2.16). Only degenerate packings exist; the supremum is zero.

Outer: let $I_1, \dots, I_N$ be a finite cover of $A$ and let $F = \overline{I_1} \cup \cdots \cup \overline{I_N}$, the union of closures — a closed set of total length $\sum_j \lvert I_j \rvert$ at most (closing an interval adds endpoints, which carry no length). $F$ contains $A$, and $F$ is closed, so $F$ contains $\overline{A} = [0,1]$: the rationals are dense in the unit interval (Volume One, Theorem 2.15), and a closed superset of a set contains its closure. A finite union of intervals containing $[0,1]$ has total length at least $1$ (order the pieces meeting $[0,1]$ and walk from $0$ to $1$: each step is paid for by disjoint stretches of the pieces; the desk notes this is Jordan’s own counting, or Volume One’s Heine–Borel walk in miniature). Hence every finite cover totals at least $1$; the interval $[0,1]$ itself achieves $1$; the infimum is $1$.

□

1.4 The Outer Measure

Definition 1.7 ($(\circledast)$ Lebesgue outer measure — ring fifteen).

For any $A \subseteq \R$,

\[ m^{*}(A) \ =\ \inf \Bigl\{ \, \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \lvert I_k \rvert \ : \ A \subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} I_k, \ I_k \text{ intervals} \Bigr\}, \]

the infimum over all countable interval covers of the total length (a series of nonnegative terms: convergent or honestly infinite). Covers exist for every $A$ ($\R$ itself is a countable union of intervals), so the set of totals is a nonempty subset of $[0, \infty]$; if any total is finite, the set of finite totals is nonempty and bounded below by $0$, and the bell rings — the fifteenth, the first of the volume: the completeness axiom (Volume One, Axiom 2.6), and nothing else, delivers the greatest lower bound. (If every total is infinite, $m^{*}(A) = \infty$, an honest verdict.) The remaining suprema and infima of this chapter are clerical work upon this one and carry the standing annotation: completeness — small change, uncounted.

Remark 1.8 (Extended arithmetic; open covers at negligible cost).

Values live in $[0,\infty]$ with $a + \infty = \infty$; $\infty - \infty$ is never written. Two service facts, used silently hereafter. Fattening: any cover $\{I_k\}$ may be upgraded to open intervals $J_k \supseteq I_k$ with $\lvert J_k \rvert \le \lvert I_k \rvert + \varepsilon / 2^{k}$ — total cost at most $\varepsilon$, the geometric series footing the bill — so the infimum may be computed over open covers alone. Degenerate intervals (points) are permitted in covers and change nothing.

Proposition 1.9 (Monotonicity).

$A \subseteq B$ implies $m^{*}(A) \le m^{*}(B)$.

Proof.

Every cover of $B$ covers $A$, so the infimum for $A$ runs over a larger family of candidate totals and cannot exceed the infimum for $B$. (With clause (i) of the contract: $m^{*}(\emptyset) = 0$, the empty set hiding under covers of arbitrarily small total.)

□
Theorem 1.10 (The interval clause is kept).

For every interval $I$ with endpoints $a \le b$: $m^{*}(I) = b - a$.

Proof.

($\le$) $I \subseteq (a - \varepsilon, b + \varepsilon)$, a one-interval cover of total $b - a + 2\varepsilon$, for every $\varepsilon > 0$; the infimum dips below each, hence to $b - a$ at most.

($\ge$), the deep direction; it suffices to treat $I = [a,b]$ closed (any interval contains closed subintervals of length as near its own as desired, and monotonicity transfers the bound). Let $\{I_k\}$ be any countable cover of $[a,b]$; fatten to open $J_k$ at cost $\varepsilon$ (Remark 1.8). The open $J_k$ cover the compact $[a,b]$; Heine–Borel (Volume One, Theorem 5.18) extracts a finite committee $J_{k_1}, \dots, J_{k_N}$ still covering. Audit the committee by the walk: some member $(\alpha_1, \beta_1)$ contains $a$; if $\beta_1 > b$ stop; otherwise $\beta_1 \in [a,b]$ is covered by some member $(\alpha_2, \beta_2)$ with $\alpha_2 < \beta_1 < \beta_2$; continue. The walk visits distinct members with strictly increasing right endpoints, so it stops within $N$ steps, at some $\beta_M > b$. Then

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{M} \lvert J_{(i)} \rvert \ \ge\ (\beta_1 - \alpha_1) + (\beta_2 - \beta_1) + \cdots + (\beta_M - \beta_{M-1}) \ =\ \beta_M - \alpha_1 \ >\ b - a, \]

each step’s length at least the fresh ground gained (since $\alpha_{i+1} < \beta_i$). Hence $\sum_k \lvert I_k \rvert \ge \sum_k \lvert J_k \rvert - \varepsilon \ge b - a - \varepsilon$, for every cover and every $\varepsilon$; the infimum is at least $b - a$. — The clause cost compactness, exactly as narrated: the countable conspiracy collapses to an auditable committee, and Jordan’s own counting convicts the committee.

□
Theorem 1.11 (Countable subadditivity — the gambit’s debut).

For any sets $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots$

\[ m^{*} \Bigl( \bigcup_{k} A_k \Bigr) \ \le\ \sum_{k} m^{*}(A_k). \]
Proof.

If any $m^{*}(A_k) = \infty$ the claim is vacuous. Otherwise let $\varepsilon > 0$. For each $k$, the infimum’s approximation property (Volume One’s oldest reflex) supplies a cover $\{I_{k,j}\}_j$ of $A_k$ with $\sum_j \lvert I_{k,j} \rvert \le m^{*}(A_k) + \varepsilon/2^{k}$. Pool the covers: $\{I_{k,j}\}_{k,j}$ is a countable family (a countable union of countable families — Volume One, Chapter 3’s census) covering $\bigcup_k A_k$, of total

\[ \sum_{k} \sum_{j} \lvert I_{k,j} \rvert \ \le\ \sum_k \Bigl( m^{*}(A_k) + \frac{\varepsilon}{2^k} \Bigr) \ =\ \sum_k m^{*}(A_k) + \varepsilon, \]

the geometric series picking up the bill. One cover within $\varepsilon$ of the target, for every $\varepsilon$: the infimum obeys. The epsilon-over-two-to-the-k gambit, christened — a fixed tolerance spent across countably many claimants; it recurs until the finale.

□
Theorem 1.12 (Every countable set is null).

If $A \subseteq \R$ is countable, then $m^{*}(A) = 0$.

Proof.

Enumerate $A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots\}$ (finite sets: pad the argument or stop early). Given $\varepsilon > 0$, house $a_k$ in an interval of length $\varepsilon / 2^{k}$ centred there. The family covers $A$ with total $\varepsilon$ exactly. So $m^{*}(A) \le \varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$, and $m^{*}(A) \ge 0$ always: $m^{*}(A) = 0$. In particular $m^{*}(\Q \cap [0,1]) = 0$ and $m^{*}(\Q) = 0$: the set that defeated Jordan is dispatched, and the entire difference is the word countable.

□
Definition 1.13 (Measure zero).

$A \subseteq \R$ has measure zero (is null) when $m^{*}(A) = 0$: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there are countably many intervals covering $A$ of total length below $\varepsilon$. This is the phrase Volume One’s finale quoted as prophecy (Volume One, Chapter 8, the Lebesgue criterion, still on credit — proof at Chapter 4 of this volume); debt eleven pays its first instalment here.

Proposition 1.14 (The null family is a sigma-ideal).

(i) If $B \subseteq A$ and $A$ is null, $B$ is null. (ii) If each of $A_1, A_2, \dots$ is null, so is $\bigcup_k A_k$.

Proof.

(i) is monotonicity (Proposition 1.9). (ii) is countable subadditivity (Theorem 1.11): $m^{*}(\bigcup A_k) \le \sum_k 0 = 0$. Hereditary downward, closed under countable assembly: negligibility with working papers. When “almost everywhere” arrives in force, these two laws discharge exceptional sets by the countable armful.

□
Theorem 1.15 (The catch — stated on credit).

There is no assignment $\mu$ defined on all subsets of $\R$ satisfying the full contract of Definition 1.1 — nonnegativity with $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$, intervals to length, translation invariance, and countable additivity. In particular $m^{*}$, which keeps the first three clauses on all sets, is not countably additive on all sets.

On credit. The proof is the Vitali construction, Chapter 3’s centrepiece, with the Axiom of Choice named in the indictment; nothing in this volume uses the catch before its trial. Until then it stands as the season’s motivation: some sets must be turned away, and the honest question is which stay.

1.5 Sigma-Algebras and the Borel Estate

Definition 1.16 (Algebra; sigma-algebra).

A collection $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $\R$ (or of any ambient set) is a sigma-algebra when: (i) $\emptyset \in \mathcal{A}$; (ii) $A \in \mathcal{A}$ implies $A^{c} \in \mathcal{A}$; (iii) $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$ implies $\bigcup_k A_k \in \mathcal{A}$. Weakening (iii) to finite unions defines an algebra. Consequences, by De Morgan (Volume One, Chapter 1’s toolkit): the ambient set belongs ($\emptyset^{c}$); countable intersections belong ($\bigcap A_k = (\bigcup A_k^{c})^{c}$); differences belong. The walls are drawn exactly where the traffic runs: complements and countable assemblies are what limiting arguments perform.

Remark 1.17 (Three specimens).

The tribe of two, $\{\emptyset, \R\}$: closed under everything, useless for everything. The all-subsets tribe $\mathcal{P}(\R)$: maximal hospitality, and the catch (Theorem 1.15) bars it from carrying the contract. The countable-or-co-countable tribe $\{A : A \text{ countable or } A^{c} \text{ countable}\}$: a genuine sigma-algebra containing every singleton yet not $[0, \tfrac12]$ — dissected in Problem II.1. Membership is a distinction.

Theorem 1.18 (Generated sigma-algebras exist — from above).

Let $\mathcal{G}$ be any collection of subsets. Among all sigma-algebras containing $\mathcal{G}$ there is a smallest, written $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$: it is contained in every sigma-algebra containing $\mathcal{G}$.

Proof.

The family of sigma-algebras containing $\mathcal{G}$ is nonempty: $\mathcal{P}(\R)$ qualifies. Let $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$ be their intersection — the sets belonging to every sigma-algebra containing $\mathcal{G}$, the unanimous sets. The three closure checks, each one line: $\emptyset$ belongs to every chamber, hence unanimously. If $A$ is unanimous then, chamber by chamber, $A^{c}$ belongs (each chamber is a sigma-algebra), hence $A^{c}$ is unanimous. If $A_1, A_2, \dots$ are unanimous then each chamber contains them all and therefore their union: the union is unanimous. So $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$ is a sigma-algebra; it contains $\mathcal{G}$ (every chamber does); and it is contained in every containing sigma-algebra, being their intersection — unanimity cannot outvote a chamber. Floor-by-floor construction from below is not available at any countable stage (the Notes gesture at the transfinite abyss); the intersection from above is the whole trick.

□
Definition 1.19 (The Borel estate).

$\mathcal{B} = \sigma(\{\text{open subsets of } \R\})$: the Borel sets. Every set this course has ever named in anger — intervals, opens, closeds, $\Q$, the irrationals, the dust, every continuity set — is Borel; producing a non-Borel set requires the ghost-machinery of Chapter 3 or deliberate transfinite bureaucracy (Notes).

Theorem 1.20 (The estate does not depend on the founder’s taste).

$\sigma(\text{open sets}) = \sigma(\text{all intervals}) = \sigma(\text{open intervals}) = \sigma(\text{open intervals with rational endpoints})$.

Proof.

Write $\mathcal{B}$, $\mathcal{I}$, $\mathcal{I}_{o}$, $\mathcal{I}_{\Q}$ for the four generated tribes; we chain inclusions both ways, using twice the master principle: if every member of $\mathcal{G}_1$ lies in $\sigma(\mathcal{G}_2)$, then $\sigma(\mathcal{G}_1) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{G}_2)$ — $\sigma(\mathcal{G}_2)$ is a sigma-algebra containing $\mathcal{G}_1$, and the smallest such is contained in it.

$\mathcal{I}_{\Q} \subseteq \mathcal{I}_{o} \subseteq \mathcal{I}$: generators nest. $\mathcal{I} \subseteq \mathcal{B}$: every interval is Borel — open intervals are open; $[a,b] = \bigcap_n (a - \tfrac1n,\, b + \tfrac1n)$, a countable intersection of opens; half-open likewise, one side at a time. $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{I}_{o}$: every open set is a countable disjoint union of open beads — Volume One’s structure theorem (Proposition 5.3) — hence belongs to $\sigma(\text{open intervals})$, care of closure clause (iii); unbounded beads are countable unions of bounded ones. $\mathcal{I}_{o} \subseteq \mathcal{I}_{\Q}$: every open interval is a countable union of rational-endpoint ones — pick rational endpoints marching out to the true ones, density (Volume One, Theorem 2.15) supplying the recruits. The chain closes: all four tribes are one estate. The rational-endpoint generating set is countable — a fact of consequence for the season’s bookkeeping and for Chapter 8’s dictionary.

□
Definition 1.21 (The ladder’s first rungs).

A set is $F_{\sigma}$ when it is a countable union of closed sets; $G_{\delta}$ when a countable intersection of open sets. (F for fermé, sigma for sum; G for Gebiet, delta for Durchschnitt: the subject’s bilingual fossils.) Complementation exchanges the two rungs, by De Morgan.

Proposition 1.22 (Rung calibrations).

(i) $\Q$ is $F_{\sigma}$; the irrationals are $G_{\delta}$. (ii) Every closed set is $F_{\sigma}$ and every open set $G_{\delta}$, trivially; moreover every open set is $F_{\sigma}$ and every closed set $G_{\delta}$.

Proof.

(i) Singletons are closed; $\Q = \bigcup_{q \in \Q} \{q\}$ is a countable union of them. Complement and De Morgan deliver the irrationals as $G_{\delta}$. (ii) For open $U$: write $U$ as a countable union of open beads (Volume One, Proposition 5.3) and each bounded bead $(a,b)$ as $\bigcup_n [a + \tfrac1n,\, b - \tfrac1n]$ (fattened endpoints, run inward; unbounded beads as $\bigcup_n [a + \tfrac1n,\, n]$ and kin) — a countable union of closed sets. A countable union of countable unions is countable (Volume One, Chapter 3). Closed sets as $G_{\delta}$: complements.

□

1.6 The Continuity-Set Anatomy

Definition 1.23 (Oscillation).

For $f : \R \to \R$ (or on an interval), the swing on a set $W$ where $f$ is bounded is $\omega_f(W) = \sup_W f - \inf_W f$ (unbounded: $\omega_f(W) = \infty$). The oscillation at a point is

\[ \omega_f(x) \ =\ \inf_{\delta > 0} \, \omega_f\bigl( (x - \delta,\, x + \delta) \bigr), \]

the swings settling downward as windows shrink (monotone in $\delta$; the infimum is small change, uncounted).

Lemma 1.24 (Continuity is oscillation zero).

$f$ is continuous at $x$ if and only if $\omega_f(x) = 0$.

Proof.

($\Rightarrow$) Given $\varepsilon > 0$, continuity yields $\delta$ with $\lvert f(y) - f(x) \rvert < \varepsilon/2$ on the window, so the swing there is at most $\varepsilon$; the infimum dips below every $\varepsilon$. ($\Leftarrow$) If $\omega_f(x) = 0$: given $\varepsilon$, some window swings below $\varepsilon$; on it, $\lvert f(y) - f(x) \rvert \le \omega_f(W) < \varepsilon$ — both values live in the window’s range. That is continuity’s definition (Volume One, Chapter 6).

□
Remark 1.25 (Calibrations).

The static (Volume One, Definition 6.24): every window contains rationals and irrationals (Volume One, 2.15–2.16), so every swing is $1$: $\omega_D \equiv 1$. Thomae (Volume One, Definition 6.26): at irrational $x$, oscillation zero (continuity, Volume One, Theorem 6.27); at rational $p/q$ in lowest terms, oscillation exactly $1/q$ — the value there is $1/q$, while nearby values are small (the counting lemma inside Volume One’s Theorem 6.27 forces large denominators nearby) and irrationals nearby read zero, so the swing settles to the fraction’s own loudness. The step function: oscillation equal to the jump’s height at the jump, zero elsewhere. The cornered ramp (Volume One, Chapter 8): oscillation zero even at the corner — the gauge detects broken continuity, not broken smoothness.

Lemma 1.26 (The quiet sets are open).

For every $c > 0$, the set $V_c = \{ x : \omega_f(x) < c \}$ is open.

Proof.

Let $x \in V_c$. The infimum’s approximation property produces a witness window $W = (x - \delta, x + \delta)$ with $\omega_f(W) < c$. Every resident $y \in W$ owns a sub-window $W' \subseteq W$ centred at $y$ (openness of the interval), and swings only shrink on subsets: $\omega_f(W') \le \omega_f(W) < c$, so $\omega_f(y) < c$. Thus $W \subseteq V_c$: quietness certified by one window is inherited by everyone living in it, and $V_c$ contains a neighbourhood of each of its points — open, by the very definition (Volume One, Chapter 5).

□
Theorem 1.27 (Every continuity set is a $G_{\delta}$).

For every $f$, the set $C_f$ of points of continuity satisfies

\[ C_f \ =\ \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \, \Bigl\{ x : \omega_f(x) < \tfrac{1}{n} \Bigr\}, \]

a countable intersection of open sets.

Proof.

By Lemma 1.24, $x \in C_f$ iff $\omega_f(x) = 0$ iff $\omega_f(x) < 1/n$ for every $n$ — a nonnegative quantity below every threshold is zero, the Archimedean gate (Volume One, Theorem 2.12). Each threshold set is open by Lemma 1.26. No hypothesis on $f$ was used: the theorem binds every function whatsoever.

□

1.7 The Refusal and the Ceremony

Theorem 1.28 (The rationals are not a $G_{\delta}$).

There do not exist open sets $G_1, G_2, \dots$ with $\Q = \bigcap_n G_n$.

Proof.

Suppose otherwise. Each $G_n \supseteq \Q$ is open, and dense — it houses the dense tenant $\Q$ (Volume One, Theorem 2.15). For each rational $q$, the punctured line $\R \setminus \{q\}$ is open (a point is closed) and dense (no interval fits in a point). The family $\{G_n\}_n \cup \{\R \setminus \{q\}\}_{q \in \Q}$ is a countable family of open dense sets (countable plus countable, Volume One, Chapter 3). Its intersection is empty: a surviving point would be rational (unanimity of the $G_n$) yet avoid every rational (the punctures). But Baire’s theorem (Volume One, Theorem 5.28, with its dense-intersection reading) makes any countable intersection of open dense subsets of $\R$ dense, hence nonempty. Contradiction; the supposition dies. Monsieur Baire’s second and final engagement, discharged.

□
Theorem 1.29 (The ceremony: debt nine paid).

There is no function $f : \R \to \R$ whose set of continuity points is exactly $\Q$.

Proof.

$C_f$ is a $G_{\delta}$ (Theorem 1.27); $\Q$ is not (Theorem 1.28). Two lines, nine episodes in the arranging.

□
Remark 1.30 (The census closes exactly).

Thomae’s function realizes the irrationals as a continuity set (lawfully: they are $G_{\delta}$); the rationals are forbidden; and the characterization is complete in both directions: every $G_{\delta}$ subset of $\R$ is the continuity set of some function — Problem III.1, starred, the two-sign construction staged in hints. The continuity sets are precisely the $G_{\delta}$ sets; nothing else about a candidate set — cardinality, density, order — is relevant. II.2 (solution in full).  ($\Rightarrow$) is Proposition 1.3(i). ($\Leftarrow$) Let $E_1, E_2, \dots$ be pairwise disjoint members with union $E$. The partial unions $U_n = \bigcup_{k \le n} E_k$ form a swelling chain in the sigma-algebra with union $E$, so continuity gives $\mu(E) = \lim_n \mu(U_n)$. Finite additivity prices each stage: $\mu(U_n) = \sum_{k \le n} \mu(E_k)$, a partial sum. The limit of the partial sums is the series (nonnegative terms; Volume One, Chapter 4’s monotone license makes the limit exist in $[0,\infty]$): $\mu(E) = \sum_{k} \mu(E_k)$. Countable additivity holds; the engine and the clause are one mechanism seen from two sides, as the episode claimed.

II.3 (solution in full).  Fix $A$ and $t$. If $\{I_k\}$ covers $A$, then $\{I_k + t\}$ covers $A + t$ — $a \in A$ gives $a \in I_k$ for some $k$, so $a + t \in I_k + t$ — and translated intervals keep their lengths: $\lvert I_k + t \rvert = \lvert I_k \rvert$ (endpoints shift together). So every candidate total for $A$ is a candidate total for $A + t$: $m^{*}(A + t) \le m^{*}(A)$, the infimum running over a family at least as rich. Apply the same inequality to the set $A + t$ and the shift $-t$: $m^{*}(A) = m^{*}((A + t) + (-t)) \le m^{*}(A + t)$. The two inequalities close: $m^{*}(A + t) = m^{*}(A)$. Clause three of the contract is kept by the new instrument — the clause that will sign Chapter 3’s confession is, for now, in impeccable standing.

2.2 The Criterion

Definition 2.1 (Carathéodory measurability).

$E \subseteq \R$ is measurable when for every $T \subseteq \R$,

\[ m^{*}(T) \ =\ m^{*}(T \cap E) \; + \; m^{*}(T \setminus E). \]

The collection of measurable sets is written $\mathcal{M}$.

Remark 2.2 (The free direction).

$T = (T \cap E) \cup (T \setminus E)$, so countable subadditivity (Theorem 1.11, the gambit’s theorem) gives $m^{*}(T) \le m^{*}(T \cap E) + m^{*}(T \setminus E)$ always, for every $E$ and $T$. Membership in $\mathcal{M}$ is therefore exactly the reverse inequality

\[ m^{*}(T \cap E) + m^{*}(T \setminus E) \ \le\ m^{*}(T) \qquad \text{for every } T, \]

and this is the only inequality ever checked below: no phantom mass at the cut. (When $m^{*}(T) = \infty$ the reverse inequality is automatic; all content lives on finite test cakes.)

Proposition 2.3 (Null sets are citizens).

If $m^{*}(N) = 0$ then $N \in \mathcal{M}$; consequently (the null ideal being hereditary, Chapter 1) every subset of a null set is measurable. This property of $\mathcal{M}$ is called completeness.

Proof.

For any $T$: $m^{*}(T \cap N) \le m^{*}(N) = 0$ by monotonicity, and $m^{*}(T \setminus N) \le m^{*}(T)$ likewise. Their sum is at most $m^{*}(T)$; Remark 2.2 closes the pinch.

□
Theorem 2.4 (The turnstile: half-lines are citizens).

For every $a \in \R$: $(a, \infty) \in \mathcal{M}$.

Proof.

Let $T$ have $m^{*}(T) < \infty$ (Remark 2.2) and let $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose a cover $\{I_k\}$ of $T$ with $\sum_k \lvert I_k \rvert \le m^{*}(T) + \varepsilon$ (approximation property). The station $a$ splits each interval cleanly: $I_k' = I_k \cap (a, \infty)$ and $I_k'' = I_k \cap (-\infty, a]$ are intervals (possibly empty or degenerate) with $\lvert I_k' \rvert + \lvert I_k'' \rvert = \lvert I_k \rvert$ — schoolroom arithmetic, case by case on where $a$ sits relative to $I_k$. Now $\{I_k'\}$ covers $T \cap (a,\infty)$ and $\{I_k''\}$ covers $T \setminus (a, \infty)$, so

\[ m^{*}\bigl( T \cap (a,\infty) \bigr) + m^{*}\bigl( T \setminus (a,\infty) \bigr) \ \le\ \sum_k \lvert I_k' \rvert + \sum_k \lvert I_k'' \rvert \ =\ \sum_k \lvert I_k \rvert \ \le\ m^{*}(T) + \varepsilon . \]

As $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, the reverse inequality holds; the half-line balances every audit.

□

2.3 Carathéodory’s Theorem

Theorem 2.5 (Carathéodory, 1914).

$\mathcal{M}$ is a sigma-algebra containing every null set, and $m^{*}$ restricted to $\mathcal{M}$ is countably additive: for pairwise disjoint $E_1, E_2, \dots \in \mathcal{M}$,

\[ m^{*} \Bigl( \bigcup_k E_k \Bigr) \ =\ \sum_k m^{*}(E_k). \]

The proof is the season’s longest, as promised, and is taken in the episode’s four steps. Throughout, $T$ denotes an arbitrary test set with $m^{*}(T) < \infty$.

Lemma 2.6 (Step one: symmetry).

$\emptyset, \R \in \mathcal{M}$, and $E \in \mathcal{M}$ implies $E^{c} \in \mathcal{M}$.

Proof.

$T \cap \emptyset = \emptyset$ (outer measure zero) and $T \setminus \emptyset = T$: the empty set balances trivially, and $\R$ with it. The criterion is symmetric in $E$ and $E^{c}$, since $T \cap E^{c} = T \setminus E$ and $T \setminus E^{c} = T \cap E$: the two slices merely exchange names, and the balanced equation is the same equation.

□
Lemma 2.7 (Step two: composition).

If $E, F \in \mathcal{M}$ then $E \cup F \in \mathcal{M}$ (hence, with symmetry, $E \cap F$ and $E \setminus F$ as well: $\mathcal{M}$ is an algebra).

Proof.

The episode’s four pieces of $T$ are: within both ($T \cap E \cap F$); within $E$ only; within $F$ only; within neither ($T \setminus (E \cup F)$). The bookkeeping, tightly: cut $T$ by $E$ (exact, $E$ being a citizen); then cut the outside slice $T \setminus E$ by $F$ (exact again, the criterion holding for every test set — here is where the universal quantifier pays):

\[ m^{*}(T) = m^{*}(T \cap E) + m^{*}\bigl( (T \setminus E) \cap F \bigr) + m^{*}\bigl( (T \setminus E) \setminus F \bigr). \]

Now $(T \setminus E) \setminus F = T \setminus (E \cup F)$ exactly, and the union’s inside decomposes as $T \cap (E \cup F) = (T \cap E) \cup \bigl( (T \setminus E) \cap F \bigr)$ — a point of $T$ in the union is in $E$, or else in $F$ but not $E$. Subadditivity on that two-piece decomposition gives

\[ m^{*}\bigl( T \cap (E \cup F) \bigr) \ \le\ m^{*}(T \cap E) + m^{*}\bigl( (T \setminus E) \cap F \bigr), \]

and substituting into the display:

\[ m^{*}\bigl( T \cap (E \cup F) \bigr) + m^{*}\bigl( T \setminus (E \cup F) \bigr) \ \le\ m^{*}(T). \]

Remark 2.2 closes the pinch: honest knives, used in sequence, make an honest compound cut. Finite unions follow by iteration; intersections and differences by De Morgan with Lemma 2.6.

□
Lemma 2.8 (Step three: proportion).

Let $E_1, \dots, E_n \in \mathcal{M}$ be pairwise disjoint. Then for every test set $T$,

\[ m^{*} \Bigl( T \cap \bigcup_{k=1}^{n} E_k \Bigr) \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n} m^{*}(T \cap E_k). \]
Proof.

Induction on $n$; the case $n = 1$ is an identity. For the step, cut the cake $T \cap \bigcup_{k \le n} E_k$ with the knife $E_n$ — a citizen, so the cut is exact. By disjointness, the inside slice is

\[ \Bigl( T \cap \bigcup_{k \le n} E_k \Bigr) \cap E_n = T \cap E_n, \]

the newest piece alone, and the outside slice is $T \cap \bigcup_{k \le n-1} E_k$, the accumulated rest — nothing shared, nothing lost. Exactness reads

\[ m^{*} \Bigl( T \cap \bigcup_{k \le n} E_k \Bigr) = m^{*}(T \cap E_n) + m^{*} \Bigl( T \cap \bigcup_{k \le n-1} E_k \Bigr), \]

and the induction hypothesis expands the second term into the remaining sum.

□
Proof of Theorem 2.5 (step four: the limit).

Let $E_1, E_2, \dots \in \mathcal{M}$ be pairwise disjoint, with union $E$ and partial unions $U_n = \bigcup_{k \le n} E_k$ (citizens, by composition). Fix a test set $T$. For each $n$, cutting $T$ by $U_n$ exactly and applying proportion to the inside:

\[ m^{*}(T) \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n} m^{*}(T \cap E_k) \; + \; m^{*}(T \setminus U_n) \ \ge\ \sum_{k=1}^{n} m^{*}(T \cap E_k) \; + \; m^{*}(T \setminus E), \]

the last step by monotonicity ($T \setminus U_n \supseteq T \setminus E$: sieging armies only grow). The left side is free of $n$; let $n \to \infty$ (the monotone license: partial sums of nonnegative terms climb to the series):

\[ m^{*}(T) \ \ge\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} m^{*}(T \cap E_k) \; + \; m^{*}(T \setminus E) \ \ge\ m^{*}(T \cap E) + m^{*}(T \setminus E), \]

the second inequality by countable subadditivity applied to $T \cap E = \bigcup_k (T \cap E_k)$. By Remark 2.2 the outer terms are equal, so every inequality in the chain is an equality. Reading the chain twice: first, $E$ satisfies the criterion — $\mathcal{M}$ is closed under disjoint countable unions, and with the algebra operations of Lemma 2.7, under arbitrary countable unions (disjointify: $A_k' = A_k \setminus \bigcup_{j < k} A_j \in \mathcal{M}$, same union) — the walls are complete, and $\mathcal{M}$ is a sigma-algebra. Second, take $T = E$: then $T \cap E_k = E_k$ and $T \setminus E = \emptyset$, and the equality of the chain reads

\[ m^{*}(E) \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} m^{*}(E_k): \]

countable additivity on the tribe. Null citizens were Proposition 2.3. The machinery bill: subadditivity, induction, the monotone license — no compactness, no Baire, no new ring of the bell. The definition carried the depth; the proof collected it.

□
Corollary 2.9 (The Borel estate immigrates entire).

$\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{M}$: every Borel set is measurable.

Proof.

$\mathcal{M}$ is a sigma-algebra (Theorem 2.5) containing every half-line (Theorem 2.4), hence every interval ($(a,b] = (a,\infty) \setminus (b,\infty)$, and kin by the algebra operations), hence — the estate’s robustness, Theorem 1.20 — all of $\mathcal{B} = \sigma(\text{intervals})$: the smallest sigma-algebra containing the intervals sits inside any sigma-algebra containing them.

2.4 Lebesgue Measure and Its Credentials

Definition 2.10 (Lebesgue measure).

$m = m^{*} \restriction_{\mathcal{M}}$: outer measure, restricted to the measurable tribe.

Corollary 2.11 (The contract, settled).

On $\mathcal{M}$, all five clauses of Chapter 1’s contract hold: $m \ge 0$ with $m(\emptyset) = 0$; $m(I) = b - a$ on intervals; $m(E + t) = m(E)$; and countable additivity. Moreover the crankshaft dividends operate legally: continuity along swelling chains, and along shrinking chains with a finite start (Proposition 1.3, whose hypotheses $\mathcal{M}$ and $m$ now satisfy).

Proof.

Clauses one, two, three are Chapter 1’s theorems for $m^{*}$, inherited by restriction (intervals are citizens by Corollary 2.9); clause four is Theorem 2.5. The crankshaft was proved for any countably additive assignment on a sigma-algebra.

□
Proposition 2.12 (Citizenship translates).

If $E \in \mathcal{M}$ and $t \in \R$, then $E + t \in \mathcal{M}$.

Proof.

For any test $T$: $T \cap (E + t) = \bigl( (T - t) \cap E \bigr) + t$ and $T \setminus (E + t) = \bigl( (T - t) \setminus E \bigr) + t$ (shift the whole audit by $-t$, cut, shift back). Translation invariance of $m^{*}$ (Chapter 1, Problem II.3) prices the shifted slices identically, and $E$ cuts the shifted cake $T - t$ exactly.

□
Theorem 2.13 (Uniqueness: nothing else was on offer).

Let $\nu$ be countably additive on $\mathcal{M}$ (or on $\mathcal{B}$), with $\nu(I) = \lvert I \rvert$ on every interval. Then $\nu = m$ on its domain.

Proof.

On open sets: beads (Volume One, Proposition 5.3) and countable additivity force $\nu(U) = \sum \lvert \text{beads} \rvert = m(U)$. Within a frame $[-N, N]$, for measurable $E \subseteq [-N,N]$: given $\varepsilon$, Theorem 2.16 supplies open $U \supseteq E$ (intersected with an open frame-pad, still open) with $m(U) \le m(E) + \varepsilon$; then $\nu(E) \le \nu(U) = m(U) \le m(E) + \varepsilon$, so $\nu(E) \le m(E)$ — monotonicity of $\nu$ being additivity’s child. Run the same estimate on the frame’s remainder $[-N,N] \setminus E$ and add: both split the frame, whose $\nu$- and $m$-prices agree (an interval), so the two $\le$'s must both be equalities: $\nu(E) = m(E)$ on framed citizens. Unframed citizens are swelling unions of framed ones ($E \cap [-N,N] \uparrow E$), and both parties obey continuity from below (Corollary 2.11 for $m$; the crankshaft, whose proof used only countable additivity, for $\nu$). Frame-patching details: Problem II.6’s ambit. The translation-invariant strengthening — $\nu$ shift-blind and finite on frames forces $\nu = c \cdot m$ — is Problem III.2.

□

2.5 Where the Typo Came From

Definition 2.15 (Lebesgue’s inner measure, 1902).

For bounded $E$ contained in a frame $[a,b]$:

\[ m_{*}(E) \ =\ (b - a) \; - \; m^{*}\bigl( [a,b] \setminus E \bigr), \]

the frame’s length less the complement’s outer price (independent of the chosen frame: enlarging the frame adds equal amounts to both terms — Problem II.2’s warm-up). Lebesgue’s original definition: a bounded set is measurable when $m_{*}(E) = m^{*}(E)$ — cage meets packing, the two-ledger architecture of Darboux and Jordan one storey up.

The reconciliation — on bounded sets, inner-meets-outer holds if and only if Carathéodory’s criterion does — is Problem II.2, solved in full. One direction is transparent (the frame is one test cake; Carathéodory quantifies over all); the other is the useful surprise: balancing the frame suffices to balance every cake, outer regularity doing the generalizing.

2.6 The Measure’s Manners

Theorem 2.16 (Approximation: open surpluses, closed and compact certificates).

Let $E \in \mathcal{M}$ and $\varepsilon > 0$.

  1. (i)

    There is an open $U \supseteq E$ with $m(U \setminus E) \le \varepsilon$.

  2. (ii)

    There is a closed $F \subseteq E$ with $m(E \setminus F) \le \varepsilon$.

  3. (iii)

    If $E$ is bounded, the $F$ of (ii) is compact.

Proof.

(i) First suppose $m(E) < \infty$. A cover within $\varepsilon$ of $m(E)$, fattened open (Chapter 1’s fattening trick, at cost folded into $\varepsilon$), unions to an open $U \supseteq E$ with $m(U) \le \sum \lvert J_k \rvert \le m(E) + \varepsilon$ (subadditivity); additivity on the citizen pair $(E, \, U \setminus E)$ converts the total into a surplus: $m(U \setminus E) = m(U) - m(E) \le \varepsilon$ — subtraction lawful, the minuend finite. For $m(E) = \infty$: frame the line, $E_N = E \cap [-N, N]$; take open $U_N \supseteq E_N$ with surplus $\varepsilon / 2^{N}$ (the gambit, as ever); $U = \bigcup U_N$ is open, contains $E$, and $U \setminus E \subseteq \bigcup (U_N \setminus E_N)$ prices below $\varepsilon$ by subadditivity.

(ii) Complements: apply (i) to $E^{c}$ (a citizen, by symmetry), obtaining open $V \supseteq E^{c}$ with $m(V \setminus E^{c}) \le \varepsilon$; then $F = V^{c}$ is closed, $F \subseteq E$, and $E \setminus F = V \setminus E^{c}$ exactly.

(iii) A closed subset of a bounded set is closed and bounded: compact (Volume One, Theorem 5.18’s characterization). The bounded case is Problem II.3, solved in full with the frame bookkeeping displayed; the display above is its skeleton.

□
Theorem 2.17 (The structural jewel: Borel plus veil).

$E \subseteq \R$ is measurable if and only if $E = G \setminus Z$ for some $G_{\delta}$ set $G$ and null $Z$ — equivalently $E = F \cup Z'$ with $F$ an $F_{\sigma}$ and $Z'$ null.

Proof.

($\Rightarrow$) Take open $U_n \supseteq E$ with surplus $1/n$ (Theorem 2.16(i)); $G = \bigcap_n U_n$ is a $G_{\delta}$ containing $E$, and $G \setminus E \subseteq U_n \setminus E$ has measure at most $1/n$ for every $n$: null. Then $E = G \setminus (G \setminus E)$. The $F_{\sigma}$ form is the mirrored (ii). ($\Leftarrow$) $G_{\delta}$ sets are Borel, hence citizens (Corollary 2.9); null sets are citizens (Proposition 2.3); and $\mathcal{M}$ is closed under difference and union. — Every citizen is a Borel body wearing a null veil; the commonwealth’s vastness is entirely veil.

□

The Cantor set $C$ (Volume One, Definition 3.20) is measurable with $m(C) = 0$ — an uncountable set (Volume One, Chapter 3) of measure zero.

2.8 The Fat Dust and the Two Courts

Proposition 2.19 (Smith–Volterra–Cantor).

Construct $S \subseteq [0,1]$ by removing, at stage $n$, from each of the $2^{n-1}$ surviving closed pieces its open middle interval of length $4^{-n}$. Then $S$ is closed, nowhere dense (contains no interval; Volume One, Definition 5.27), and

\[ m(S) \ =\ 1 - \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{n-1} \cdot 4^{-n} \ =\ 1 - \tfrac12 \ =\ \tfrac12 . \]

Fattening the removals to total any $\varepsilon$ yields closed nowhere-dense sets of measure $1 - \varepsilon$; measure exactly $1$ is unattainable (the complement is open, dense, and nonempty, hence contains an interval of positive measure).

Proof.

The removals are pairwise disjoint open intervals; their total, the geometric series $\sum_n 2^{n-1} 4^{-n} = \tfrac12 \sum_n 2^{-n} = \tfrac12$. $S$ is the complement in $[0,1]$ of their union: closed, and citizen’s arithmetic (additivity, subtraction within the finite frame) prices it at $\tfrac12$. Nowhere dense: the stage-$n$ construction leaves $2^{n}$ disjoint closed pieces of total length $1 - r_n < 1$, so each piece is shorter than $2^{-n} \cdot 1$; an interval $I \subseteq S$, being connected, would lie within a single piece at every stage, forcing $\lvert I \rvert \le 2^{-n} \to 0$. A closed set with empty interior is nowhere dense.

□
Proposition 2.20 (The stacked dusts: meagre yet full).

Let $S_j$ be fat dusts with removals totalling $2^{-j}$, and $M = \bigcup_j S_j$. Then $M$ is meagre (a countable union of nowhere-dense sets) with $m(M) = 1$; its complement $R = [0,1] \setminus M$ is residual in $[0,1]$ and null. In particular $[0,1] = M \sqcup R$ partitions the interval into a meagre set and a null set.

Proof.

Meagreness is the definition. $m(M) \ge m(S_j) = 1 - 2^{-j}$ for every $j$ (monotonicity), and $m(M) \le 1$: so $m(M) = 1$. Additivity within the frame prices $R$ at nought; $R$ is the complement of a meagre set in $[0,1]$, which is residual by definition. Formalities of the partition claim: Problem II.5.

□
Remark 2.21 (The two courts; the standing convention).

Proposition 2.22 (Jordan absorbed).

Every Jordan-measurable bounded set $A$ (Chapter 1, Definition 1.4) is Lebesgue measurable, and $m(A) = c(A)$, the Jordan content.

Proof.

Then $A = A^{\circ} \cup (A \cap \partial A)$: an open citizen united with a subset of a null set, measurable by completeness (Proposition 2.3) and the algebra. The old deeds transfer unaltered.

2.10 Completion, and the Size of the Commonwealth

Definition 2.23 (Completion of a measure).

Given a sigma-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ with a countably additive $\mu$, its completion $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ consists of all sets $A \cup Z$ with $A \in \mathcal{A}$ and $Z$ a subset of some $\mu$-null member of $\mathcal{A}$; $\overline{\mu}(A \cup Z) = \mu(A)$. (Well-definedness and the sigma-algebra checks: Problem III.1, with the universal property — the smallest complete extension.)

Theorem 2.24 (Lebesgue is completed Borel).

$\mathcal{M} = \overline{\mathcal{B}}$: the measurable sets are exactly the Borel sets adjusted by subsets of Borel null sets, and $m$ is the completion of its own restriction to $\mathcal{B}$.

Proof.

($\supseteq$) Borel sets are citizens; subsets of null sets are citizens (completeness); unions of citizens are citizens. ($\subseteq$) The structural jewel (Theorem 2.17), $F_{\sigma}$ form: $E = F \cup Z'$ with $F$ Borel and $Z'$ null — and $Z'$, being null, sits inside a Borel null set (the $G_{\delta}$ hull of Theorem 2.17 applied to $Z'$). Measures agree since $\overline{m}(F \cup Z') = m(F) = m(E)$.

3.2 The Vitali Construction

Proposition 3.1 (The sorting).

The relation $x \sim y \iff x - y \in \Q$ is an equivalence on $\R$. Each class $[x] = x + \Q$ is countable and dense in $\R$; the classes partition $\R$; and there are uncountably many classes.

Proof.

Reflexive: $x - x = 0 \in \Q$. Symmetric: negatives of rationals are rational. Transitive: $(x - y) + (y - z) = x - z$, and $\Q$ is closed under addition (Volume One, Chapter 2). Each class is the bijective image $q \mapsto x + q$ of $\Q$: countable, and dense since $\Q$ is (translation preserves density). Partition: equivalence classes always partition. Uncountably many: a countable family of countable classes would render $\R$ countable (the census, Volume One, Chapter 3, read in reverse) — contradicting the diagonal.

□
Definition 3.2 (The delegation — a conjuring, declared).

Each class meets $[0,1]$ (density). By the Axiom of Choice, select exactly one point of $[0,1]$ from each class; let $V$ be the set of delegates. No rule performs the selection: the classes are structureless, uncountably many, and almost all their members are unnameable (Volume One, Chapter 3’s census); the axiom asserts the selection’s existence and provides nothing further.

Lemma 3.3 (The tiling).

Let $q_1, q_2, \dots$ enumerate $\Q \cap [-1, 1]$. Then:

  1. (i)

    the sets $V + q_k$ are pairwise disjoint;

  2. (ii)

    $[0,1] \ \subseteq\ \bigcup_k (V + q_k) \ \subseteq\ [-1, 2]$.

Proof.

(i) If $v + q_k = w + q_j$ with $v, w \in V$, then $v - w = q_j - q_k \in \Q$: $v \sim w$, so $v = w$ (one delegate per class), whence $q_k = q_j$.

(ii) Right inclusion: $V \subseteq [0,1]$ and $q_k \in [-1,1]$. Left: let $x \in [0,1]$; its class has a delegate $d \in V \cap [0,1]$; then $q = x - d \in \Q$ and $q \in [-1, 1]$ (both points in $[0,1]$), so $q = q_k$ for some $k$ and $x \in V + q_k$.

□
Theorem 3.4 (Vitali, 1905: the catch, proved).

$V$ is not Lebesgue measurable. Consequently there is no assignment $\mu$ on all subsets of $\R$ satisfying the contract of Chapter 1 (nonnegativity, intervals to length, translation invariance, countable additivity).

Proof.

Suppose $V \in \mathcal{M}$ with $m(V) = v$. Citizenship translates (Chapter 2), so each $V + q_k \in \mathcal{M}$ with measure $v$; the union $U = \bigsqcup_k (V + q_k)$ is a countable disjoint union of citizens, and countable additivity prices it:

\[ m(U) \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} m(V + q_k) \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} v \ =\ \begin{cases} 0, & v = 0,\\ \infty, & v > 0 \end{cases} \]

(a series of identical nonnegative terms admits no third verdict; Volume One, Chapter 4). But the sandwich (Lemma 3.3) and monotonicity demand

\[ 1 = m([0,1]) \ \le\ m(U) \ \le\ m([-1,2]) = 3 . \]

Door one ($v = 0$): $m(U) = 0 < 1$. Door two ($v > 0$): $m(U) = \infty > 3$. Both contradict. So $V \notin \mathcal{M}$. For the second statement: the argument above used only the contract’s clauses — additivity, translation invariance, interval pricing, and monotonicity (additivity’s child) — so any full-contract $\mu$ on all sets meets the same two doors with $\mu(V)$ in place of $v$. No such $\mu$ exists. Debt thirteen: proved as charged.

□
Proposition 3.5 (The ghost’s ledgers).

$m^{*}(V) > 0$, while every closed (indeed every measurable) $F \subseteq V$ is null — the inner measure of $V$ is zero.

Proof.

If $m^{*}(V) = 0$, $V$ would be null, hence a measurable citizen (Chapter 2), contradicting Theorem 3.4. For the inner ledger, let $F \subseteq V$ be measurable. The shifts $F + q_k$ are pairwise disjoint (inherited from Lemma 3.3(i)) and lie in $[-1,2]$; countable additivity and invariance give $\sum_k m(F) \le 3$, forcing $m(F) = 0$.

□
Theorem 3.6 (The ghost factory).

Every $E \subseteq \R$ with $m^{*}(E) > 0$ contains a non-measurable subset.

Proof.

It suffices to treat $E \subseteq [0,1]$ ($E$ meets some unit frame in positive outer measure — countable subadditivity across frames — and subsets transfer). Write $E = \bigcup_k \bigl( E \cap (V + q_k) \bigr)$, the tiling restricted to $E$ (Lemma 3.3(ii), with $\Q \cap [-1,1]$ re-enumerated as needed). If every piece $E \cap (V + q_k)$ were measurable, each would be null — it sits inside a translate of $V$, and Proposition 3.5’s argument applies verbatim to measurable subsets of translates — whence $E$, a countable union of null sets, would be null: contradicting $m^{*}(E) > 0$. Some piece is non-measurable.

3.3 The Alphabet Trick

Definition 3.9 (Equidecomposability; paradoxical sets).

Let a group $G$ act on a set $X$. Subsets $A, B \subseteq X$ are $G$-equidecomposable, $A \sim_G B$, when $A$ and $B$ admit partitions $A = \bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i$, $B = \bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n} B_i$ with $B_i = g_i A_i$ for some $g_i \in G$. (Transitivity requires refining pieces against pieces: Problem II.4.)

Definition 3.10 (Paradoxical).

$X$ is $G$-paradoxical when $X$ contains disjoint subsets $A, B$ with $A \sim_G X$ and $B \sim_G X$: two full copies, carved from one, with room to spare.

Theorem 3.11 (The free group doubles).

Let $F$ be the free group on letters $a, b$, acting on itself by left multiplication, and for each symbol $x \in \{a, a^{-1}, b, b^{-1}\}$ let $W(x)$ be the set of reduced words with leading symbol $x$. Then

\[ F \ =\ \{e\} \sqcup W(a) \sqcup W(a^{-1}) \sqcup W(b) \sqcup W(b^{-1}), \]
\[ F \ =\ W(a) \ \sqcup\ a \, W(a^{-1}), \qquad F \ =\ W(b) \ \sqcup\ b \, W(b^{-1}), \]

whence $F$ is $F$-paradoxical: the four bins assemble two copies of the dictionary, the empty word to spare.

Proof.

The five-fold partition is the definition of “leading symbol.” For the first reassembly: $a \, W(a^{-1})$ consists of the reductions of $a w$ with $w$ leading with $a^{-1}$ — write $w = a^{-1} u$ where $u$ is reduced and does not lead with $a$ (else $w$ was not reduced); then $a w = u$. As $w$ ranges over $W(a^{-1})$, $u$ ranges over exactly the reduced words not leading with $a$ (including $e$, from $w = a^{-1}$): $a \, W(a^{-1}) = F \setminus W(a)$. Disjointness is the same computation read backward: a word cannot both lead with $a$ and be the reduction of $a \cdot (a^{-1}u)$ with $u$ not leading with $a$. The $b$-reassembly mirrors. Paradoxicality: take $A = W(a) \cup W(a^{-1})$ and $B = W(b) \cup W(b^{-1})$ — disjoint; each reassembles $F$ with one one-letter motion. (Guided as the Tier II summit, Problem II.5, where every cancellation is tracked.)

□
Remark 3.12 (Commutative alphabets collapse).

If $ab = ba$, the word $a b a^{-1} b^{-1}$ reduces to $e$: bins leak into one another, the leading-symbol sorting is not even well-defined on normal forms, and no four-bin doubling exists — correctly so, since Banach’s extensions (Remark 3.16) forbid paradox in the commutative motion groups of the line and plane. Freedom of the letters is the paradox’s fuel; the desk’s amenability note (Problem 3.5) is this observation grown up.

3.4 The Sphere and the Ball

Remark 3.13 (Free rotations: the fingerprint).

Two rotations by $\arccos \tfrac13$ about perpendicular axes generate a free subgroup of the rotation group: in coordinates, each letter multiplies entries by thirds, and a reduced word of length $n$ carries an uncancellable factor $3^{-n}$ in designated entries — a nontrivial word cannot act as the identity, whose entries are whole. Classical references (\'Swierczkowski; Wagon, ch. 2); the fingerprint staged for the ambitious at Problem III.1.

Theorem 3.14 (The sphere and ball double — on stated references).

The sphere’s surface, minus the countable set of poles of the free rotations’ words, is paradoxical under rotations: sort the remaining points into orbits of the embedded free group; by Choice (conjuring, declared) select one delegate per orbit; the four bins of Theorem 3.11, applied to the delegates’ words, carve the punctured sphere into four pieces that reassemble two punctured spheres. The countable exceptional set is absorbed (a rotation of infinite order moves it off itself; the hotel manoeuvre — circle rehearsal at Problem II.6); radial cones carry the sphere’s carving to the punctured ball; the centre is absorbed by a rotated interior circle; and with Robinson’s optimization, five pieces suffice (and four do not). Full assembly: Wagon, chs. 2–4; Stromberg’s Monthly exposition; Tomkowicz–Wagon.

Theorem 3.15 (Banach–Tarski, 1924 — stated).

A closed ball in space is equidecomposable, under rigid motions, with the disjoint union of two copies of itself — in five pieces. Strong form: any two bounded subsets of space with nonempty interior are equidecomposable (the pea and the sun). No proof is attempted here beyond Theorem 3.14’s staging; the references carry the construction entire.

Remark 3.16 (The defusal; dimensions one and two).

II.5 (solution in full).  Partition. Every nonempty reduced word has a unique leading symbol: the five parcels are disjoint and exhaustive by definition.

Reassembly. Fix $w \in W(a^{-1})$: $w = a^{-1} u$ with $u$ reduced and not leading with $a$ (an $a$ after $a^{-1}$ would have cancelled in $w$). Then $a w = a a^{-1} u = u$. The map $w \mapsto a w$ thus sends $W(a^{-1})$ into the reduced words not leading with $a$; it is onto them — given $u$ not leading with $a$, the word $w = a^{-1} u$ is reduced (no cancellation at the join, $u$ not leading with $a$) and leads with $a^{-1}$, with $a w = u$; and it is injective (left multiplication in a group). So $a\,W(a^{-1}) = F \setminus W(a)$ exactly, including $e = a \cdot a^{-1}$. Disjointness: $W(a) \cap a W(a^{-1}) = W(a) \cap (F \setminus W(a)) = \emptyset$. Union: $W(a) \cup a W(a^{-1}) = W(a) \cup (F \setminus W(a)) = F$. The $b$-computation is the same with letters renamed.

4.2 Measurable Functions

Definition 4.1 (Measurable function).

Let $E \in \mathcal{M}$. A function $f : E \to [-\infty, \infty]$ is measurable when $\{ f > a \} = \{ x \in E : f(x) > a \}$ is a citizen for every $a \in \R$.

Proposition 4.2 (Equivalent thresholds; Borel preimages).

For $f : E \to [-\infty, \infty]$ the following are equivalent: $\{f > a\} \in \mathcal{M}$ for all $a$; $\{f \ge a\} \in \mathcal{M}$ for all $a$; $\{f < a\} \in \mathcal{M}$ for all $a$; $\{f \le a\} \in \mathcal{M}$ for all $a$. Any of them implies $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{M}$ for every Borel $B \subseteq \R$.

Proof.

$\{f \ge a\} = \bigcap_n \{f > a - \tfrac1n\}$ and $\{f > a\} = \bigcup_n \{f \ge a + \tfrac1n\}$: countable tribe operations convert the two strict/weak forms; complements within $E$ convert to the two below-forms. For Borel preimages: the collection $\{ B : f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{M} \}$ is a sigma-algebra (preimages respect complements and unions), and it contains the half-lines, hence the intervals, hence — the estate’s robustness, Chapter 1, Theorem 1.20 — all of $\mathcal{B}$.

□
Proposition 4.3 (The parade).

Continuous functions on citizens are measurable ($\{f > a\}$ is the trace of an open set); monotone functions (the pile above a waterline is an interval or half-line trace); step and simple functions; indicators of citizens; the static and Thomae’s function; and any function on a null ground. Ghost indicators ($\mathbf{1}_{V}$, $V$ Vitali’s delegation) are the canonical non-measurable specimens.

Proof.

Each is a one-line pile audit; the desk records the static’s: $\{D > a\}$ is $\emptyset$ ($a \ge 1$), $\Q \cap E$ ($0 \le a < 1$), or $E$ ($a < 0$) — citizens all. Problem I.1 drills the rest.

Proposition 4.4 (The algebra).

If $f, g$ are measurable (finite-valued where combined) and $c \in \R$: $cf$, $f + g$, $fg$, $\lvert f \rvert$, $\max(f,g)$, $\min(f,g)$ are measurable.

Proof.

The sum, the one with an idea: $f + g > a$ iff some rational $q$ has $f > q$ and $g > a - q$ (density splits the surplus), so $\{f + g > a\} = \bigcup_{q \in \Q} \bigl( \{f > q\} \cap \{g > a - q\} \bigr)$ — a countable union of citizens; the picket fence’s contract, executed at the function level. Products via $fg = \tfrac14 \bigl( (f+g)^2 - (f-g)^2 \bigr)$ once squares are settled ($\{f^2 > a\}$ unwinds to two piles); moduli and max/min are unions and intersections of piles. Problem I.1’s second half.

Proposition 4.5 (Composition: the safe direction).

If $f$ is measurable and $g : \R \to \R$ is Borel measurable (in particular continuous), then $g \circ f$ is measurable. The reverse composition — a measurable function of a continuous function — can fail: Theorem 4.22.

Proof.

$(g \circ f)^{-1}(a, \infty) = f^{-1}\bigl( g^{-1}(a,\infty) \bigr)$: $g^{-1}(a, \infty)$ is Borel, and Proposition 4.2 finishes.

Theorem 4.6 (Limit stability — the crown of the class).

Let $f_1, f_2, \dots$ be measurable on $E$. Then $\sup_n f_n$, $\inf_n f_n$, $\limsup_n f_n$, $\liminf_n f_n$ are measurable; and if $f_n \to f$ pointwise on $E$, then $f$ is measurable.

Proof.

4.3 Atoms, Staircases, and the Integral

Definition 4.7 (Simple functions; the merchant’s multiplication).

$\varphi$ is simple when it takes finitely many values, each on a citizen: $\varphi = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ with $A_i \in \mathcal{M}$ disjoint. For simple $\varphi \ge 0$,

\[ \int \varphi \, dm \ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \, m(A_i) \qquad (0 \cdot \infty = 0). \]
Proposition 4.8 (Well-priced; simple linearity and monotonicity).

The price is independent of the representation; for simple $\varphi, \psi \ge 0$ and $c \ge 0$: $\int (\varphi + \psi) = \int \varphi + \int \psi$, $\int c\varphi = c \int \varphi$, and $\varphi \le \psi \Rightarrow \int \varphi \le \int \psi$.

Proof.

Theorem 4.9 (The staircase of the range).

Let $f : E \to [0, \infty]$ be measurable. Define

\[ \varphi_n \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n 2^{n}} \frac{k-1}{2^{n}} \, \mathbf{1}_{A_{n,k}} \; + \; n \, \mathbf{1}_{\{f \ge n\}}, \qquad A_{n,k} = \Bigl\{ \tfrac{k-1}{2^n} \le f < \tfrac{k}{2^n} \Bigr\}. \]

Each $\varphi_n$ is simple; $\varphi_n \le \varphi_{n+1} \le f$; and $\varphi_n \to f$ pointwise on $E$ — uniformly on any set where $f$ is bounded.

Proof.

The bins $A_{n,k}$ are differences of piles: citizens (Proposition 4.2); finitely many storeys: simple. Monotonicity: passing $n \to n+1$ halves each denomination — a point binned at $\tfrac{k-1}{2^n}$ is re-binned at $\tfrac{2k-2}{2^{n+1}}$ or $\tfrac{2k-1}{2^{n+1}}$, never lower — and raises the ceiling. Convergence: if $f(x) < \infty$, then for $n > f(x)$ the assignment sits within $2^{-n}$ of $f(x)$; if $f(x) = \infty$, the assignments are $n \uparrow \infty$. Where $f \le K$: for $n > K$ the error is uniformly under $2^{-n}$.

Definition 4.10 ($(\circledast)$ The integral — ring sixteen).

For measurable $f : E \to [0, \infty]$,

\[ \int_{E} f \, dm \ =\ \sup \Bigl\{ \int \varphi \, dm \ : \ 0 \le \varphi \le f, \ \varphi \text{ simple} \Bigr\} . \]

The bell rings — the sixteenth: the supremum over the inventory of under-staircases exists in $[0, \infty]$ by the completeness axiom (finite case) and honest convention (infinite), the same authority that manufactured the Darboux ledgers at rings thirteen and fourteen. Nothing is refused; the value $\infty$ is an honest verdict.

Remark 4.11 (The handshake: the identity, re-integrated).

For $f(x) = x$ on $[0,1]$, the staircase $\varphi_n$ of Theorem 4.9 has bins $A_{n,k} = [\tfrac{k-1}{2^n}, \tfrac{k}{2^n})$ of measure $2^{-n}$, so

\[ \int \varphi_n \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}} \frac{k-1}{2^{n}} \cdot \frac{1}{2^{n}} \ =\ \frac{1}{4^{n}} \cdot \frac{2^{n}(2^{n}-1)}{2} \ =\ \frac12 - \frac{1}{2^{n+1}} \ \uparrow\ \frac12 , \]

and no under-staircase exceeds $\tfrac12$ (each is dominated by the Riemann upper ledger, or directly: Problem I.3). The new integral returns the schoolroom’s one half; the general agreement is Theorem 4.19.

Definition 4.12 (Integrable; the general integral).

For measurable $f$: $f^{\pm} = \max(\pm f, 0)$ are measurable (Proposition 4.4), $f = f^{+} - f^{-}$, $\lvert f \rvert = f^{+} + f^{-}$. $f$ is integrable when $\int \lvert f \rvert \, dm < \infty$ (equivalently both $\int f^{\pm} < \infty$), and then $\int f \, dm = \int f^{+} - \int f^{-}$ — lawful subtraction. If exactly one part is infinite the integral is $\pm\infty$; if both, undefined.

Proposition 4.13 (Manners within tonight’s reach).

(i) Monotonicity: $0 \le f \le g$ measurable $\Rightarrow \int f \le \int g$ (every under-staircase of $f$ under-staircases $g$). (ii) $\int c f = c \int f$ for $c \ge 0$ (staircases scale). (iii) Ground conventions: $\int_E f = \int f \mathbf{1}_E$, the two definitions agreeing storey by storey. (iv) $f = 0$ a.e. $\Rightarrow \int f = 0$ (every under-staircase’s storeys of positive value stand on null ground). (v) Bounded additivity: for bounded measurable $f, g$ on finite-measure ground, $\int (f + g) = \int f + \int g$ — via Proposition 4.14’s sandwiches (Problem II.2’s ambit).

Proposition 4.14 (The bounded estate: two ledgers meet).

Let $f$ be measurable and bounded on $E$ with $m(E) < \infty$. Then

\[ \sup_{\substack{\varphi \le f \\ \varphi \text{ simple}}} \int \varphi \ =\ \inf_{\substack{\psi \ge f \\ \psi \text{ simple}}} \int \psi . \]
Proof.

4.4 The Old Regime, Absorbed

Remark 4.18 (The boundary post: improper integrals).

The absorption below covers the proper Riemann integral. A conditionally convergent improper integral — finite by cancellation between infinite gains and infinite losses — has $\int \lvert f \rvert = \infty$ and is refused by Definition 4.12, on the same grounds Volume One’s rearrangement theorem convicted conditional series: totals that depend on the order of summation have no order-free value, and the sorted count is order-free by construction. Theorem 4.19 (Riemann absorbed).

Let $f$ be (properly) Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$. Then $f$ is measurable, integrable, and $\int_{[a,b]} f \, dm = \text{(R)}\!\int_a^b f$.

Proof.

$f$ is bounded (Riemann’s ticket, Volume One). Take partitions $P_n$, each refining the last, mesh $\to 0$, with Darboux ledgers $L(f, P_n) \uparrow$ and $U(f, P_n) \downarrow$ toward the common Riemann value $R$ (integrability). Refinement makes $\varphi_n \uparrow \varphi$ and $\psi_n \downarrow \psi$ pointwise (monotone bounded sequences; Volume One’s license), with $\varphi \le f \le \psi$; both limits are measurable (Theorem 4.6). On the finite-measure frame, the bounded estate prices

\[ \int (\psi - \varphi) \ \le\ \int (\psi_n - \varphi_n) \ =\ U(f, P_n) - L(f, P_n) \ \longrightarrow\ 0 \]

(monotonicity; bounded additivity for the differences, Proposition 4.13(v)); so $\int (\psi - \varphi) = 0$ with $\psi - \varphi \ge 0$, forcing $\psi = \varphi$ a.e. (Problem I.6’s little lemma: a nonnegative function of zero integral vanishes a.e.). Then $f$, trapped between a.e.-equal measurable functions, equals $\varphi$ a.e.: measurable by amnesty (Proposition 4.16(ii)), and

\[ \int f \ =\ \int \varphi \ =\ \lim_n \int \varphi_n \ =\ \lim_n L(f, P_n) \ =\ R \]

(the middle equality by the bounded two-ledger pinch against $\psi_n$; partition endpoints, countably many, are null and filed).

4.6 The Devil’s Staircase

Theorem 4.21 (The Cantor function — debt eight’s account closes).

There is a function $c : [0,1] \to [0,1]$, continuous and nondecreasing, with $c(0) = 0$, $c(1) = 1$, constant on each interval removed in the dust’s construction — hence differentiable with $c' = 0$ at almost every point — and $\int_{[0,1]} c \, dm = \tfrac12$. Its entire unit of ascent is accomplished on the null dust.

Proof.

Construction. Assign to the stage-$n$ removed middles, read left to right, the values $\tfrac{1}{2^n}, \tfrac{3}{2^n}, \dots, \tfrac{2^n - 1}{2^n}$ (each middle the dyadic mean of its neighbours’ values); the assignments are consistent under staging and monotone across the complement. For $x \in C$ set $c(x) = \sup \{ c(t) : t \text{ in removed middles}, t \le x \}$ (sup over a nonempty bounded set — small change), with $c(0) = 0$; monotonicity extends.

Continuity. $c$ is monotone with range dense in $[0,1]$ — every dyadic value is attained on a middle. A monotone function’s only discontinuities are jumps, and a jump omits an interval of values, contradicting dense range. So $c$ is continuous (and $c(1) = 1$ by the sup over values climbing to one).

Flatness a.e. On each removed middle $c$ is constant, so $c' = 0$ there; the middles carry measure one (Chapter 2); the dust where ascent happens is null.

The integral. The construction is symmetric under $x \mapsto 1 - x$ with values reflecting through $\tfrac12$: $c(x) + c(1 - x) = 1$ for all $x$ (verified on middles, extended by continuity). Reflection invariance of $m$ (Chapter 3, Problem I.7) gives $\int c(1 - x) \, dm = \int c \, dm$, so $2 \int c = \int \bigl( c(x) + c(1-x) \bigr) \, dm = m([0,1]) = 1$ (bounded additivity), and $\int c = \tfrac12$.

5.2 The Climbing License

Theorem 5.1 (Monotone convergence — Levi, 1906).

Let $f_n : E \to [0, \infty]$ be measurable with $f_n \le f_{n+1}$ pointwise and $f_n \to f$ pointwise. Then $f$ is measurable and

\[ \int_E f_n \, dm \ \uparrow\ \int_E f \, dm . \]

(A.e. monotonicity and convergence suffice: patch the null set, Proposition 4.16.)

Proof.

$f$ is measurable as a pointwise limit (Theorem 4.6). Free half: $f_n \le f$ gives $\int f_n \le \int f$ (manners); the integrals climb (monotonicity again), so $L = \lim_n \int f_n$ exists in $[0, \infty]$ with $L \le \int f$.

Substance. Fix a simple $\varphi$ with $0 \le \varphi \le f$ and a discount $c \in (0, 1)$; write $\varphi = \sum_{i=1}^{N} a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}$. The sufficiency sets

\[ E_n \ =\ \{ x \in E : f_n(x) \ge c\,\varphi(x) \} \]

are citizens ($f_n - c\varphi$ measurable), swell with $n$ (the climb), and exhaust $E$: where $\varphi(x) = 0$ membership is immediate; where $\varphi(x) > 0$, $c \varphi(x) < \varphi(x) \le f(x)$, so some $f_n(x)$ has cleared $c \varphi(x)$. Then

\[ \int f_n \ \ge\ \int_{E_n} f_n \ \ge\ c \int_{E_n} \varphi \ =\ c \sum_{i=1}^{N} a_i \, m(A_i \cap E_n) \ \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{}\ c \sum_{i=1}^{N} a_i \, m(A_i) \ =\ c \int \varphi, \]

the arrow by the crankshaft (continuity along swelling chains, Proposition 1.3(i)) applied to each of the finitely many storeys $A_i \cap E_n \uparrow A_i$. Hence $L \ge c \int \varphi$ for every $c < 1$: $L \ge \int \varphi$ (let the discount expire); and taking the supremum over under-staircases, $L \ge \int f$ by ring sixteen’s definition. With the free half, $L = \int f$.

5.3 The One-Way Valve

Theorem 5.5 (Fatou, 1906).

For measurable $f_n \ge 0$:

\[ \int \liminf_n f_n \, dm \ \le\ \liminf_n \int f_n \, dm . \]
Proof.

$g_n = \inf_{k \ge n} f_k$ is measurable (Theorem 4.6), climbs, and $g_n \uparrow \liminf f_n$ by definition. From $g_n \le f_n$: $\int g_n \le \int f_n$, so $\lim \int g_n \le \liminf \int f_n$ (the left limit exists: MCT’s free half). MCT prices the left limit as $\int \liminf f_n$.

Theorem 5.8 (Dominated convergence — Lebesgue).

Let $f_n$ be measurable, $f_n \to f$ a.e., and $\lvert f_n \rvert \le g$ a.e. for every $n$, with $g$ integrable. Then $f$ is integrable and $\int f_n \, dm \to \int f \, dm$.

Proof.

Patch the null exceptions (the amnesty); assume the hypotheses everywhere. $\lvert f \rvert \le g$ in the limit: $f$ integrable with room ($\int \lvert f \rvert \le \int g < \infty$). The sequences $g + f_n \ge 0$ and $g - f_n \ge 0$ are lawful valve inputs. Fatou on the first:

\[ \int g + \int f \ =\ \int (g + f) \ \le\ \liminf \int (g + f_n) \ =\ \int g + \liminf \int f_n , \]

additivity (Corollary 5.3) expanding both ends; cancel $\int g$ — finite, the lid’s integrability spent, first time: $\int f \le \liminf \int f_n$. Fatou on the second, the same bookkeeping (the second expenditure): $\int g - \int f \le \int g - \limsup \int f_n$, i.e. $\limsup \int f_n \le \int f$. The pinch closes: $\lim \int f_n = \int f$.

5.5 What the Lid Buys

Theorem 5.13 (Continuity in a parameter).

Let $f(x, t)$ be measurable in $x$ for each $t$ in an interval, continuous in $t$ for a.e. $x$, and dominated: $\lvert f(x, t) \rvert \le g(x)$, $g$ integrable, for all $t$. Then $F(t) = \int f(x, t) \, dm(x)$ is continuous.

Proof.

Theorem 5.14 (Differentiation under the integral sign).

Let $f(x, t)$ be integrable in $x$ for each $t$ in an open interval, differentiable in $t$ for a.e. $x$, with the derivative dominated: $\lvert \partial_t f(x, t) \rvert \le g(x)$, $g$ integrable, for all $t$ in the interval. Then $F(t) = \int f(x, t)\, dm(x)$ is differentiable, and

\[ F'(t) \ =\ \int \partial_t f(x, t) \, dm(x). \]
Proof.

Fix $t$; take any $h_k \to 0$. The quotients $q_k(x) = \bigl( f(x, t + h_k) - f(x, t) \bigr) / h_k$ converge a.e. to $\partial_t f(x, t)$; the mean-value bridge: for each $x$ and $k$, Volume One’s mean value theorem plants a witness $s$ between $t$ and $t + h_k$ with $q_k(x) = \partial_t f(x, s)$, so $\lvert q_k(x) \rvert \le g(x)$ — the derivative’s lid covers every quotient. Theorem 5.8 passes the limit through the integral; linearity identifies the quotient of $F$ with $\int q_k$. Every sequence $h_k$ agreeing, $F'(t)$ exists and equals the display.

6.4 The Exiles

Theorem 6.11 (Young’s inequality).

Let $p, q \in (1, \infty)$ with $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q = 1$. For $a, b \ge 0$: $ab \le \tfrac{a^p}{p} + \tfrac{b^q}{q}$, with equality iff $a^p = b^q$.

Theorem 6.12 (Hölder’s inequality).

\[ \int \lvert f g \rvert \, dm \ \le\ \Bigl( \int \lvert f \rvert^p \Bigr)^{1/p} \Bigl( \int \lvert g \rvert^q \Bigr)^{1/q} \ =\ \lVert f \rVert_p \, \lVert g \rVert_q . \]

\[ \int \lvert f + g \rvert^p \le \int \lvert f \rvert \, \lvert f + g \rvert^{p-1} + \int \lvert g \rvert \, \lvert f + g \rvert^{p-1} \le \bigl( \lVert f \rVert_p + \lVert g \rVert_p \bigr) \, \bigl\lVert \, \lvert f+g \rvert^{p-1} \bigr\rVert_q \]

by the pointwise triangle then Hölder twice; and $\lVert \lvert f+g \rvert^{p-1} \rVert_q = ( \int \lvert f+g \rvert^p )^{1/q}$ since $(p-1)q = p$. 7.4 The Crime Scene and the Fine Print

Proposition 7.10 (The staircase and its shadows).

On $[0,\infty)^2$ let

\[ f \ =\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \Bigl( \mathbf{1}_{[k, k+1) \times [k, k+1)} \; - \; \mathbf{1}_{[k+1, k+2) \times [k, k+1)} \Bigr). \]

Then both iterated integrals exist and

\[ \int \! \Bigl( \int f \, dm(y) \Bigr) dm(x) \ =\ 1, \qquad \int \! \Bigl( \int f \, dm(x) \Bigr) dm(y) \ =\ 0, \]

while $\iint \lvert f \rvert = \infty$: the integrability check fails, and no license was issued.

Proof.

Columns (fix $x \in [j, j+1)$, integrate $dm(y)$): for $j = 0$ the column meets only the $k{=}0$ positive block: inner integral $1$. For $j \ge 1$: the positive block $k = j$ contributes $+1$ on $y \in [j, j+1)$ and the shadow with $k+1 = j$, i.e. $k = j - 1$, contributes $-1$ on $y \in [j-1, j)$: inner integral $0$. Outer: $\int = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. Rows (fix $y \in [k, k+1)$, integrate $dm(x)$): the storey’s positive block on $x \in [k, k+1)$ and its own shadow on $x \in [k+1, k+2)$: inner integral $0$, every $k \ge 0$ — shadows are cast sideways, so no storey lacks one. Outer: $0$. Absolute mass: each of infinitely many blocks carries $1$: $\iint \lvert f \rvert = \infty$ (Tonelli’s own count, either order). Diagnosis: the two itineraries are two orderings of the conditionally convergent block-series $1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots$-flavoured mass; Volume One’s rearrangement theorem, staged on the quarter-plane. Solved in full as Problem II.2, block by block.

Remark 7.11 (Sigma-finiteness’s witness).

Pair $([0,1], m)$ with $([0,1], \#)$ — counting measure, not sigma-finite — and take $f = \mathbf{1}_{\Delta}$, the diagonal’s indicator. Theorem 7.12 (The layer-cake representation).

Proof.

Lemma 8.1 (Pushforward).

For a random variable $X$ on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, $P_X(B) = P(X^{-1}(B))$ defines a measure on the Borel line with total mass one, and for $g \ge 0$ Borel, $\mathbb{E}\, g(X) = \int g \, dP_X$ (the change-of-variables identity).

Proposition 8.2 (Independence; factored expectations).

Random variables $X, Y$ are independent iff the joint pushforward on the plane is the product of the marginals: $P_{(X,Y)} = P_X \times P_Y$. In that case, for integrable $X, Y$: $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}X \cdot \mathbb{E}Y$.

8.2 Borel–Cantelli

Theorem 8.3 (First lemma: finite risk).

If $\sum_n P(A_n) < \infty$, then $P(A_n \text{ infinitely often}) = 0$: almost surely only finitely many $A_n$ occur.

Proof.

This is Chapter 6’s tail-trap under its proper name, cross-reference stamped (Theorem 6.5’s engine): with $B_K = \bigcup_{n \ge K} A_n$, $P(B_K) \le \sum_{n \ge K} P(A_n) \to 0$ (subadditivity; the convergent tail), and $\{A_n \text{ i.o.}\} = \bigcap_K B_K$ has mass at most every tail: zero. The gambit’s skeleton, redeemed as promised.

□
Theorem 8.4 (Second lemma: independent infinite risk).

If the $A_n$ are independent and $\sum_n P(A_n) = \infty$, then $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 1$.

Proof.

Complements: for $K \le M$, $P\bigl( \bigcap_{n=K}^{M} A_n^{c} \bigr) = \prod_{n=K}^{M} (1 - P(A_n))$ (independence passes to complements: Problem I.5) $\le \prod \exp(-P(A_n)) = \exp\bigl( - \sum_{K}^{M} P(A_n) \bigr) \to 0$ as $M \to \infty$ (divergence; the inequality $1 - t \le e^{-t}$ is the desk’s one-line convexity-free calculus: the difference vanishes at $t=0$ with nonpositive derivative — Volume One’s harvest; the exponential rides Volume One’s $e$-manufacture). So $P(\bigcup_{n \ge K} A_n) = 1$ for every $K$; shrinking intersection over $K$ (mass one throughout, the finite floor trivial) keeps mass one.

Theorem 8.6 (The weak law of large numbers).

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent with a common distribution, $\mathbb{E} X_i = \mu$, $\operatorname{Var} X_i = \sigma^2 < \infty$; let $\bar{X}_n = \tfrac1n \sum_{i \le n} X_i$. Then $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ in probability.

Proof.

$\mathbb{E} \bar{X}_n = \mu$ (linearity, Chapter 5’s discharged credit). Variance: $\operatorname{Var} \bar{X}_n = \tfrac{1}{n^2} \sum_i \operatorname{Var} X_i = \sigma^2 / n$, the cross-terms $\mathbb{E}(X_i - \mu)(X_j - \mu)$ vanishing for $i \ne j$ by factored expectations (Proposition 8.2) — the two-line computation displayed at Problem II.2. Theorem 8.8 (Radon–Nikodym — stated; necessity proved).

Let $\mu, \nu$ be sigma-finite on a common tribe. There exists measurable $w \ge 0$ (the density, $w = \tfrac{d\nu}{d\mu}$, unique up to $\mu$-amnesty) with

\[ \nu(A) \ =\ \int_A w \, d\mu \quad \text{for all citizens } A \]

if and only if $\nu \ll \mu$ (absolute continuity: $\mu(A) = 0 \Rightarrow \nu(A) = 0$). Necessity is one line: an integral over a $\mu$-null set is zero (Chapter 4, manners (iv)). Sufficiency is stated on references (von Neumann’s Hilbert- space route rides the seventeenth ring’s geometry; the classical route rides a supremum-of-candidates exhaustion): Axler 9A, Folland §3.2, Royden ch. 18. Proposition 8.9 (The staircase’s distribution: the singular witness).

Let $c$ be the Cantor function (Chapter 4) read as a cumulative distribution: the measure $\mu_c$ with $\mu_c\bigl( [0, x] \bigr) = c(x)$. Then $\mu_c$ is a mass-one measure carried entirely by the dust ($\mu_c(C) = 1$, though $m(C) = 0$), with no atoms ($\mu_c\{x\} = 0$ for every $x$), and consequently no density with respect to length exists: $\mu_c \not\ll m$. Singular continuous — the standing witness that Theorem 8.8’s clause is real.

Proof.

Existence and countable additivity of $\mu_c$: $c$ is continuous and nondecreasing; the Stieltjes-flavoured construction runs Carathéodory on intervals priced by increments $c(b) - c(a)$ — Chapter 2’s staircase verbatim (Problem II.5 audits the rectangle clauses). Mass on the dust: each removed middle carries increment zero ($c$ constant there), so the complement’s countably many beads carry $\mu_c$-mass zero; additivity leaves everything on $C$. No atoms: $\mu_c\{x\} = c(x) - c(x^{-}) = 0$ by continuity. No density: $m(C) = 0$ with $\mu_c(C) = 1$ violates absolute continuity; necessity (proved above) forbids the density. Definition 8.10 (Conditional expectation).

Let $X$ be integrable on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ and $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ a sub-tribe. The conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the ($P$-a.s. unique) $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, integrable random variable $Z$ with

\[ \int_G Z \, dP \ =\ \int_G X \, dP \quad \text{for every } G \in \mathcal{G}. \]

Existence and uniqueness: apply Theorem 8.8 on $(\Omega, \mathcal{G})$ to $\nu(G) = \int_G X \, dP$ against $P\restriction_{\mathcal{G}}$ ($X \ge 0$ first; parts for signs) — $\nu \ll P$ by manners (iv), and the density is $Z$. (The finale states R–N; conditional expectation inherits the same stated-with-references standing for existence, with everything else below proved outright.)

Proposition 8.11 (The finite case; first properties).

(i) If $\mathcal{G}$ is generated by a finite partition $\{G_1, \dots, G_m\}$ with $P(G_j) > 0$, then on each cell $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \tfrac{1}{P(G_j)} \int_{G_j} X \, dP$: the cell average, the obvious best guess. (ii) Linearity; monotonicity. (iii) Tower rule: for $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$, $\mathbb{E}\bigl[ \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \bigm| \mathcal{H} \bigr] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$; in particular $\mathbb{E}\bigl[ \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \bigr] = \mathbb{E}X$. (iv) Taking out what is known: for bounded $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $Y$: $\mathbb{E}[XY \mid \mathcal{G}] = Y \, \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$.

Proof.

(i): the displayed $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable (constant on cells) and matches integrals on each generator, hence on the generated tribe (finite unions); uniqueness by the defining property. (ii): integrate the defining identity. (iii): both sides are $\mathcal{H}$-measurable and match integrals over every $H \in \mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$ — two applications of the definition. (iv): for indicators $Y = \mathbf{1}_{G'}$ the identity is the definition restricted to $G \cap G'$; staircases and bounded convergence (Chapter 5’s BCT, mass-one ground) lift to bounded $Y$. Worked two-block portfolio: Problem II.3. Theorem 8.12 (The bounded estate recovered — D12, bounded case).

Let $f : [a,b] \to \R$ be differentiable at every point of $[a,b]$ (one-sidedly at the endpoints), with $\lvert f'(x) \rvert \le M$ for all $x$. Then $f'$ is Lebesgue integrable on $[a,b]$ and

\[ \int_{[a,b]} f' \, dm \;=\; f(b) - f(a). \]
Proof.

Step 1: extend. Prolong $f$ to $[a,\, b+1]$ along the tangent line at $b$: set $f(x) := f(b) + f'(b)(x - b)$ for $x \in (b,\, b+1]$. The extension is differentiable at every point of $[a,\, b+1]$ (at $b$ the one-sided derivatives agree at $f'(b)$), its derivative is bounded by $M$ throughout, and $f$ is continuous.

Step 2: quotients under a constant lid. For $n \ge 1$ define on $[a,b]$

\[ q_n(x) := n \Bigl( f\bigl(x + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr) - f(x) \Bigr). \]

Each $q_n$ is continuous, hence measurable; differentiability at each point gives $q_n \to f'$ everywhere on $[a,b]$ (at $b$, exactly: the linear extension makes $q_n(b) = f'(b)$ for every $n$). So $f'$ is a pointwise limit of measurable functions — measurable by limit stability (Theorem 4.6) — and bounded by $M$, hence integrable on finite land (Proposition 4.14). The mean value theorem — Volume One’s Chapter Seven workhorse, proved there in full — supplies the lid: $q_n(x) = f'(\xi)$ for some $\xi \in (x,\, x + \tfrac{1}{n})$, so $\lvert q_n \rvert \le M$: a constant, integrable on $[a,b]$.

Step 3: the licensed swap. Dominated convergence (Theorem 5.8) gives

\[ \int_{[a,b]} f' \, dm \;=\; \lim_{n} \int_{[a,b]} q_n \, dm . \]

Step 4: compute the right-hand side with Volume One’s own instruments. Each $q_n$ is continuous, so its Lebesgue integral is its Riemann integral (Theorem 4.19); we may compute in the old regime. Let $F(x) := \int_a^x f$ (Riemann) on $[a,\, b+1]$; the fundamental theorem’s first part (Volume One, Chapter Eight; $f$ continuous) gives $F' = f$, the chain rule (Volume One, Chapter Seven) gives $\frac{d}{dx} F\bigl(x + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr) = f\bigl(x + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr)$, and the evaluation part then yields

\[ \int_a^b f\bigl(x + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr)\, dx = F\bigl(b + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr) - F\bigl(a + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr), \]

whence, subtracting $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$ and scaling,

\[ \int_{[a,b]} q_n \, dm \;=\; n \int_b^{b + 1/n} f \;-\; n \int_a^{a + 1/n} f . \]

For any $c$, continuity of $f$ at $c$ prices the average:

\[ \Bigl\lvert \, n \int_c^{c + 1/n} f \; - \; f(c) \Bigr\rvert = \Bigl\lvert \, n \int_c^{c + 1/n} \bigl( f - f(c) \bigr) \Bigr\rvert \le \sup_{[c,\, c + 1/n]} \lvert f - f(c) \rvert \longrightarrow 0 . \]

The two averages converge to $f(b)$ and $f(a)$, and the theorem follows.
