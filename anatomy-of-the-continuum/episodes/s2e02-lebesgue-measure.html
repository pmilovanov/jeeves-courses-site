Lebesgue Measure

The Measure of All Things, Season Two, Episode Two

The Typo

Good day, sir.

Tonight's episode turns on a single definition, and I wish to prepare you for it honestly: when you first hear it, you will suspect a misprint. It will sound circular, or backwards, or both; it mentions neither intervals nor lengths nor anything visibly geometric; and it quantifies, extravagantly, over every set of real numbers at once. It is Constantin Carathéodory's criterion of measurability, published in nineteen fourteen, and it is the best typo in mathematics: one strange sentence from which the entire contract of last week — countable additivity and all — unfolds by pure bookkeeping. The men who built this subject circled the right tribe of sets for a decade; Carathéodory wrote one line and the gate swung open.

Last week left the establishment in a precise predicament, and I shall restate it as the evening's terms of reference. We possess the outer measure: a number for every set, the infimum over countable interval covers — ring fifteen of the bell, still warm. We possess four clauses of the contract, kept in full: nought for the empty set, intervals at their schoolroom lengths, indifference to translation, and countable subadditivity — the whole never exceeding the sum of the parts. And we possess a confession, stated on credit: the fifth clause, exact additivity across disjoint pieces, cannot hold for every set; the trial is next week. Tonight's business is therefore the honest retreat's second half. Last week we designed the fortification — the sigma-algebra, walls drawn where the traffic runs. Tonight we must garrison it: name the tribe of measurable sets, prove the tribe is a sigma-algebra, and prove that on this tribe the outer measure keeps the entire contract. That is Carathéodory's theorem, and its proof is the season's longest desk engagement — I say so now, with relish, so that the desk may brace itself.

And when the garrison stands, sir, the payoffs arrive in procession: the dust is measured at last — act three of the oldest running contract in the register; a fattened cousin of the dust arrives to divorce two notions of smallness that Season One allowed you to conflate; and the Borel estate is discovered to be — I choose the word carefully — a suburb of the measurable commonwealth. To work.

The Good Citizen

Here is the definition, sir, verbatim in words. A set E is measurable when, for every set T whatsoever — every set of real numbers, tested without exception — the outer measure of T equals the outer measure of the part of T inside E, plus the outer measure of the part of T outside E.

I shall let that sit a moment, because every first encounter files the same three objections, and each objection, examined, becomes the definition's defence.

Objection the first: where has the geometry gone? No intervals, no lengths — the criterion never mentions what E looks like, only what E does. Precisely so, sir, and deliberately: measurability is not a property of appearance but of conduct. The definition does not ask "is E assembled tastefully from intervals"; it asks "does E cut cleanly." Think of E as a knife and every test set T as a cake: slice T by E — the part within, the part without — and ask whether the two slices account for the whole cake, crumb for crumb, on the outer measure's scale. A measurable set is an honest knife: it cuts every cake without waste.

Objection the second: is the demand not trivially met? Outer measure is subadditive — last week's theorem — so the whole T never exceeds the sum of its two slices; that direction is free, always, for every E and T. The criterion's entire content is the reverse inequality: the slices never total more than the whole — no phantom mass appears at the cut. And that, sir, is exactly the failure mode the catch warned of: there exist sets so ragged that slicing along them manufactures outer measure from nothing — the two slices of some innocent test cake total strictly more than the cake. Such sets are the dishonest knives, and the criterion is their exclusion order, written in the minimum possible ink.

Objection the third: why must every set serve as a test — why not merely intervals, or opens, or something checkable? Because, sir, the criterion is a citizenship examination, and citizenship is tested against the whole commonwealth, not against one's friends. A knife that cuts intervals cleanly but mangles some exotic cake is not an honest knife; and — here is the engineering point — the universal quantifier is what makes the theorem's bookkeeping close. Every proof tonight proceeds by taking an arbitrary test set and splitting it; the strength of the hypothesis is precisely what pays for the strength of the conclusion. One more metaphor, from your own trade, and I shall let the definition stand: E is measurable when the ledger of any audit splits against E without discrepancy — E is a set against which every account balances. Let us check one candidate by hand, sir, before trusting the examination to theorems — the half-line beyond a station a, against the simplest cakes. Test it on an interval: the station splits the interval into two sub-intervals, and their lengths add to the whole — the schoolroom's own arithmetic; the books balance to the penny. Test it on a two-interval cake straddling the station: each piece splits cleanly, the split totals add piecewise, and the assembly inherits the balance. The pattern is visible already: cakes built from intervals cannot embarrass a half-line, because the station's cut respects interval bookkeeping. The general proof — every cake, not merely the tame ones — is the first citizens' business below, and it will consist of exactly this observation filtered through the covering definition: cakes are priced by interval covers, and the station cuts covers cleanly. The customs officer, the honest knife, the balanced audit: three coats, one criterion — and one candidate already looking employable.

First Citizens

Before the theorem, two admissions establish that the examination is passable — that the tribe is inhabited by more than the empty set and its complement.

First: every null set is measurable. Let E have outer measure zero and let T be any test cake. The slice of T inside E sits inside E, so its outer measure is zero — monotonicity, and the null ideal's hereditary law from last week. The slice outside E sits inside T, so its outer measure is at most T's. Slices totalling at most nought plus T's measure: the free direction closes the pinch, and the books balance. Every null set cuts cleanly, sir — a set of no substance cannot manufacture discrepancy — and mark the dividend: the tribe will contain every subset of every null set, a property called completeness that Season One's fine print will be glad of. The gambit cost two lines.

Second, and this is the admission that connects the tribe to geometry: every half-line is measurable — all points beyond a fixed station a. The strategy fits in the ear. Take any test cake T and any thrifty countable cover of it. The station a splits each covering interval into two sub-intervals — the part beyond a, the part before — and lengths add across a clean cut of an interval: that much is schoolroom. The beyond-parts cover T's beyond-slice; the before-parts cover T's before-slice; and per interval, the two sub-lengths total the original exactly — the station cuts each interval cleanly, whatever it does to T. So: take a cover of T within epsilon of T's outer measure — the approximation property, as always; split every interval at the station; the beyond-family prices T's beyond-slice, the before-family prices the before-slice, and the two prices together equal the cover's total, at most T's measure plus epsilon. Slices totalling at most the whole plus every epsilon: at most the whole. Infimum against infimum, the slices total at most the whole; the free direction supplies the reverse; the half-line balances every audit. And with half-lines admitted, sir, the sigma-algebra machinery — once tonight's theorem certifies the tribe is a sigma-algebra — admits everything they generate: intervals are intersections of half-lines; opens are countable strings of intervals, Season One's beads; and the entire Borel estate follows. The geometry enters the commonwealth through one turnstile, and the turnstile is a half-line.

And what, sir, would failure look like? A preview, for contrast, of next week's defendant. A dishonest knife is a set E and some test cake T — the unit interval will serve — whose two slices outprice the whole: the part of T inside E and the part outside, each measured by honest countable covers, totalling strictly more than T itself. No cover conspiracy can be blamed; the covers are doing their best; the set's own boundary is so uniformly ragged that inside and outside interpenetrate at every scale, and separating them wastes measure — the raggedness is the phantom mass. Vitali's construction manufactures exactly this, and the manufacture requires choosing one delegate from each of uncountably many families with no rule for the choosing. Every set you can build without that manoeuvre passes the examination. Hold the contrast: the criterion is not a bureaucratic hurdle; it is the precise boundary of honesty, and everything nameable is on the honest side.

The Theorem of Nineteen Fourteen

Now the theorem, sir — Carathéodory's, the garrison itself. Statement first, in full: the measurable sets form a sigma-algebra; the outer measure, restricted to that sigma-algebra, is countably additive; and the tribe contains every null set. In one sentence: on the tribe of honest knives, the entire contract of Episode One holds.

The proof lives at the desk — the season's longest, as advertised, and I shall not pretend to carry its bookkeeping in the ear. What I shall carry is its architecture, which is a staircase of exactly four steps, each step a reusable manoeuvre.

Step one: the criterion is symmetric. Inside and outside enter the definition on equal footing — swap E for its complement and the two slices merely change names. So the tribe is closed under complement, gratis; the first wall of the sigma-algebra costs nothing at all.

Step two: two honest knives compose. If E and F are measurable, so is their union — and this is the step where the universal test-set quantifier earns its keep. Slice a test cake T by E; slice each slice by F; four pieces, each accounted, by two clean cuts run in sequence — the hypothesis on E applies to the cake, the hypothesis on F applies to each slice, because the hypotheses apply to every cake. Watch one cut in detail, sir, because the manoeuvre repeats all season. Name the four pieces of the cake T: within both E and F; within E only; within F only; within neither. The union's inside is the first three pieces together; its outside is the fourth. Cut T by E — the criterion prices the cake as inside-E plus outside-E, exactly. Cut the inside-E slice by F, and the outside-E slice by F: two more exact splits, four priced pieces. Now reassemble: subadditivity says the three union-side pieces together cost at most their sum — and the sum, reassembled through the same three exact cuts run backward, is exactly the whole cake's price minus the fourth piece. The union's two slices total at most the cake; the free direction totals at least; balanced. The desk performs the bookkeeping with every epsilon shown; the ear retains: honest knives, used in sequence, make an honest compound cut. Finite unions, and with complements finite intersections, are secured; the tribe is at least an algebra.

Step three: disjoint honest knives cut in proportion — the key lemma, sir, and the proof's genuine idea. For finitely many pairwise disjoint measurable sets, and any test cake T: the outer measure of T's intersection with their union equals the sum of the outer measures of T's intersections with each. Not merely for T the whole line — for every test cake, the union's slice decomposes additively. The proof is induction on the count, and the inductive step is one clean cut: take the cake's intersection with all n sets' union, and cut it with the n-th knife. The knife is honest, so the cut is exact; and disjointness makes the two pieces exactly the n-th slice and the first n-minus-one slices' union — the newest piece separates from the accumulated rest with nothing shared and nothing lost. Exactness plus the induction hypothesis, and the proportion extends by one. This lemma is the engine block: additivity, proved first in its test-set-relative form, where the induction has room to turn.

Step four: pass to the limit. Given countably many measurable sets, disjointify — the rings-of-growth manoeuvre from Episode One's engine, at no cost to the union. For each finite stage, step three gives exact proportion; monotonicity lets the finite stages besiege the infinite union from below, while subadditivity caps it from above; and the two besieging armies meet — the supremum of the partial sums is the series, Season One's monotone license, working the same shift it has worked since Episode Four of the first season. For the union's own citizenship, the same siege serves: test any cake against the finite stage — an honest compound knife by step two — and the cut is exact; the cake's outside-the-stage slice only shrinks toward outside-the-union as stages mount, while the inside slices climb by proportion; the limit hands the union an exact cut of every cake. Conclusion, both barrels: the countable union is itself measurable — the tribe is a full sigma-algebra, walls complete — and the outer measure of a disjoint countable union is exactly the sum of the parts. Countable additivity, proved, on the tribe.

Four steps, sir: symmetry, composition, proportion, limit. And an audit of the machinery bill, in the house style, because the bill is the remarkable thing: step one costs nothing; step two costs subadditivity — last week's gambit; step three costs induction — Season One, Episode One's toolkit; step four costs the monotone license — Episode Four of the first season. No compactness, no Baire, no derivative, no new ring of the bell: the season's longest proof is built entirely from the course's oldest and cheapest instruments, arranged with perfect economy. That is Carathéodory's real achievement — not a deep proof but a shallow one, made possible by a definition that carries the depth. The desk writes perhaps four pages; the architecture is those four words, and the words will recur — the same staircase builds product measures in Episode Seven and abstract measures wherever you meet them. Carathéodory's criterion is not a trick for the real line; it is the standard turnstile of the trade, and you have now watched it turn once at full ceremony.

Where the Typo Came From

A word on provenance, sir, because the criterion did not spring from nowhere, and its ancestry explains its shape. Lebesgue's own definition, in the thesis, was the one you would have invented: a two-ledger test, in the image of Darboux and Jordan. Measure the set from outside — the outer measure, last week's instrument. Measure it from inside — and for a bounded set there is a handsome trick: the inside of E is what the outside of its complement leaves behind; within a fixed frame interval, subtract from the frame's length the outer measure of everything in the frame not in E. Inner measure, so defined; and Lebesgue declared a bounded set measurable when inner meets outer — the books balance, cage meets packing, the third appearance of the oldest architecture in this course. It is a perfectly good definition, sir, and the desk proves tonight, as a full-dress problem, that on bounded sets it agrees exactly with Carathéodory's.

Its defect is reach. Unbounded sets have no frame; patching frames together works but creaks; and every theorem proved from the two-ledger form drags the frame's scaffolding through its bookkeeping. Carathéodory's insight, twelve years after the thesis, was that the inner ledger is a disguise: subtracting the complement's outer measure from the frame is secretly a splitting condition — the frame is a test cake, and inner-meets-outer says precisely that E splits the frame without waste. Then generalize ruthlessly: not one frame — every test set at once; no boundedness, no frames, no inner measure at all; one line, all cakes. The typo, sir, is Jordan's two ledgers compressed into a single universally quantified sentence — the season's opening unification (contents are the indicator's ledgers) run one storey higher, and that is why the theorem unfolds from it so smoothly: the definition is the proof strategy, worn as a sentence.

The Scorecard Settled

Let the establishment pause, sir, and stamp the paperwork, for a season-long undertaking closes here.

Define, at last and officially: Lebesgue measure is the outer measure restricted to the measurable tribe. On that tribe, run the scorecard of Episode One down its five lines. Nonnegativity: inherited. The empty set at nought: inherited. Intervals at their schoolroom lengths: inherited — and intervals are citizens, admitted through the half-line turnstile. Translation invariance: inherited, and citizenship itself translates — sliding a measurable set yields a measurable set, since covers and test cakes slide together; the desk files the two lines. And countable additivity — the engine clause, the hinge of the first episode, the entry the scorecard carried as half-paid with a jurisdiction pending: paid in full, on the tribe, by the theorem of nineteen fourteen. The contract of Episode One holds, entire, on the measurable commonwealth. What the catch forbade globally is delivered locally — and the local jurisdiction, we shall see in a moment, is so vast that the word local slanders it.

Every dividend of the engine follows at once: measure is continuous along swelling chains and along shrinking chains with a finite start — Episode One's crankshaft proposition, proved then for exactly this moment; the null ideal sits inside the tribe with every subset of every null set — completeness, banked at the first citizens' admission. And one credential more, which the establishment has been claiming by its use of the definite article all evening: uniqueness. Lebesgue measure is not a solution to Episode One's contract; on the tribe, it is the solution. Suppose a rival assignment kept the contract — intervals at schoolroom lengths, countable additivity, the lot — on the measurable sets. On intervals, rival and incumbent agree by clause; on open sets, agreement extends bead by bead — the structure theorem plus additivity, no discretion anywhere; and on an arbitrary citizen, tonight's approximation manners trap both parties between the same open estimates from above and closed certificates from below, and squeeze them into agreement — the desk conducts the squeeze within finite frames and patches frames countably. There is, sir, exactly one way to measure length honestly. Indeed the desk records a stronger clause, guided as a problem: drop even the interval clause, keep only translation invariance, countable additivity, and finiteness on frames, and the rival is forced to be a constant multiple of Lebesgue — the one degree of freedom being the choice of unit. Honest, shift-blind measurement on the line is Lebesgue measure up to the choice of a yardstick; the finale will meet this rigidity again when probability normalizes the yardstick to one. The contract, it turns out, was not a specification but a description: we did not choose Lebesgue measure; we discovered that nothing else was ever on offer.

And a licensing note the season will spend next week: nothing tonight was one-dimensional but the vocabulary. Run the same construction in the plane with rectangles for intervals — area of a box, countable covers by boxes, the same infimum, the same gambit — and in space with rectangular blocks, and every theorem of the evening repeats verbatim: Carathéodory's criterion is dimension-blind, the staircase climbs unchanged, and area and volume emerge with the full contract exactly as length did. The desk states the transposition and assigns nothing — the proofs are tonight's proofs with the words exchanged — but the license matters: next week's trial exhibits live in three dimensions, and I decline to speak of volume next week on credit when it can be owned outright tonight.

The machine, sir, is assembled and idling. Now we drive it at the oldest question in the register.

Driving the Machine

Numbers, sir — the new machine deserves to be driven around the estate once, aloud, before the ceremonies.

The measure of an open set: the sum of its beads. Every open set is a countable string of disjoint open intervals — Season One's structure theorem — and countable additivity, now a theorem, prices the string as the series of the beads' lengths. One formula, every open set; the desk drills it. And watch the membership pipeline run once at full length, because every citizenship claim for the rest of the course compresses this walk: the dust is closed, so its complement is open; the open complement is a countable string of beads; each bead is an interval — the meeting of two half-lines; each half-line entered through the turnstile; the sigma-algebra walls assemble beads from half-lines, strings from beads, and the complement's citizenship from the string's; and complementation, the criterion's free symmetry, hands the dust its papers. Closed set to citizen in five moves, sir, every move a theorem of the last two evenings; the pipeline never lengthens, whatever the set. The measure of a closed set within a frame: the frame minus the open complement — subtraction now lawful within finite frames, additivity again; and mark the clerical guard-rail, for it is the season's one arithmetic prohibition in action: subtraction is licensed only where the minuend is finite. Within a unit frame, all is well; on the whole line, "the complement of a null set" is priced by additivity run forward — infinite minus nought would be nonsense, but nought plus the complement equalling infinity is a perfectly honest ledger line, read in the lawful direction. The irrationals of the unit interval: the rationals there are null — last week's theorem — and additivity across the two-piece partition prices the irrationals at one, the full length. Say it plainly, sir, for it completes a thought Episode Three of last season began with counting and could not finish: almost every number is irrational, and "almost every" is now a statement about land, not just about census. The algebraic numbers, countable, are likewise null; the transcendentals of the unit interval measure one. Liouville's exotic creatures, sir, are not merely more numerous than the familiar numbers — they own essentially all the property.

And one demonstration of the engine in motion, because it closes a two-season circle. Run the dust's construction and watch the removals: stage one removes a third; the first two stages together, a third plus two ninths; the running total after stage n, one minus two-thirds to the n — a swelling chain of open sets, and continuity from below, the crankshaft proved in Episode One and installed tonight, prices the total removal at the limit: one. The middle thirds, sir, eat the entire interval — every last acre of the unit interval's measure is carried off in the countable procession of removals. Which is, of course, tonight's headline ceremony read from the other side of the ledger: what the removals take, the residue lacks. I merely note that we have now computed it twice, once from each side, and the books agree — as, since the theorem of nineteen fourteen, they must. And one clerical habit to file: the whole line, infinite in measure, is a countable union of unit frames, each finite — the trade says the measure is sigma-finite, and the phrase is fine print now but becomes load-bearing in Episode Seven, where a theorem of Fubini's fails without it. Filed, sir; you will hear it again.

The Old Regime, Honoured

One reconciliation before the manners, sir, owed to the century we deposed last week. Jordan's theory was not wrong; it was provincial — and the new commonwealth absorbs the province intact. Every Jordan-measurable set is Lebesgue measurable, and the two theories price it identically: finite covers are countable covers with tails of empty grace, so outer content dominates outer measure; finite packings certify from inside; and when Jordan's two ledgers agree, Lebesgue's verdict is squeezed between them into the same figure. The desk writes the squeeze in four lines. The surveyor's every field, the draughtsman's every region, carries its old deed unaltered into the new registry — the revolution, sir, expropriated no one; it merely opened the rolls to the rationals, the dust, and everything the nineteenth century had turned away at the door. When Episode Four performs the same absorption for integrals — every Riemann verdict preserved, the new machine agreeing with the old wherever the old could speak — you will recognize tonight's clause as its rehearsal.

The Measure's Manners

Three manners of the new measure, quickly, before the ceremonies — each a theorem at the desk, each a habit you will use daily.

First manner: approximation from outside by open sets. For any measurable set and any tolerance epsilon, there is an open set containing it whose surplus — the open set minus the citizen — measures below epsilon. The proof is the covering definition read with open eyes: a thrifty cover, fattened open at negligible cost, is an open superset of nearly the right measure; additivity converts nearly-right totals into small surpluses. Second manner, the mirror: approximation from inside by closed sets — and, for bounded citizens, by compact ones, Season One's conserved finiteness presenting itself for duty in the new theatre. Third manner, the consequence that will grow into a doctrine: every measurable set is an open set shaved by an epsilon, a closed set padded by an epsilon — measurable sets are nearly open, nearly closed, nearly as tame as you please. File the phrase, sir: nearly. Episode Six of this season belongs to Littlewood, whose three principles all turn on that one adverb, and tonight's approximation theorems are the first principle in embryo. And the principle's proper statement, the one Littlewood will actually quote, goes one step homelier: a citizen of finite measure is nearly a finite union of intervals — for any tolerance, there is a finite union of intervals whose symmetric difference from the set, the land where the two disagree, measures below the tolerance. Not open sets, sir: finitely many honest intervals, the surveyor's own vocabulary, approximating any measurable set of finite extent to any precision. The proof rides tonight's open approximation — an open superset within epsilon is a countable string of beads, and a long enough finite prefix of the string carries all but epsilon of it; the desk stages it as a guided problem, and Episode Six will quote the result by name. They are also, I note for your trade, the working face of the subject: in practice one rarely computes a measure from covers, any more than one integrates from Darboux sums; one traps the set between an open estimate and a compact certificate and lets the epsilon do the clerking — every tail bound and concentration estimate you have ever shipped is a distant grandchild of this section. The desk proves the bounded cases in full and stages the general ones; and one structural jewel falls out en route, worth stating aloud: every measurable set is a G-delta set shaved of a null set — a Borel body wearing a null veil. Remember the jewel; it returns within the quarter hour.

The Dust, Act Three

And now, sir, the register, for the payment three episodes and two seasons in the making. The dust — born in Season One, Episode Three, the middle-thirds residue, uncountable yet nowhere an interval; toured in Episode Five as the paradox lounge; the sealed question of its extent formally opened last week, the number withheld. Tonight the seal comes off. How long is the dust?

The computation, after two seasons of preparation, is four sentences. Recall the construction's bookkeeping: after stage n of the middle-third removals, the dust sits inside a union of two-to-the-n closed intervals, each of length one over three-to-the-n — total length, two-thirds to the power n. Each stage's union is a finite union of intervals, measurable through the turnstile; the dust, sitting inside every stage, has measure at most two-thirds to the n for every n — monotonicity, nothing more. And two-thirds to the n marches to nought — Season One, Episode Four, the geometric decay that has paid so many bills. A nonnegative number below every positive number is zero.

The dust has measure zero, sir. An uncountable set — the diagonal certified it the size of the continuum itself, as many points as the whole line — of total length nought. Read the account's history into the record, as ceremony requires. Born: Season One, Episode Three, the middle-thirds residue, with two contracts and a sealed question attached. Toured: Episode Five, the paradox lounge — closed, perfect, totally disconnected, nowhere dense, every topological strangeness in one address. Serviced: Episode Eight, the indicator exhibit — an uncountable defect set the Riemann integral forgave, cardinality convicted of irrelevance in the vocabulary crisis. Opened: last week, formally, the question of extent, the number withheld. Paid: tonight, in four sentences, by covers the construction itself supplies. No account in the register has run longer or gathered more interest, and none closes more quietly. Let the wonder beat sound here, for this is the evening's designated moment and the register has waited two seasons to strike it. Last week the rationals taught you that a dense set may be null, and the lesson was already sharp: countability, we said, is what the covers chase down. Tonight the dust repeals even that explanation. Nothing about countability was used — the dust is as uncountable as sets come; what the covers chased was the construction's geometry, the relentless two-thirds decay, and cardinality never entered the ledger. And let the intuition be caught in the act, sir, because its error is instructive. Watching the construction, one feels each stage keeps two-thirds of what it had — surely, the feeling runs, something substantial survives so gentle a diet. But the feeling is compounding the wrong way: two-thirds of two-thirds of two-thirds is the geometric decay, and the survivors' land marches to nought even as their census holds at the continuum. What survives is not land but structure — the addresses, the branching, the skeleton of the interval with all its flesh removed. The dust is what remains of the unit interval when everything with width has been carried off: pure architecture, zero acreage. Size as number-of-points and size as extent-of-land are now formally divorced, with the dust as the decree absolute: maximal in the first currency, nought in the second. Episode Three of Season One asked, in its closing teaser, how long the dust could possibly be; Episode Five made it a paradox lounge; last week I withheld the number with malice aforethought. Debt eight, the question of extent: paid, with ceremony, and the answer is the most eloquent zero in mathematics.

Two grace notes on the decree, sir. The dust keeps its address system — Episode Three's dolls assigned every point a ternary itinerary, left-right-left without end, and that addressing was the very proof of uncountability; tonight's verdict says the addresses are infinitely many and the land is nought: a complete census over vanished ground, the bureaucratic triumph of the age. And a forward wink your trade will cash in the finale: when probability arrives and "length one" becomes "certainty," tonight's zero becomes the statement that a point sampled at random from the unit interval lands in the dust with probability zero — almost surely misses it — though the dust stands as populous as the line itself. Almost surely, sir: the phrase is six episodes from its formal christening, and you have tonight computed its first nontrivial instance.

One codicil keeps the account honest. The dust's remaining engagement — the stranger return promised in Season One — is not discharged tonight; it arrives with Episode Four's rebuilt integral, where the dust's indicator and its cousins become the test specimens of the new machine, and a Cantor-built staircase function will do strange and load-bearing work. The character remains on the payroll; only the extent question closes.

The Fat Dust

Now the stranger guest, sir — and I confess this construction is among my favourite objects in the two seasons, precisely because it is engineered to break a lazy intuition the ordinary dust might have left you.

One might leave tonight believing: dust-like sets — closed, no intervals anywhere, all boundary — are null; substance requires containing intervals. Reasonable, tidy, and false. Rebuild the dust with a lighter hand: from the unit interval remove, at stage one, not the middle third but a middle interval of length one quarter; at stage two, from each of the two remaining pieces, a middle interval of length one sixteenth; at stage n, from each of the two-to-the-n-minus-one pieces, a middle interval of length one over four-to-the-n. Two Smallnesses
