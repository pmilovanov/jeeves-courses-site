Continuity, Earned

The Anatomy of the Continuum, Season One, Episode Six

The Pen Lifts

Good day, sir.

Ask anyone what a continuous function is and you will receive the schoolroom's answer, delivered with the confidence of settled law: a curve you can draw without lifting the pen. It is a lovely answer. It is visual, it is memorable, and by the end of this hour it will be in ruins — not vandalized for sport, but demolished by inspection, the way Episode One demolished the infinitesimal: because when the load came on, it could not carry it. Tonight you will meet a function that is continuous at every irrational number and discontinuous at every rational one. I invite you, sir, to attempt to draw that — to even attempt to imagine drawing it. The pen was never the point. Something better is required, and we have spent five episodes buying its parts. Hence tonight's title, sir: not continuity defined — continuity earned.

Here is the hour's architecture. First, the definition — and I have excellent news: it is not a new game but the old one with the move order changed; you have been training for this definition since Episode Four without being told. Then three portraits of the same notion — the game, the sequences, and a third formulation so clean it will follow us out of this course and into every mathematics you ever touch. Then the payoff of last week's surveying: three crown theorems in a single hour, each one powered by a piece of terrain we mapped in Episode Five — compactness buys the first, tearlessness the second, and compactness again the third, which is the theorem that finally cashes the name of Heine. And then, sir, the gallery. The monsters were promised in the first hour of this course; tonight the doors open. One function that is pure static. One function that should not exist and irrefutably does — tonight's wonder, and I have been saving it. And at the very end, an asymmetry so strange that the season cannot pay for it; only Season Two can, and I shall log the debt with ceremony.

Last week the landscape. Tonight the population.

The Game, New Move Order

Let me give you the definition the way it should always be given: as a rematch.

You know the convergence game. The skeptic proposes a tolerance; you answer with a threshold; beyond the threshold, every term complies. That game certified the arrivals of sequences. Tonight's question is different in kind: not where a function's values are going, but whether the function can be trusted — whether small causes produce small effects. Take a function f and a point — call it the anchor, little a. The claim "f is continuous at the anchor" is the claim that inputs near the anchor produce outputs near f of the anchor: no ambush, no jump, no lurch. And the game makes the vague word "near" pay its bills twice over.

The skeptic moves first, as always, and his move is a challenge on the output side: a tolerance, epsilon, any positive amount, insultingly small as ever. He demands that the outputs stay within epsilon of f of the anchor. Your move is a response on the input side: a second tolerance — delta — with the guarantee that whenever an input lies within delta of the anchor, its output lies within epsilon of f of the anchor. Challenge on the outputs; response on the inputs. If you hold a winning strategy — a delta manufactured for every epsilon the skeptic can name — then f is continuous at the anchor. If the skeptic owns even one epsilon that no delta can answer, continuity fails there, and the point is a defect: a jump, a tear, a lurch, certified.

Say the template once, slowly, and let it join the sequence template in permanent memory: for every challenge epsilon, however small, there is a response delta such that every input within delta of the anchor sends its output within epsilon of the value at the anchor. The same grammar as Episode Four — for every, there exists, and the order is the entire content — with the tolerance split into a matched pair, one for each side of the function. Epsilon is the customer's demand; delta is the engineering spec that meets it. And note carefully what may depend on what, because a quantifier is about to move and I want you to feel the floorboards when it does: the response delta may depend on the challenge epsilon — of course — and it may also depend on the anchor itself. A steep neighbourhood needs a stingier delta than a flat one. Hold that thought for a quarter of an hour; it becomes the third crown.

Now play, because the definition only becomes yours in the hands. First match: the identity function — f of x is simply x — at any anchor whatsoever. The skeptic names epsilon. Response: delta equals epsilon. Inputs within delta of the anchor are the outputs within epsilon of the value; the function transmits distances untouched. Match won with the cheapest strategy in the book. Second match: a constant function. The skeptic names his epsilon; we respond with delta equal to one, or one million — any delta at all — since every output sits at distance zero from the anchor's value. The skeptic, as in Episode Four, resigns in some disgust; constants are beneath his talents.

Third match, a straight line — f of x equals three x plus one — at any anchor whatsoever: the output error is exactly three times the input error, so the winning response is delta equals epsilon over three. Steeper transmission, proportionally stingier response; the exchange rate is the whole story. And file the shape — a single response formula serving every anchor identically — because the third crown will make much of it. One housekeeping clause besides, used silently forever after: any response smaller than a winning one also wins — responses are downward closed — so we shrink deltas without apology whenever bookkeeping asks.

Fourth match, and the first with real instruction in it: f of x equals x squared, at an anchor a. The output error is the size of x squared minus a squared, which factors — and here the desk will make you write it — into the input error times the size of x plus a. The input error we control with delta; the co-factor, x plus a, is the menace: it grows with the anchor. The strategy is one you already own from Episode Four's product law — handcuff the co-factor first. Keep delta at most one, so that x stays within one of the anchor, which pins the co-factor beneath twice the size of the anchor plus one; then shrink delta further so that the input error times that pinned bound dies under epsilon. The desk runs the full arithmetic; what I want you to hear tonight is the shape of the answer: the delta that wins depends on epsilon, naturally — and on the anchor. Far from zero, the parabola is steep, and the response must be stingy. The dependence is not a blemish in the bookkeeping, sir. It is a fact about the parabola, and it is the seed of the hour's deepest idea.

Fifth match, and now we prosecute. The step function: zero for negative inputs, one for zero and everything after. At the anchor zero, the schoolroom's pen must lift, and the game convicts in one move. The skeptic names epsilon equal to one half. Any delta whatsoever — however microscopic — admits inputs just below zero, whose outputs sit at zero, a full unit from the value one at the anchor: outside the tolerance, every time. No response exists; continuity fails at zero, certified. Note the eternal shape of prosecution, unchanged from Episode Four: one fixed challenge, victorious over every response. And note what the conviction consisted of — not a picture of a cliff, but an inequality that no delta satisfies. The pen has become a corollary.

Two footnotes seal the definition's grammar. The negation, written as a game, is the prosecution's standing brief: there exists a challenge epsilon such that for every response delta, some input within delta of the anchor sends its output astray beyond epsilon — one unanswerable challenge, the shape you have now used across two seasons of prosecutions. And a curiosity bearing Episode Five's fingerprints: at an isolated point of a function's domain — a hermit with an estate — every function is continuous, vacuously. Choose delta small enough that the only input within delta is the anchor itself, whose output misses its own value by exactly nothing. Continuity, like every regulation beginning with the words for every, is satisfied most easily by those with nothing to regulate.

Three Portraits of the Same Notion

A concept this central deserves more than one formulation, and continuity has three, each earning its keep in a different theatre. The desk proves all the equivalences in full; tonight you get each portrait and the reason it exists.

The first portrait is the game itself: epsilon and delta, challenge and response. It is the definition of record and the one you compute with.

The second portrait speaks Episode Four's native tongue. A function f is continuous at the anchor precisely when it respects arrivals: whenever a sequence of inputs converges to the anchor, the sequence of outputs converges to f of the anchor. Every road into the anchor is mapped to a road into the value. The proof of equivalence is a pleasant hour at the desk — one direction is the two games composed; the other is a contrapositive harvest of the kind Episode Five drilled — but the meaning, sir, deserves saying aloud, because it connects to the season's darkest account. To say f is continuous is to say: f of the limit equals the limit of the f's. Continuity is a swap permit. It is precisely the license to move a function past a limit — the very manoeuvre whose illegal use produced Cauchy's false theorem in Episode One and the rearrangement outrage in Episode Four. The swap account, opened with ceremony two episodes ago, records its first lawful instrument tonight: continuous functions are the functions through which limits pass undamaged. Half the uses of continuity in your professional life, whether you knew it or not, were exactly this permit being stamped. And for daily work, note which portrait convicts fastest: the sequential. To convict the step function, exhibit one disrespected arrival — the inputs minus one over n converge to zero; their outputs, all zero, converge to zero; the value at the anchor is one. Arrival disrespected; case closed in a line, and not a delta handled.

The third portrait is the one in travelling clothes, and it is the reason Episode Five happened. A function — take it defined on the whole line, and the desk annotates the general case honestly — is continuous everywhere precisely when the preimage of every open set is open: whenever you take an open set of outputs and pull it back through the function, gathering every input that lands inside, the gathered set of inputs is itself open. Feel how the quantifiers have vanished into vocabulary. No epsilon, no delta, no sequences — just open sets, pulled backward. It is the same statement: an open target around f of the anchor is the skeptic's challenge; the preimage being open hands every anchor inside it a buffer, and the buffer is the response delta, spatialized. And recall the wink from last week, because it lands squarely here: open sets are the verifiable conditions, the ones a finite-precision instrument can affirm. The third portrait says a continuous function is one along which verifiability pulls back — a certifiable demand on the outputs becomes a certifiable condition on the inputs. That is what continuity is for, in the deepest accounting, and it is why this formulation — not the pen, not even the epsilons — is the one that survives in every mathematical country, including the ones where distance does not exist. The passport, sir, as promised, is valid abroad. Calibrate the pull-back once in each direction. Through the parabola, pull back the open interval from one to four: what returns is the set of inputs whose squares land there — two open intervals, one on each side of zero; an open set, two beads on Episode Five's string, exactly as the theorem requires of a continuous function. Now pull the open interval from one half to three halves back through the step function: what returns is zero and everything to its right — a set closed at zero, not open — and there stands the step function convicted a third time, once per portrait: three instruments, one criminal, the same verdict.

The Algebra of the Continuous

Before the crowns, the working inventory, briskly — because the second portrait makes it nearly free. Sums of continuous functions are continuous; products likewise; quotients wherever the denominator is not zero; and compositions — a continuous function of a continuous function — continuous through and through. The proofs, at the desk, are one line each: feed a converging sequence of inputs into the sequential portrait, and Episode Four's limit laws do every stroke of the work. The algebra of limits becomes the algebra of continuity by inheritance, and this is the first taste of tonight's running theme — Episode Six, you will notice, keeps paying for things with money we already banked.

Consequences, immediately: every polynomial is continuous everywhere — built from the identity and constants by sums and products, all licensed; every rational function is continuous wherever its denominator lives; and chains of these, continuous. The functions of daily commerce are all law-abiding, which is exactly why the schoolroom's pen survived as long as it did: in the shop where everyone works, the pathology never walks in. Add three small tools while the drawer is open: the absolute value is continuous — the reverse triangle inequality says its output error never exceeds its input error, so delta equals epsilon serves everywhere, uniformly in fact; consequently the larger and the smaller of two continuous functions are continuous, assembled at the desk from sums and absolute values by a schoolroom identity; and the assembly line runs deep — a specimen like the absolute value of x squared minus two is certified continuous by composition alone, without a single epsilon handled by hand. Tonight, of course, we are going to the other shop. But crowns first, monsters after.

The First Crown: Extremes Attained

Now the harvest. Three theorems in the next half hour, each among the load-bearing results of all analysis, and each falling to terrain we already hold. I remind you of last week's closing promise: three crowns, powered by compactness, tearlessness, and compactness again. Watch the invoices as we go, because tonight has a peculiarity the register will note: not one new ring of the bell. Every crown runs on completeness already paid for — the axiom, sir, has begun to yield compound interest.

The extreme value theorem: a continuous function on a closed, bounded interval — on any nonempty compact set — attains a greatest value and a least. Not approaches; not approximates arbitrarily well; attains. Some input is actually mapped to the maximum, some to the minimum. This is the theorem your entire profession leans on every time it says "take the best parameters" and expects the best to exist — optimization's birth certificate. And mark why attained beats approached in daily practice: an attained maximum has an address — an actual input one may name, examine, and build upon — where an unattained supremum is a rumour about values. Half the constructions of the next two episodes begin with the words take the point where the maximum occurs, and tonight is the license for that phrase to mean anything at all.

And the proof is two ideas we already own, snapped together. First idea, the master lemma of the hour: the continuous image of a compact set is compact. Push a compact set through a continuous function and compactness survives the journey. Hear the whole proof; it is one breath of Episode Five. Take any sequence of outputs. Each output came from some input in our compact set; the inputs form a sequence in compact territory, so — sequential compactness, the workhorse road — some subsequence of inputs converges to a point of the set. Continuity, in its second portrait, respects arrivals: the corresponding outputs converge to the image of that point, which is an output. Every sequence of outputs has a subsequence converging within the image: the image is sequentially compact, hence compact, the three roads being one road. Second idea: last week's little dividend — a nonempty compact set contains an actual largest member and an actual smallest; the supremum, for once, is staffed. Snap them together: the image of our interval is compact, so it contains its maximum and minimum — and those are attained values of the function. The skeleton I showed you a week early, sir, now dressed in a function, exactly as promised. Weierstrass proved this in his Berlin lectures around eighteen sixty-one, and it is sometimes called his theorem; the man is becoming this season's landlord.

Do not leave the crown without touring the fence, because every hypothesis is load-bearing, and the counterexamples are Episode Five's convictions resurrected one by one. Drop closedness: the identity function on the open interval from zero to one is continuous and bounded, its supremum is one — and no input attains it; the maximum has leaked out through the absent endpoint, the same leak that broke the blanket audit. Drop boundedness: a continuous function on the whole line may climb forever, or may creep toward a ceiling it never touches — the escape to the horizon, wearing a function. And drop continuity itself, keeping the compact stage, and nothing survives either: pierce one over x onto the closed unit interval by assigning it any value you please at zero, and the function is unbounded regardless. Every strut in the crown is load-bearing, and the desk's audit problem has you test each one. Compactness is not one sufficient condition among many, sir; it is the exact patch over the exact two failure modes, and the extreme value theorem is the blanket problem's first dividend paid in the currency of functions: on compact ground, the search terminates, and the best exists. One inventory note, since the theorem speaks of compact sets and not merely intervals: the dust qualifies. A continuous function on the Cantor set attains its extremes — the paradox lounge is, for optimization, a perfectly serviceable stage. Monstrosity and compactness, as last week established, are independent portfolios.

The Second Crown: No Skipping

The intermediate value theorem: if a continuous function on a closed interval is negative at the left end and positive at the right — or the reverse — then somewhere between, it is exactly zero. More generally, a continuous function on an interval attains every value between any two values it attains. No skipping. A continuous journey from below sea level to above it crosses the shoreline.

It sounds, I grant, like the one theorem the pen actually got right — surely a curve from minus one to plus one must cross the axis? But recall Episode Two, sir, and hold the rationals in mind: the function x squared minus two, restricted to rational inputs, is negative at one, positive at two, perfectly continuous as a function of rationals — and it crosses zero nowhere, because the crossing point was stolen from the rationals before the theorem arrived. The intermediate value theorem is not a fact about continuity alone. It is a fact about continuity on a line with no holes, and it is exactly here that last week's tearlessness comes due.

Two proofs tonight, sir — the aristocrat's and the engineer's — and the pairing is instructive enough that the desk carries both.

The aristocrat's proof is one sentence of pure Episode Five: a continuous function cannot tear what is untorn. The continuous image of a connected set is connected — a tear in the image would pull back, through the third portrait, to a tear in the domain — and the connected subsets of the line are exactly the intervals, last week's theorem. So the image of an interval is an interval; and an interval that contains a negative value and a positive value contains zero, by the very definition of interval. No skipping, because no tearing. The theorem is tearlessness wearing a function, precisely as filed last week; the desk runs the pull-back argument in full for functions on the line and annotates the general case honestly.

The engineer's proof, now — and here the register must note a transfer of personnel. The bisection hunt was contracted last week for the extreme values; it reports instead to the intermediate ones, where, historically, it was first employed — the venue changes, the obligation is discharged, and I have amended the character's file accordingly. Suppose the function is negative at the left endpoint, positive at the right. Bisect the interval and inspect the midpoint. If its value is zero, the hunt ends embarrassingly early. Otherwise the sign at the midpoint disagrees with one endpoint or the other — so one of the two halves still carries a sign change across it. Keep that half. Bisect again; keep again; forever. The dolls assemble — left endpoints where the function is at or below zero, right endpoints where it is at or above, widths halving — and the survivor c is cornered as always. Now continuity closes the case, in its sequential portrait: the left endpoints converge to c and their values, all at most zero, converge to f of c — so f of c is at most zero, by Episode Four's order law; the right endpoints converge to c likewise, forcing f of c at least zero. At most zero and at least zero: exactly zero. The hunt has cornered a root.

And I said the engineer's proof, sir, with intent, because this argument is not merely a proof — it is an algorithm, and one your trade runs daily under another name. Bisect, test the sign, keep the honest half: that is binary search, aimed at a root. Each step halves the interval of uncertainty — one bit of the answer per test — and after some fifty-odd steps the root is pinned to roughly the precision of the machine arithmetic you ship on. The desk records the error bound with the widths: the uncertainty after n tests is the starting width over two to the n, and since two to the ten comfortably tops a thousand, some fifty-three halvings pin a unit interval down to roughly the sixteenth decimal place — which is about where machine arithmetic itself gives out. The desk grounds the arithmetic on Chapter One's powers-of-two credit. When Bolzano wrote this argument out in Prague in eighteen seventeen — the very paper Episode One mourned, the rigorous proof no one read — he was, among his other misfortunes, publishing the convergence analysis of a root-finder a century and a half before anyone would pay for one. Tonight the world catches up to Prague again.

Applications, briefly, because the theorem is a workhorse and deserves its harness. Every polynomial of odd degree has a real root — far out to the right the leading term rules and the sign is its; far to the left, the opposite sign; somewhere between, zero — the desk makes "far out" honest with the taming inequality as a problem. The square root of two exists — again: the parabola minus two changes sign between one and two, and the crossing is a number whose square is two; that is, if I count correctly, the third independent construction of that particular number this season, and I intend to keep collecting them like clocks. And every continuous function on a closed interval has, by the two crowns together, a range that is exactly a closed bounded interval — from its attained minimum to its attained maximum, nothing skipped between: the two theorems snap together into one clean sentence, and the desk files it as a corollary with your name on the proof. And one crown jewel of a corollary, famous enough to deserve its own breath: every continuous function from the closed unit interval to itself holds some point fixed — some input is its own output. The proof is the crossing theorem in a costume change. Consider f of x minus x. At zero it is at least zero, since f maps into the interval and cannot dip below it; at one it is at most zero, by the mirror clause; if either endpoint gives exactly zero we are done already, and otherwise the signs differ and the crossing theorem plants a root between — an input where f of x equals x. The desk files the two-line paperwork. The theorem's higher-dimensional cousins — the ones that let one say a stirred cup of tea, confined to its cup, holds some point where it began — run half of mathematical economics; ours tonight is the one-dimensional ancestor, and it comes free with the crown.

The Third Crown: Uniformity, or the Quantifier That Moves

Now, sir, the crown I have been preparing you for since the parabola match — the most underrated concept in this course, and I choose the words deliberately. Underrated because it looks like a technicality; it is instead the hinge on which Episodes Seven and Eight, and a good half of Season Two, will turn.

Return to the parabola. Continuity at each anchor: granted, proven, filed. But the delta we manufactured depended on the anchor — steeper ground, stingier response. Now let the skeptic ask a nastier question. Never mind each anchor separately, he says: I name one challenge, epsilon, for the whole territory at once. Can you issue a single delta — one response, printed once — that answers my challenge at every anchor simultaneously? Any two inputs within delta of each other, anywhere in the domain, outputs within epsilon of each other. A uniform, sir, rather than a wardrobe of tailored suits.

That property is called uniform continuity, and mark with great care what has changed, because it is nothing except the order in which the players move — and everything hangs on it. Pointwise continuity: for every anchor, and for every epsilon, there exists a delta — the anchor is named before the response, so the response may be tailored to it. Uniform continuity: for every epsilon, there exists a delta such that for every anchor — the response is issued before the anchor is named, so it must serve them all. The quantifier governing the anchor has marched from the front of the sentence to the back, past the delta, and that single transposition is the entire distinction. You have seen this movie before: it is Episode Four's lesson that the order of quantifiers is the theorem, now with three players on the board. The full symbolic display of both statements, side by side with the moved quantifier circled in effect, lives on paper, where such contrasts belong; the ear carries the game: in the pointwise game the skeptic names the anchor first; in the uniform game he names it last, after your delta is already printed and distributed.

Feel the difference on the two standing counterexamples, both destined for the desk. The parabola, on the whole line: continuous at every anchor, uniformly continuous nowhere near it — for the skeptic, given your printed delta, simply walks rightward to steeper and steeper ground, where a delta-step in the input swings the output by roughly twice the anchor times delta, eventually dwarfing any epsilon you were defending. The tailored deltas shrink as the anchor grows; no single uniform fits the whole line. Or one over x, on the open interval from zero to one: continuous at every anchor, but near the cliff at zero the function moves violently across tiny input distances, and again no printed delta survives the skeptic's walk toward the edge. Two failure modes, sir, and hear the echo: unbounded steepness escaping to the horizon; violence concentrating at an absent endpoint. The escape and the leak. You know what patches those. For the positive contrast before the patch arrives: straight lines are uniformly continuous on the entire line — slope three took delta equals epsilon over three at every anchor identically, one response printed once and served to all — and the moral generalizes: wherever steepness is capped, a uniform response exists, the cap fixing the exchange rate between input and output errors. Capped steepness is a theme with a future, sir; Episode Seven arms it properly, with the mean value theorem as the capping instrument.

Heine's theorem — and here, as promised last week, the name on the covering theorem finally cashes out, for this is what Eduard Heine actually proved in eighteen seventy-two, the covering idea working implicitly in his margins: on a compact set, continuity is automatically uniform. Every continuous function on a closed bounded interval is uniformly continuous — the tailored wardrobe collapses into a single uniform, free of charge, whenever the ground is compact. Tradition appends Cantor's name to the theorem — adjacency in that extraordinary vintage year being what it is — and the double-barrelled Heine–Cantor is what your references will say.

The proof, sir, is the single most direct deployment of compactness in the entire season — the blanket problem's promise kept to the letter, and I shall give you all of it. The skeptic names his epsilon, once, for everywhere. Every anchor, by pointwise continuity, owns a tailored response: a private delta-neighbourhood on which the function stays within half of epsilon of its value at that anchor — the halving is the one piece of cunning, and you will see it earn its fee in a moment. These private neighbourhoods — shrunk, for bookkeeping the desk explains, to half their radius — are open blankets, and they cover the territory. The territory is compact: the finite audit convenes, and finitely many blankets suffice. Take delta to be the smallest of the finitely many surviving half-radii — a minimum of finitely many positive numbers, positive, and there, sir, in that one clause, is the entire theorem; over infinitely many blankets the minimum could drain to nothing, and does, on the parabola's endless line. Now take any two inputs within delta of each other. The first lies inside one of the surviving half-blankets — say the one centred at a certain anchor — at distance under half that anchor's radius; the second is within delta of the first, and delta is at most half of every surviving radius; so, by the triangle inequality, the second sits within the anchor's full radius as well. Both inputs inside one anchor's full private neighbourhood: both outputs within half epsilon of that anchor's value, hence within epsilon of each other. And there the halving earns its fee — without it, two nearby inputs could straddle two different blankets and compare their outputs to two different anchors, and the audit would leak at the seams. One delta, printed once, serving every anchor: uniform continuity, delivered by the finite audit exactly as Episode Five's operating manual said it would be — infinitely many local certificates, one global guarantee, the conversion performed by compactness and by nothing else. The desk also records the rival proof, by contradiction and the bisection-hunt's sequential cousin, as a guided problem; connoisseurs keep both.

Why does uniformity matter so much that I have spent a fifth of the hour on it? A forward glance, redeemable later. Next episode, when we ask whether a limit of well-behaved functions is well-behaved, the answer will turn on precisely such a quantifier transposition — same players, same move, catastrophic stakes; you now have the grammar in hand before the crisis arrives. And in Episode Eight, when the integral is built, it is uniform continuity — not the pointwise kind — that will make the approximating sums of a continuous function settle down and behave. Pointwise continuity makes local promises with a local budget; uniform continuity makes local promises with a global budget; and theorems, like institutions, run on global budgets. One wink toward your trade besides, redeemable in two episodes: when one tabulates a function on a grid — evaluates at mesh points and trusts the gaps — the trust is precisely a uniform-continuity certificate: one mesh size, chosen once, bounding the error everywhere in the table. Pointwise continuity licenses no table at all; it prices every gap separately. Episode Eight's integral is, at bottom, one enormous table, and it will present exactly this certificate at the door. And one more dividend, small and load-bearing: a uniformly continuous function transports huddles to huddles — feed it a Cauchy sequence and the outputs huddle too, the printed delta converting the inputs' closeness at a fixed rate. Pointwise continuity does no such thing: one over x is continuous at every point of the open unit interval, yet it takes the huddling reciprocals — pressed ever tighter — to one, two, three, marching off entirely. Continuity respects arrivals; uniformity respects even the arrivals that have not yet landed. The desk files the three-line proof, and Episode Eight will quietly spend it.

Three crowns, sir. Compactness, tearlessness, compactness. Last week's terrain, this week's theorems, and not a single new ring of the bell — the inheritance simply compounding. Now, as promised: the gallery.

The Monster Gallery Opens: Pure Static

Episode One's roadmap promised monsters, and one of them has been waiting in the wings, unnamed, for five episodes. Tonight, sir, the foreshadow steps onstage, and the register stamps a birth: the first great monster of the course.

The definition is two clauses of perfect innocence. At every rational input, the value one. At every irrational input, the value zero. That is all — the characteristic function of the rationals, the books call it; I shall call it what it is, and the name is hereby entered in the character log: the static.

It was proposed by Dirichlet — our recurring gentleman of the Fourier wars — in eighteen twenty-nine, inside the very memoir on Fourier convergence that Episode One told you about, and he proposed it for a pointed reason: as a function beyond the reach of his era's notion of the integral. Born in a footnote of the Fourier wars, sir, with an appointment already booked — and the appointment it keeps in Episode Eight is exactly the one Dirichlet booked for it. Few characters enter a drama holding their own third-act contract.

Now the audit, and it is mercifully brief: the static is continuous nowhere. Not at a single point of the line. The proof fits in one breath, and it is Episode Two paying a dividend. Take any anchor whatsoever. Both fences run through every neighbourhood of it: the rationals are dense, and the irrationals are dense — however slim the buffer, it contains inputs of value one and inputs of value zero, both. So the skeptic names his challenge once, for the entire line: epsilon equals one half. Whatever the anchor's own value — one if rational, zero if irrational — every delta, however microscopic, admits an input of the opposite persuasion, whose output sits at distance one from the anchor's value: twice the tolerance, every time, everywhere. One challenge, unanswerable at every point of the continuum. Continuity: nowhere. Certified.

And observe, in the sequential portrait, just how total the lawlessness is: approach any anchor along rationals and the outputs sit at one; approach along irrationals and they sit at zero; the function respects no arrival whatsoever — every limit that can be disrespected, is. This is why I insist on the name. The static is not a curve that misbehaves; it is not a curve at all. Its graph is two dense sheets of points, at heights zero and one, interleaved beyond any resolution — no pen, no plotter, no zoom will ever render it, because at every magnification both sheets persist in full. Noise with a domain, sir. The pen-drawing definition of continuity did not merely fail to anticipate this object; it lacked the vocabulary to deny it. The game convicts it in a sentence.

Two small properties for the drawer: restrict the static to any interval, however microscopic, and you hold the same beast in miniature — both fences run everywhere, so no local repair exists, no window of good behaviour anywhere on the line; and one minus the static is the characteristic function of the irrationals — the gallery's photographic negative, convicted by the identical argument. The desk drills both.

Act two, Episode Eight: the static walks into the Riemann integral and defeats it utterly — Dirichlet's booking honoured. Act three, Season Two, Episode Four: the static is tamed, in one line, by a better integral. Remember that schedule, sir; it is the spine of the whole course's second half.

Thomae, or the Impossible Function

And now the wonder, which I have been saving all evening. The static, for all its lawlessness, is at least uniformly lawless — discontinuous everywhere, a democracy of failure. The function I present now is stranger by an order of magnitude, and I shall state its property before defining it, so that you may enjoy the ten minutes of impossibility in full: there exists a function that is continuous at every irrational number and discontinuous at every rational number. Continuous on one dense set; discontinuous on another dense set; the two interleaved down to every scale. Sit with that, sir. Between any two points of continuity, infinitely many failures; between any two failures, infinitely many points of perfect continuity. The pen does not lift here — the pen resigns.

The definition, delivered slowly, because every clause matters. At every irrational input: the value zero. At a rational input, written in lowest terms as p over q — the fraction fully reduced, q positive — the value is one over q. So at one half, the value is one half. At one third and at two thirds, the value is one third. At three sevenths, one seventh. At every integer, one — an integer is n over one in lowest terms. The trade calls it the popcorn function, or the ruler function, for reasons a desk sketch makes clear; tonight I shall speak instead of loudness. Picture every rational as emitting a chirp: the loudness is one over the denominator. Crude fractions — halves, thirds — chirp loudly. Refined fractions, with denominators in the thousands, barely whisper. Irrationals are silent. Thomae's function is the loudness map of the rationals against the silence of everything else. Two clauses of housekeeping before the audit. The reduction to lowest terms is load-bearing, not pedantry: two quarters and one half are the same rational number, and a definition must issue it one value — so the fraction is reduced before the denominator is read, and one half chirps at one half, full stop. As for the trade's other nickname: plot the loudnesses across the unit interval and something like a ruler's engraving appears — the half's mark tallest, the thirds and quarters shorter, refinements trailing off toward the limit of the engraver's art. The ruler function; the desk sketches it.

The easy half of the audit first: discontinuity at every rational. At the anchor p over q, the function's value is one over q — a definite, positive loudness. But the silent crowd is dense: every neighbourhood of the anchor, however slim, contains irrational inputs, output zero, at distance one over q from the anchor's value. The skeptic names half of one over q; no delta answers; convicted. Every rational anchor, the same conviction — sequentially, if you prefer, in one line: silent inputs marching on the anchor, outputs frozen at zero, target one over q; arrival disrespected. So far, mere bookkeeping.

Now the astonishment: continuity at every irrational — and the entire proof hangs on one observation, which is the wonder's engine. Loud rationals are rare. Say the skeptic, at some silent anchor a, names his challenge epsilon. Call a rational loud if its chirp reaches the challenge — if its denominator is at most one over epsilon, so its value is at least epsilon — and quiet otherwise. The observation: within any bounded window around the anchor — take the window of radius one — the loud rationals are finitely many. Count them by denominator, as Episode Three taught you to count: for each denominator q up to the loudness threshold, the fractions with that denominator are spaced one over q apart — a picket fence of definite coarseness — so only finitely many posts stand inside the window; and there are only finitely many denominators below the threshold to begin with. Finitely many fences, finitely many posts each: finitely many loud chirpers near the anchor, full stop.

And the anchor is irrational — so it is not one of them. A finite set of points that excludes the anchor must keep its distance: the nearest loud rational stands some definite positive distance away, call it m. There is the response, sir, gift-wrapped: let delta be the smaller of one and m. Within delta of the anchor, there are no loud rationals at all — they are all at least m away. Every rational inside the buffer is quiet: denominator above the threshold, chirp below epsilon. Every irrational inside is silent. Every output within the buffer lies within epsilon of zero — which is precisely the function's value at the anchor. Challenge answered. And nothing about this epsilon was special: for a stingier challenge, the loud set grows but stays finite, the nearest loud chirper stands off at some smaller positive distance, and the recipe reprints. A winning strategy at the anchor; continuity certified. At every irrational anchor, the same strategy verbatim.

Let the reveal land, because this is the one open-throated passage of the evening, and it has been budgeted since the season began. For ten minutes this function was impossible: continuity dense, discontinuity dense, interleaved — no picture supports it, no pen survives it. And then one counting argument, and it is not merely possible but inevitable: the loud values thin out, so near any silent point the function fades to silence in every direction at once, and continuity is nothing more than that fading, certified against tolerances. The error of intuition, sir, exposed in full: it imagined continuity as a property of curves — of ink, of motion, of drawing. It is a property of tolerances, and a tolerance can be satisfied by rarity just as honestly as by smoothness. Thomae's function is where the definition we built tonight stops being a translation of the pen and becomes strictly stronger than it — the game plays comfortably, without turning a hair, in country the pen cannot enter at all. If you wanted the single cleanest exhibit for why the nineteenth century rebuilt this subject from inequalities, it is this: the rebuilt definition does not merely reproduce the old intuitions; it adjudicates where they fall silent.

The creature was published by Carl Johannes Thomae in eighteen seventy-five — in a textbook, of all places; the finest monster ever housed in a syllabus. The desk makes the whole argument formal as a guided problem with the solution supplied: the counting lemma at its heart, and a corollary worth framing — any sequence of fractions converging to an irrational must send its denominators to infinity. The rationals can sneak up on an irrational only by becoming ever more refined; crude fractions keep their distance. That single sentence, sir, is Thomae's function in prose. And one forward contrast, filed without explanation: when the two monsters walk into the Riemann integral, the static will defeat it — and Thomae's function, discontinuous on a dense set though it is, will pass. The gallery's two exhibits are about to diverge in a way no vocabulary we currently own can account for, and the accounting of that divergence is very nearly the plot of Season Two. A teaser, redeemable in two episodes.

The Asymmetry

One question remains, and it is the question a mathematician cannot help asking. Thomae's continuity set — the exact collection of points where it is continuous — is the irrationals. Symmetry demands the mirror: produce a function continuous at every rational and discontinuous at every irrational. One monster deserves a twin, surely. The two sets are so alike — both dense, both boundaryless in the walking sense, interleaved forever; what could possibly favour one over the other?

The answer, sir, is one of the strangest facts in this season, and I deliver it plainly: there is no such function. Not merely none among the polite functions, none that anyone has found — none at all, in the entire totality of functions from the line to the line, however monstrous, however artificial. No function whatsoever is continuous exactly on the rationals. The irrationals may serve as a continuity set; the rationals may not. Two dense sets, indistinguishable to the strolling eye — and as habitats for continuity, not interchangeable: one is zoned for it, and the other is, by mathematical law, condemned.

Why can tonight not prove it? Because the proof requires knowing the anatomy of continuity sets in general — which subsets of the line can be the set of continuity points of some function. That anatomy has an exact answer: such sets are precisely the countable intersections of open sets, a species with a name and a family that Season Two christens in its very first evening. And once that anatomy is in hand, the conviction of the rationals is delivered by an instrument you already possess: Monsieur Baire's. The rationals — dense, yet meagre, as we established last week — cannot be a countable intersection of dense open sets without violating the non-exhaustion principle; more than that silhouette I shall not say, because the ceremony belongs to Season Two.

So the register, with tonight's date. Entered as a formal debt: no function is continuous exactly on the rationals — asserted Episode Six, savoured, proof deferred; payable Season Two, Episode One; paying instruments, the anatomy of continuity sets and the theorem of Baire, whose second engagement this hereby becomes. And do notice, sir, what this entry retroactively explains: last week's loading of the instrument was never decorative. Baire now holds two contracts — one falling due next episode, when he pronounces on which functions are typical, and this one, the far shore of the season break. A tool acquired before its uses are visible, and then twice indispensable: that is what good mathematics feels like from the inside, and I permit myself the observation because the register makes it objective.

Two closing notes, to sharpen the asymmetry's edges. First, the inventory: tonight's gallery already exhibits three continuity sets — everything, for the polynomials; nothing, for the static; the irrationals, for Thomae — and the desk's first star adds a fourth, a single point. Which sets can occur at all is precisely the anatomy question, and Season Two answers it completely on its first evening. Second, the culprit: the conviction of the rationals will run through last week's meagreness — their countability supplying merely the stack of films, Baire supplying the verdict. The two smallnesses, teased twice now, are being loaded with more weight each week; they will bear all of it.
