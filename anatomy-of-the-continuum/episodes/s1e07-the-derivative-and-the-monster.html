The Derivative and the Monster

The Anatomy of the Continuum, Season One, Episode Seven

The Pen Shakes

Good day, sir.

In eighteen oh six, André-Marie Ampère — a name your household current answers to daily — published a proof that every function worthy of the name possesses a derivative, apart from a scattering of isolated bad points. The century's textbooks repeated the claim for decades; the schoolroom, in its heart, believes it still. A curve has a slope, surely, give or take a corner here and a kink there — how could it not? The pen that draws the curve moves in some direction at every instant; the direction is the slope; what is there to prove?

Tonight, sir, the answer arrives, and it is the cruelest reveal of the season. We shall define the derivative honestly and pay the last of Zeno's invoices with it — velocity at an instant, made rigorous at last. We shall install the mean value theorem as the workhorse of the differential calculus and collect three consequences your whole mathematical life has run on unproven. Monsieur Darboux will show you that derivatives are stranger objects than anyone advertises. Then, precisely when the construction demands it and not a moment sooner, a new mode of convergence — and with it the first scheduled instalment on the swap account, paid in full over the corpse of Cauchy's most famous mistake. And then we build the monster: a function continuous at every point of the line and differentiable at not one — saw-teeth upon saw-teeth at every scale — the object that made Hermite recoil in print, in words I have verified against the original correspondence and shall quote with the promised relish. And at the very end, Monsieur Baire's instrument, loaded two episodes ago, fires its first shot, and the schoolroom's world turns upside down.

Last week the pen lifted. Tonight it shakes.

Velocity at an Instant, or Zeno's Last Invoice

Begin where the course began: with Zeno, and the one paradox still unpaid. The Arrow, from Episode One. At any single instant, the arrow in flight occupies a region exactly its own length — it fills its place, and to fill one's place is to be at rest. In an instant, nothing moves; no time passes in which to do the moving. Yet the flight is nothing but instants. Where, Zeno asks, does the motion live?

Episode Four paid Achilles with the geometric series. The Arrow is subtler, because the Arrow is not about adding up a journey — it is about what motion could possibly mean at a single point of time. And Zeno's mockery has a genuine target: if you try to define velocity at an instant as distance covered in the instant divided by the duration of the instant, you get zero over zero, which is not a number but an incantation. On that arithmetic, Zeno wins. The nineteenth century's answer — the answer we can at last afford, with six episodes of machinery in the bank — is that velocity at an instant was never a fact about the instant alone. It is a fact about every neighbourhood of the instant: a limit.

One small dialect note first, because we need a phrase Episode Six did not quite define: the limit of a function at a point. It is the same game as ever, with one witness excused. Say the inputs approach an anchor. The claim that f of x approaches a candidate value L means: for every challenge epsilon, there is a response delta such that every input within delta of the anchor — the anchor itself excused from testimony — sends its output within epsilon of L. The excusal is the only novelty: continuity asked that the function's value at the anchor agree with its neighbours; a limit asks only what the neighbours are converging on, whether or not the anchor's own value plays along, or even exists. One condition, inherited from Episode Five's vocabulary: the anchor must be a limit point of the domain — a point with company. The hermits, having no neighbours, give no testimony, and no limit can be taken at them. The desk formalizes the definition, proves the sequential bridge — the limit of a function can be audited along arriving sequences, exactly as continuity could — and inherits the entire algebra of Episode Four in one stroke.

Now the definition the century fought for. Take a function f and an anchor a. Form the difference quotient: f of x minus f of a, all over x minus a. Geometrically it is the slope of the secant — the straight chord through the curve's points above x and above a. Physically, if f is position and the inputs are times, it is the average velocity over the window from a to x. The derivative of f at a — written f prime of a — is the limit of the difference quotient as x approaches a, provided that limit exists. The chord's far end slides toward the anchor; the secant tilts; if the tilting settles, the settled slope is the derivative, and the line through the anchor with that slope is the tangent.

And there, sir, is the Arrow's invoice, paid in full. Velocity at the instant a is not distance-in-the-instant over duration-of-the-instant — Zeno was quite right that this is nonsense, and twenty-four centuries of hand-waving deserved his contempt. It is the limit of average velocities over shrinking windows around the instant. The arrow does not move in the instant; it moves through it, and its velocity at the instant is a statement about the totality of nearby windows — a for-every-epsilon, there-exists-a-delta statement, the same grammar that has run this course since Episode Four. The instant holds a velocity the way Episode Two's cut holds a number: not as a substance located there, but as the certified limit of everything happening around it. I note it in the register with some satisfaction: debt two-b, the Arrow, issued in Episode One — paid tonight. Zeno's account, opened in the first hour of this course, closes entirely. He was owed two honest answers, and honest answers, uniquely, discharge him.

Now let us hold the new instrument and compute, because the definition only becomes yours in the hands. The constant function: the difference quotient is zero over something nonzero — identically zero — so the derivative is zero at every anchor. Stillness has velocity zero: reassuring. The identity function: the quotient is x minus a over x minus a, identically one; slope one everywhere, as the schoolroom always drew it. Now f of x equals x squared, at an anchor a: the quotient is x squared minus a squared over x minus a — and the numerator factors, as Episode Six drilled into us, into x minus a times x plus a. The menacing co-factor of last week's handcuff match returns, sir, this time as a gift: the division cancels, the quotient is simply x plus a, and its limit as x approaches a is two a. The derivative of the parabola at any anchor is twice the anchor — steeper as you walk out, exactly the steepening that broke uniform continuity last week, now measured precisely rather than merely feared. And the tangent it licenses is a genuinely better friend than other lines through the same point: subtract the tangent at an anchor from the parabola and what remains is the square of the displacement — the error dies at second order, faster than the displacement itself, which no other line achieves. The desk has you verify that touching order; it is what the word tangent has meant all along without saying so.

And the first refusal. The absolute value, at the anchor zero. From the right, every difference quotient is one; from the left, every difference quotient is minus one. Approach along positive inputs and the quotients arrive at one; approach along negative inputs, at minus one; the sequential portrait of the limit convicts in a line — two arrivals, two different verdicts, no limit. The absolute value is continuous at zero and has no derivative there. The corner is real: the secants do not settle; they snap between two allegiances forever. The corner does, note, own two half-slopes — a right derivative of one and a left derivative of minus one — and the desk names the one-sided notions properly; differentiability is precisely their existence and agreement, and the corner is the case of a disputed election.

The One-Way Street

So differentiability and continuity are different citizenships, and the traffic between them runs one way. Every function differentiable at a point is continuous there — hear the whole proof, it is one breath. The difference f of x minus f of a is the difference quotient multiplied back by x minus a. As x approaches the anchor, the quotient approaches f prime of a — a definite, finite number — and the factor x minus a approaches zero; by the product law of Episode Four, the difference approaches zero. Outputs converge to the anchor's value: continuity, certified.

And pocket, while the proof is warm, the deepest reading of the definition — the one your profession lives on, sir. To say f has derivative f prime of a at the anchor is to say the linear approximation through the anchor with that slope has an error that dies faster than the displacement: shrink the window and the function becomes exchangeable for a straight line, with a certified vanishing error rate. Differentiable means locally linear, in the honest, quantified sense; the desk files the remark formally. Every gradient your trade has ever followed is exactly this exchange, performed at scale and on trust — tonight the trust acquires paperwork.

The converse died thirty seconds ago at the corner. The absolute value is continuous everywhere and undifferentiable at zero. One corner, sir — a scratch. And here is the belief I want you to hold up to the light, because tonight is its execution: the century's belief, Ampère's belief, the schoolroom's belief, is that the scratches are always like this — isolated, scattered, a few bad points on an otherwise smooth curve. Continuity, on this view, is differentiability with a short list of exceptions. Every picture you have ever drawn agrees. Hold the belief. It has perhaps forty minutes to live.

Housekeeping before the theorems, briskly, because the sequential portrait makes it nearly free again: derivatives respect the algebra. Sums differentiate term by term. Products obey the product rule — the derivative of a product is the first times the derivative of the second, plus the second times the derivative of the first, and the proof is one add-and-subtract of a mixed term, the same modest trick that proved Episode Four's product law; the desk runs it in three lines. Quotients obey their rule wherever the denominator lives; and compositions obey the chain rule — the derivative of f after g is the derivative of f, taken at g of the anchor, times the derivative of g at the anchor: rates multiply through a relay. The chain rule's honest proof has a genuine subtlety — a division that can be illegal infinitely often — and the desk gives the fussy version its full due as a problem, because the standard hand-wave is the sort of thing this course exists to retire. Consequences: every polynomial is differentiable everywhere, the derivative of x to the n is n times x to the n minus one, and the functions of daily commerce all carry derivatives wherever their denominators permit. Note also what the one-way street already forbids: last week's monsters are ineligible. The static, continuous nowhere, is differentiable nowhere, trivially; Thomae's function, discontinuous at every rational, can carry a derivative at best on the irrationals — and the desk's second star will show that even there it never does. The new floor sits strictly above the old one.

Rolle, or the Level Ground

The theorems now, and they stack like a staircase: a lemma of Fermat's, then Rolle, then the workhorse itself, each standing on the one before, and the whole staircase standing on the first crown of Episode Six.

Fermat's lemma first — the interior extremum principle, and quite possibly the single most-used fact in your profession, sir, though it rarely gets its name said aloud. Suppose a function attains a maximum at a point interior to its domain — a point with room on both sides — and suppose the derivative exists there. Then the derivative is zero. The proof is a two-sided squeeze on the quotients, and it fits in the ear. Look at difference quotients from the right: the function's value at the maximum is the largest on offer, so numerators are at most zero while denominators are positive — every right-hand quotient is at most zero, and by Episode Four's order law, the limit from the right is at most zero. From the left, the denominators flip sign: every left-hand quotient is at least zero, so the limit from the left is at least zero. But the derivative exists: both one-sided limits equal it. At most zero and at least zero — the derivative is zero. Level ground at the summit. The same argument, upside down, serves the minimum. And this, sir, is the entire license behind every optimizer that ever set a gradient to zero: interior extrema of differentiable functions hide at critical points, and nowhere else. Fermat scribbled the idea in the sixteen thirties, before the calculus had a name; the industry built on it has yet to send a card.

Mind the fine print, because it is load-bearing: interior. At an endpoint, the squeeze has only one side, and a maximum may perch there with any slope it likes — the identity on the closed unit interval maxes at one with derivative one, unbothered. The lemma speaks only where there is room on both sides. And mind the direction of the arrow: level ground is necessary at an interior summit, never sufficient — the cube at the origin has derivative zero and extremum nowhere, sailing through its own critical point without pausing. The optimizer's saddle points and plateaus all live in that gap between necessary and sufficient, which is why finding a zero gradient is the beginning of the interrogation rather than the end.

Now Rolle's theorem, sixteen ninety-one — and I permit myself the historical relish that Michel Rolle was, of all things, a vocal skeptic of the infinitesimal calculus; the man distrusted the entire enterprise and furnished it with its foundation stone anyway, which I believe makes him the patron saint of thorough code review. The statement: let f be continuous on the closed interval from a to b, differentiable at every interior point, with equal values at the two ends. Then somewhere strictly between, the derivative is zero. If a journey ends at its starting altitude, some instant of it was level. Mark the asymmetric hypotheses, sir, for they teach the anatomy of stacked proofs: continuity demanded to the very endpoints, differentiability only within. The extreme value theorem needs the closed stage entire; Fermat testifies only at interior addresses; and the hypotheses of a stacked theorem are the union of its floors' invoices — no more, no less.

The proof is Episode Six's first crown plus Fermat's lemma, snapped together, and you may hear all of it. The interval is compact; f is continuous; the extreme value theorem attains both a maximum and a minimum — actual points, actual addresses, exactly the license we bought last week. Case one: both the maximum and the minimum sit at the endpoints. But the endpoint values are equal — so the maximum equals the minimum, the function is constant, and every interior derivative is zero; pick any point you fancy. Case two: at least one extremum sits at an interior point. It has room on both sides; the derivative exists there by hypothesis; Fermat's lemma fires, and the derivative is zero at that address. Done — and notice, sir, that the proof did not merely use the extreme value theorem; it used attainment, the address rather than the rumour. A supremum approached but never reached gives Fermat nothing to squeeze.

The hypothesis tour, briskly. Drop interior differentiability: the absolute value on the interval from minus one to one starts and ends at height one, and no interior point is level — the corner at zero steals the level ground and pockets it. Drop continuity at even one endpoint: pierce the identity at the right end of the unit interval — send one to zero — and the equal-endpoint clause is satisfied while every interior slope is stubbornly one. Every strut carries load; the desk's audit problem makes you lean on each.

The Workhorse

And now tilt the floor. Rolle speaks of journeys ending level with their start; the mean value theorem speaks of every journey whatsoever. Let f be continuous on the closed interval from a to b and differentiable inside. Then somewhere strictly between, the instantaneous slope equals the average slope: f prime of c equals f of b minus f of a, all over b minus a. Somewhere, the tangent runs parallel to the chord.

The proof is one subtraction. The chord — the straight line through the curve's two endpoints — is itself a function; subtract it from f. The difference is continuous on the interval, differentiable inside, and — since f and the chord agree at both ends — takes equal values at the two endpoints, namely zero. Rolle applies: somewhere inside, the difference has derivative zero; at that point, f's slope equals the chord's slope, which is the average. The mean value theorem is Rolle wearing a tilted floor, and the desk records the two-line paperwork.

The speedometer gloss, because it fixes the theorem in memory forever: drive sixty miles in exactly one hour, and at some instant your speedometer read exactly sixty. Not on average — at an instant. The average is the chord; the speedometer is the tangent; the theorem is the subpoena that drags one to match the other, and it is the honest mathematics behind every average-speed camera ever mounted on a motorway. The police, sir, employ Rolle with a tilted floor.

I called this theorem the workhorse, and the claim is now due for collection, because three consequences fall out in a breath each, and between them they underwrite half of everything you have ever computed.

First: a function whose derivative is zero everywhere on an interval is constant on it. Take any two points; the mean value theorem plants a c between them where the derivative equals their average slope; the derivative there is zero; so the average slope is zero; so the two values are equal. Any two points, equal values: constant. Mark the moment, sir — this is the first time in your mathematical life that this fact has been proved to you rather than gestured at, and it is the hinge of the entire integral calculus: two functions with the same derivative differ by a constant, which is the uniqueness clause in every antiderivative you have ever written down, the plus-C of the schoolroom, now a theorem. And observe the tower it stands on, all of it visible at once tonight: the plus-C stands on the mean value theorem, which stands on Rolle, which stands on the extreme value theorem, which stands on compactness, which stands on completeness. One axiom at the bottom, your homework at the top, and every floor in between built this season.

Second: a function whose derivative is positive throughout an interval is strictly increasing on it — same argument, the planted c now reporting a positive average slope between any two points, so the later value exceeds the earlier. The sign of the derivative governs the direction of travel: the fact every curve-sketch in your schooling assumed, certified at last.

Third — and here Episode Six's promissory note comes due: capped steepness. Suppose the derivative exists throughout an interval and its size never exceeds some bound M. Take any two points; the mean value theorem plants its c; the average slope between the points equals a derivative, whose size is at most M; so the outputs differ by at most M times the input separation. A uniform exchange rate between input error and output error — and that, as last week established in the third crown, is precisely a certificate of uniform continuity: the response delta equals epsilon over M, printed once, serving every anchor. Last week I told you capped steepness was a theme with a future and that Episode Seven would arm it properly; the mean value theorem is the arming. Read it also as error control: a derivative bound converts input uncertainty into output uncertainty at a guaranteed exchange rate — the inequality your numerics assume, silently, every time a tolerance is propagated through a computation. A bounded derivative is a global speed limit, and a global speed limit is a uniform continuity certificate, and the register may mark the Episode Six moral cashed in full.

Two more names belong to this family, and I shall name them, motivate them, and exile them to paper in the standing manner. L'Hôpital's rule — the schoolroom's zero-over-zero rescue — is the mean value theorem's most famous retail application, and the desk states and proves an honest form of it. And Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder — polynomial approximation with a certified error bar — is, in a phrase I intend to stand by, the mean value theorem wearing evening dress: the workhorse is the case of degree zero, and the desk dresses it one degree at a time as a guided problem. Both are named tonight so that when you meet them at the desk they arrive as family rather than strangers.

Darboux's Strange Theorem

Before the monster, a stranger question: is a derivative continuous? You differentiate a function; the derivative is itself a function; is it well-behaved? The schoolroom never asks, because the schoolroom's derivatives are polynomials of polynomials, tame all the way down. The honest answer is no. There exist functions differentiable at every point of the line whose derivatives are nonetheless discontinuous. The standard specimen — a parabola-damped oscillation, a curve that wobbles faster and faster as it approaches the origin while the wobbles' heights are crushed quadratically — lives at the desk this week, built on schoolroom trigonometry taken openly on loan; the register records the loan, and it is repaid when this course constructs the trigonometric functions properly. The moral stands without the paperwork: the derivative exists everywhere, including at the crush point, and yet near that point the derivative's values oscillate forever without settling — a derivative that exists and misbehaves.

So derivatives can be discontinuous. Now the astonishment, and it is Darboux's, from eighteen seventy-five — a good year for strange functions, sir; Thomae's vintage. Every derivative, continuous or not, has the intermediate value property. If a derivative takes two values, it takes every value between them. The conclusion of Episode Six's second crown — no skipping — holding without the second crown's hypothesis. Discontinuous, perhaps; but never discontinuous in the way of a jump, never skipping a value on the way past.

The proof strategy fits in the ear, and it is the evening's staircase reused. To find where the derivative equals some intermediate target w, tilt: subtract w times x from the function. The tilted function's derivative is the old derivative minus w — positive at one end of our interval, negative at the other, by the intermediacy of w. A positive derivative at the left end means the tilted function climbs away from that endpoint; a negative derivative at the right end means it climbs backward away from that one; so neither endpoint can be the maximum — and a maximum exists, because the interval is compact and the tilted function, being differentiable, is continuous: the first crown, again. The maximum therefore sits at an interior point; Fermat's lemma fires; the tilted derivative is zero there; the original derivative equals w. The desk runs the full inequality work.

Two dividends, each worth pocketing. First, a concrete exclusion: the step function is nobody's derivative. No function differentiable on an interval containing zero has the step for its derivative — the step jumps from zero to one and takes no value between, and Darboux forbids it. Derivatives form a strange aristocracy: they need not be continuous, but they are barred from the commonest discontinuity there is. Sharper still, and the desk stages it as a problem: if a derivative so much as possesses one-sided limits at a point, those limits must agree with its value there — a derivative cannot jump even discreetly. Its only permitted misbehaviour is the oscillating kind, the specimen's kind: discontinuity by indecision, never by leap. Second, a forward shadow: derivatives can misbehave, and Episode Eight will close the season with a derivative that misbehaves enough to break an integral — the fundamental theorem itself holed below the waterline. File the shadow; it has one episode to wait.

A New Mode of Convergence

Now, sir, the pivot of the evening. Everything so far has been the calculus of one function at a time. The monster we are contracted to build cannot be drawn, cannot be written in a closed formula, cannot be produced by any finite act. It must be assembled — as an infinite sum of functions, a limit of ever-finer stages. And the moment one says limit of functions, a question opens beneath the floor: what does it mean for a sequence of functions to converge?

The obvious answer has a name: pointwise convergence. A sequence of functions f sub n converges pointwise to a function f when, at each anchor separately, the numbers f sub n of x converge to f of x — the old game of Episode Four, played anchor by anchor, each anchor hiring its own threshold. It is the definition anyone would write down first, and it is a trap, and the trap has claimed better analysts than either of us.

Watch it fail. Take f sub n of x equals x to the n, on the closed unit interval. At any anchor strictly below one, the powers collapse to zero — Episode Four proved it. At the anchor one exactly, every power is one. So the pointwise limit exists everywhere: it is the function that is zero across the whole interval and then one at the right endpoint. Every stage of the sequence is a polynomial — continuous, differentiable, the best-behaved citizens we possess. The limit has a jump. Continuity did not survive the passage. Picture the mechanism, because it is the disease itself: each stage sags — near one, x to the n clings to height near one before diving to zero, and as n grows the cliff edge slides ever rightward, steeper and steeper, the sag forever postponed and never abolished. Every stage is fine; the limit inherits the accumulated postponement as an actual jump.

A second specimen for the cabinet, displaying the other failure mode: f sub n of x equals x over n, on the whole line. Pointwise, everything collapses to zero — divide any fixed number by ever-larger n and it dies. But the convergence is uniform only on bounded territory: on the whole line, stage n still towers to arbitrary heights far enough out — the sag here is not a cliff at a seam but a horizon that never empties. The leak and the escape, sir, Episode Five's two convicts, re-offending in the new vocabulary: the powers of x fail at an endpoint's edge, the ramps fail at the horizon. The taxonomy travels.

Now the autopsy I have owed you since Episode Four. In eighteen twenty-one, in the very Cours d'analyse that gave continuity its first serviceable definition, Cauchy stated and proved a theorem: a convergent sum of continuous functions is continuous. You have just watched the counterexample — the theorem, as stated, is false. And the proof's fatal step is the one we convicted in the abstract two episodes ago: it moves a limit past a limit. To check the sum's continuity at an anchor, one must let x approach the anchor and let the stage count n grow — two limiting processes — and Cauchy's argument quietly assumes their order does not matter. It does. The greatest rigourist of his generation, sir, undone by the precise manoeuvre his own rigour would later teach the world to outlaw; the swap account this course opened in Episode Four was opened, in effect, at his graveside. Exhibit A is now on the table, dissected. The desk completes the dissection as a solved problem: the two iterated limits of x to the n at the seam, computed both ways, disagreeing by exactly one.

What went wrong is by now a familiar shape — say it before I do. At each anchor, a threshold exists; the threshold depends on the anchor; near the seam, the thresholds explode; no single threshold serves. You saw this exact disease on Tuesday, sir, in Episode Six's third crown, and the cure is the same surgery: move the quantifier. Uniform convergence: for every challenge epsilon there is one threshold — big N — such that beyond it, every stage sits within epsilon of the limit at every anchor simultaneously. Pointwise: for every anchor, for every epsilon, there is a threshold, tailored. Uniform: for every epsilon, there is a threshold, issued once, serving all anchors at once. The quantifier governing the anchor has marched past the threshold — the third such transposition of this course, and by now you know that the transposition is never bookkeeping; it is always the entire theorem. The symbol-level statements live side by side on paper, per the standing division of labour. The picture to carry instead: uniform convergence means that for each epsilon, the entire graph of every late stage fits inside an epsilon-tube drawn around the limit's graph — the whole curve enters the tube and stays. Pointwise convergence is a handshake at each anchor separately; uniform convergence is the tube. The powers of x on the unit interval never enter any tube of height less than one — the sag near the right endpoint always pokes out — and that, precisely, is the disease's name. On a shortened interval, from zero to nine tenths, the tube swallows the sequence whole: b to the n caps every sag, and the convergence is uniform. Uniformity, note well, is a property of the domain as much as of the sequence — shrink the territory and the disease can vanish.

And now the repaired theorem, and with it the swap account's first scheduled instalment, paid in full and on time. The claim: a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. The proof is the celebrated epsilon-thirds argument — the three-legged stool, among the most quoted arguments in all of analysis — and it fits in the ear whole. Fix an anchor and a challenge epsilon. First leg: by uniformity, choose one stage — call it stage N — whose graph sits within a third of epsilon of the limit's graph everywhere; one N, all anchors, that is the purchase uniformity makes. Second leg: stage N is a single continuous function, so at our anchor it owns a response delta putting its own outputs within a third of epsilon of its value at the anchor. Now audit any input within delta of the anchor, walking the three legs: from the limit at that input, step to stage N at that input — within a third, by uniformity; walk along stage N from the input to the anchor — within a third, by continuity of the stage; step from stage N at the anchor back up to the limit at the anchor — within a third, by uniformity again. Three legs, a third each: the limit's outputs stay within epsilon of the limit's value at the anchor. Continuity, certified. The swap — limit past limit — is hereby licensed under uniform convergence, and the account records instalment one of the debt issued in Episode Four. One caution stamped on the license before it is pocketed: the permit covers continuity, and continuity only. Differentiability does not pass through even a uniform limit — tonight's monster is itself the proof, a uniform limit of piecewise-straight stages, every one of them differentiable at all but finitely many points, converging to a function differentiable at none. Moving a derivative past a limit is a stricter negotiation, with stricter fees, and the desk's notes cite the terms without conducting it. Two remain on the schedule: the limit against the integral, next week; and the crown jewels, in Season Two.

One tool more and the workshop is stocked. We shall sum a series of functions, so we need the series version of the license, and Weierstrass — the season's landlord, sir, collecting rent again — provides it: the M-test, the assembly permit. If each function in the series is dominated in size by a constant — the n-th function everywhere at most M sub n — and the constants' series converges as a series of plain numbers, then the function series converges uniformly. Domination by a summable budget buys the tube. The proof lives on paper, riding the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence, which the desk states and proves alongside it. Named, motivated, exiled; you know the drill, and the permit is exactly what the next section spends.

Building the Monster

The history first, because the monster has a longer pedigree than its famous birthday. Bolzano — poor, unread Bolzano of Prague — constructed a continuous nowhere-differentiable function around eighteen thirty. It went into the drawer with the rest of his estate and stayed unpublished for a full century; Prague's luck, sir, holding steady beyond the grave. Riemann, around eighteen sixty-one, gestured at a candidate in lectures; the paperwork, examined by later generations, failed inspection at certain points — the gesture was prophetic and the example was not. And then eighteen seventy-two — the vintage year, appearing in this course for the fourth time, and tonight at last we drink the bottle everyone remembers: Karl Weierstrass, before the Berlin Academy, presents a function continuous at every real number and differentiable at none, and proves it. The construction circulated in lectures and letters and reached print three years later through a colleague's paper, in eighteen seventy-five — that year again. The century's reaction we shall savour in a moment; the construction itself comes first.

Weierstrass's original is a sum of shrinking cosines — analytically superb, and its full audit is genuinely delicate. The desk therefore builds the monster the modern classroom builds, a cousin with the same soul and friendlier teeth, published by Takagi in nineteen oh one, with a base-ten variant by van der Waerden noted in the margins. Its trade name, sir, I report with unconcealed pleasure: the blancmange — named for the dessert it resembles, a pudding that shivers at every scale. This course hereby adopts it; the register logs the christening.

The recipe. Begin with the sawtooth: s of x, the distance from x to the nearest integer. A ridge of tents — up at slope one from each integer to the next half-integer, down at slope minus one to the following integer; height one half at the peaks; zero at every whole number. One continuous, periodic, entirely innocent zigzag. Now shrink and multiply: at stage n, compress the sawtooth horizontally by a factor of two to the n and vertically by the same factor — teeth two-to-the-n times as frequent, two-to-the-n times as short, and note the crucial invariant: the compressions cancel in the slope, so every stage's teeth climb and fall at exactly slope one in size, at every scale, forever. The blancmange is the sum of all stages: the original sawtooth, plus half-height double-frequency teeth, plus quarter-height quadruple-frequency teeth, and so on down, saw-teeth upon saw-teeth without end. Zoom in anywhere, at any magnification, and fresh teeth persist below the resolution — the graph belongs to the family a later century will christen fractal, and the course now owns two members: Episode Three's dust, and tonight's pudding.

Does the sum even exist? Stage n never exceeds half of two to the minus n; the halving series converges — Episode Four's geometric workhorse; the M-test therefore grants uniform convergence of the whole assembly. And now hear the instalment we paid ten minutes ago already earning interest: a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous — every stage is continuous, the convergence is uniform, therefore the blancmange is continuous at every real number. The monster's continuity certificate is signed by the theorem from a quarter of an hour ago; that, sir, is what introduced-at-need looks like in practice.

Now the terrible half: differentiable nowhere. The full paperwork is the desk's guided centrepiece this week, solved in full; the ear gets the mechanism honestly, and the mechanism is a heartbeat. Fix any point x whatsoever — any point; we prosecute all of them at once. At each scale m, the point x lies inside one of the dyadic intervals of width two to the minus m — the intervals between consecutive multiples of two to the minus m; Episode Three's address grid, back on duty. Compute the slope of the blancmange across that little interval: its rise over its run — call it the straddle quotient at scale m, since the interval straddles our point. Two facts, both verified at the desk in a line each. Fact one: stages finer than m contribute nothing to this quotient — their sawtooth vanishes at every multiple of two to the minus m, both endpoints included, so their rise across the interval is exactly zero. Fact two: each stage coarser than m is, across so short an interval, a single straight tooth-side — a line of slope plus one or minus one, nothing else. So the straddle quotient is a sum of m terms, each plus or minus one: an integer. And when we refine the scale by one — pass from m to m plus one — exactly one new term joins the sum: the quotient changes by exactly one. Odd, even, odd, even: the parity flips at every refinement, forever. An integer sequence that steps by one at every stage converges to nothing — it cannot even become eventually constant.

But suppose — for contradiction, the season's favourite doorway — the blancmange had a derivative at our point x. Here the desk supplies the one missing bridge, the straddle lemma: if a function is differentiable at a point, then its slopes across any sequence of shrinking intervals straddling that point are forced to converge to the derivative — not merely the slopes anchored at the point, but the slopes across it; the proof is a short weighted-average argument, and the lemma is the subpoena that drags our straddle quotients into court. And note why the lemma must speak of straddling intervals at all: our dyadic interval need not have x for an endpoint — the point sits somewhere in its interior — so the definition's own anchored quotients never testify directly. The straddle lemma is the instrument that converts testimony about a point's neighbours into a verdict about the point; without it, the heartbeat would be suggestive and nothing more. Forced to converge; incapable of converging. Contradiction — at x. And x was arbitrary: at every point of the line, the same heartbeat, the same subpoena, the same collapse. The blancmange is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere. Ampère's scattered bad points have become every point there is; the corner of the absolute value, which we met two hours ago as a curiosity, has been multiplied into a function that is corner all the way down. The pen does not lift, sir. It shakes, at every scale at once, and the shaking never bottoms out.

Hermite Recoils

The century's reception of this object deserves its own minute, because mathematics is done by people, and the people were appalled.

Charles Hermite — one of the great analysts of the age, a man of vast accomplishment and, evidently, delicate digestion — wrote to his colleague Stieltjes on the twentieth of May, eighteen ninety-three, a sentence I have verified against their published correspondence and now deliver with the relish promised two weeks ago: I turn away in fright and horror, he wrote, from this lamentable plague of continuous functions that have no derivatives. A plague, sir. The original French — which has rather more shudder in it — is preserved in the chapter notes, where its accents are safe from mispronunciation. (The correspondence with Stieltjes runs to two printed volumes; analysts of that era wrote letters the way ours write preprints, and with markedly better manners.) And Poincaré, no less, grumbled that where new functions had once been invented for practical ends, they were now invented expressly to fault the reasonings of the fathers — and he called such functions monsters, which is, of course, precisely the word this course adopted in its first hour. We have been quoting Poincaré's insult as a job title all season.

Permit the valet an opinion, since the register allows it. One sympathizes with the horror; one declines to share it. The monsters were not invented, whatever Poincaré's grievance — they were discovered, resident all along in the definitions the fathers themselves had drafted, ratified, and built upon. Weierstrass did not vandalize analysis; he audited it, and the audit found what audits find. One does not fault the auditor for the state of the books. And Hermite's epidemiology, I would submit, inverts the truth of the matter: the plague was not the arrival of the monsters. The plague was the century of believing health universal on no evidence but the pen's limitations — and from that plague, the patient was cured in eighteen seventy-two.

Yet grant Hermite this much: if the monsters were rare — freak specimens at the far edge of the zoo — one might reasonably turn away, file them under curiosities, and resume drawing curves. Are they rare? That, sir, is the question Monsieur Baire has been waiting two episodes to answer, and his answer ends the schoolroom for good.

Baire Fires

To ask whether most continuous functions are monsters, the word most must be given honest work to do — and for that we need what Episode Five quietly promised when its passport was stamped: a space whose points are functions, with a notion of distance between them.

The distance is tonight's own invention wearing geometric clothes. Given two continuous functions on the closed unit interval, define their distance to be the largest vertical gap between their graphs. Largest, mark you — attained, not rumoured: the gap between them is itself a continuous function on a compact interval, and Episode Six's first crown staffs its supremum with an actual address. Last week's theorem, sir, certifying this week's yardstick. Two functions are close when their entire graphs run close together — everywhere at once — and convergence in this distance is exactly uniform convergence: the tube, again. The new mode of convergence, it turns out, was secretly a geometry.

Call the space C: the collection of all continuous functions on the unit interval, with that distance. And now attend to something remarkable about Episode Five: it never actually used numbers. Neighbourhoods, open and closed, dense, nowhere dense, meagre, the Baire property — every definition of that hour was phrased in distances and inclusions alone, and every one of them transcribes to C word for word. The vocabulary was written in a portable dialect; tonight we carry it abroad, exactly as the passport clause foretold.

One credential must be checked at the border, and it is the load-bearing one: completeness. Baire's theorem, recall, was a theorem about complete spaces — no gaps, every huddle rewarded. Is C complete? Take a sequence of continuous functions that huddles in the uniform distance — beyond some stage, all pairs of functions sit within epsilon of each other, graphs and all. Then at each single anchor, the values form a huddling sequence of plain numbers — and the completeness of the line, the season's one axiom, hands each anchor a limiting value: a candidate function assembles pointwise. The desk verifies in three lines that the huddle's uniformity transfers to the candidate — the convergence is uniform, not merely pointwise — and then tonight's three-legged stool certifies the candidate continuous. Cauchy sequences in C converge in C. The space is complete — and there, sir, after two silent evenings, is the bell: twelve. Mark where you are standing when it rings. Not on the line at all, but in a building whose points are curves; the completeness of the real numbers has just underwritten the completeness of a space of functions. The axiom travels. I confess I find this ring the most satisfying of the twelve — wages, dividends, and now a branch office abroad.

So Baire's theorem holds in C — the proof from Episode Five transcribes verbatim once completeness is checked; the desk says so and, having checked, means it. A complete space is not a countable stack of films: not exhaustible by countably many nowhere-dense sets. The instrument is loaded in the new building. Now watch where it points.

Sort the functions of C by gentleness. For each grade n, let film number n collect every function that is somewhere gentle at grade n: possessing at least one point where all the nearby difference quotients stay bounded by n — one location, however small its neighbourhood, where the function's steepness is capped at grade n. Two claims, both desk-verified as the guided problem that mirrors Episode Five's Baire centrepiece. First, each film is closed: a uniform limit of functions gentle at grade n is itself gentle at grade n — the bound survives the passage, by an audit of limits. Second — and here is the beautiful stroke — each film is nowhere dense, and the demolition tool is tonight's own sawtooth. Take any function whatsoever, and any tolerance however small. Add to it a sawtooth of tiny height and savage frequency — height well inside the tolerance, teeth steeper than any grade you care to defeat. In the uniform distance the perturbation is invisible: the new function sits within the tolerance-ball of the old. But its steepness now exceeds grade n everywhere — the added teeth outclimb the cap at every point, and gentleness at grade n is abolished. Every ball in C, around every function, contains functions outside the film: nowhere dense, exactly as claimed. The blancmange's own teeth, sir, wielded as a demolition instrument against the smooth.

Assemble. Any function differentiable at even one point is caught by some film — a derivative at a point pins a finite steepness cap there, and some grade n exceeds it. So the functions differentiable anywhere at all — even at a single point — form a subset of a countable union of closed, nowhere-dense films: a meagre set, a stack of films in a complete space. Countably many films because countably many grades — one per counting number — and there, once more, is Episode Three quietly underwriting Episode Seven: the stack is countable because the grades are. Baire pronounces: that stack is not the whole of C — and more, its complement is residual, the generic condition, the typical case. The functions differentiable nowhere — the monsters — are not merely infinite in number. In the exact sense Baire made honest, they are almost everything there is.

Let it land, sir, because this is the hour's wonder and the season has been building it since the first roadmap: the inversion is total. Every function you have ever drawn; every polynomial; every curve in every textbook you have ever opened; every function a pen, a plotter, or a schoolroom can produce — all of them together live inside the meagre stack. Smoothness — differentiability at so much as one point — is the exception, the negligible case, the pathology. The monster is not the freak at the edge of the zoo; the monster is the population, and the smooth curves are the specimens in the jar. The schoolroom did not merely overlook the monsters — it mistook the exception for the rule, and the mistake was structural: the exceptions are the only functions a hand can draw, so the pen samples forever from the negligible and reports it as the world. Ink, sir, is a category-negligible instrument. Two episodes ago I promised that the loaded instrument would invert the schoolroom; here is the inversion, entire: the freak show was the audience all along.

And pocket the method along with the verdict, for it is a dividend in its own right: category proves existence without construction. Had Weierstrass never built a single monster, tonight's census would still certify that monsters exist — in bulk, in the majority — because the tame functions demonstrably cannot fill a complete space. Proof by census, sir: show the alleged everything is a meagre something, and the remainder must be inhabited. The desk's star closes on exactly that remark, and Season Two will use the method again.

Honesty clause, as standing orders require: typical tonight means typical in the sense of category — Baire's sense, the topological sense. Season Two will introduce a rival notion of smallness — measure, the true subject of that season — and the two smallnesses will turn out to be genuinely different judges, capable of ruling opposite ways on the same set; the teasers already filed on that account remain open. Tonight's verdict is category's, and within its jurisdiction it is final.

And the register, with ceremony. Debt four-b: the Weierstrass monster promised in Episode One — built tonight; and the claim that the monsters are typical — proved tonight, in the sense stated. Paid. Monsieur Baire's first engagement, contracted in Episode Five and formalized in Six, is discharged; his second — the asymmetry of the rationals, Season Two, Episode One — stands armed and scheduled. Loaded in Five, aimed in Six, fired in Seven; the season's bookkeeping, I permit myself to observe, is running precisely to schedule.
