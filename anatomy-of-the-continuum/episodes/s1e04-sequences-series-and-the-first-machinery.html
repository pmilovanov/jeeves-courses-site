Sequences, Series, and the First Machinery

The Anatomy of the Continuum, Season One, Episode Four

The Oldest Account on the Books

Good day, sir.

Tonight we pay the oldest account on our books. It was opened in the first hour of this course, by a Greek gentleman of Elea with a taste for embarrassment, and it has accrued interest for some twenty-three centuries: Achilles, the tortoise, and the claim that motion requires the completion of infinitely many tasks. In Episode One I gave you the schoolbook answer — the stages shrink geometrically and their total is finite — and then, you will recall, I confiscated it, on the grounds that it presumes the very concept in dispute. What does an infinite process converge to? What is the destination of a journey with no final step? Until that question has an answer — a real one, an answer made of inequalities rather than gestures — the schoolbook reply is an intention to answer, and Zeno keeps his account.

Tonight the question gets its answer, and I shall be candid about the price. The definition we build in the next quarter hour is, I maintain, the hardest small thing in mathematics: two lines that took humanity from Zeno to Weierstrass to get right, and that every mathematics undergraduate since has been expected to absorb in a fortnight, usually with visible bruising. We shall take our time instead, because this definition is not one topic among tonight's several — it is the load-bearing wall of everything from here to the end of Season Two, and it comes with a mental model, an adversarial game, that will be the grammar of the whole course. Learn the game tonight and every epsilon that follows, for twelve more episodes, is a rematch you already know how to play.

The programme, then. First the definition, earned slowly, with its failed drafts on display — one learns a definition properly by watching the wrong versions die. Then the first machinery: the algebra of arrival, and the squeeze. Then three theorems in rising order of magnificence — the licensed climb, the bisection hunt, and the huddle that arrives without a destination — with our axiom of completeness ringing like a fire alarm throughout; I advise you to keep count. Then infinite sums arraigned at last: Achilles discharged with the paperwork in order, a fourteenth-century Parisian bombshell, and, to close, the season's first true outrage — a theorem about addition, sir, ordinary addition, that I have seen unsettle professionals. Bring the seatbelt I mentioned.

The Fugitive Notion

Begin where the eighteenth century was stuck. Everyone knew what a limit was supposed to be — the value a quantity approaches, tends toward, gets ever nearer. The trouble is that every word in that sentence is a verb of motion, and motion is precisely what Weierstrass taught us to distrust: things flowing, approaching, arriving — cinema, not mathematics. Our task is to say what the sequence one, one half, one third, one quarter, and so on does with respect to zero, using no verbs of travel whatsoever: only numbers, and inequalities that hold or fail. Watch how hard this is by watching two honest attempts die.

First draft: the terms get ever closer to the limit — each term nearer than the last. Plausible, and wrong twice over. Wrong once because closeness can improve forever without ever being achieved: consider the sequence one, one half, one third, and so on, and ask how far its terms sit from minus five. The first term sits at distance six; the next at five and a half; then five and a third — the distances shrink at every single step, forever, ever closer to minus five in the draft's exact sense. Yet no term ever comes within shouting distance of minus five; the sequence is patently not going there. Ever-improving is not the same as arriving, and a definition that lets minus five pose as a destination of one over n is not a definition; it is a vulnerability. And the draft is wrong a second time in the other direction, for a sequence may converge perfectly well while fidgeting — a term slightly farther than its predecessor now and then, the approach untidy — and we have no wish to disqualify honest arrivals for bad posture. Steady improvement is neither necessary nor sufficient. Kill the draft.

Second draft, repaired: never mind step-by-step improvement — the terms get arbitrarily close to the limit; as close as you please, sooner or later. Better; still fatal. Consider the flip-flop: one, zero, one, zero, alternating forever. Its terms get arbitrarily close to one — indeed, exactly one, infinitely often. Also arbitrarily close to zero, infinitely often. The sequence is a bigamist, courting two destinations forever and marrying neither; nobody sane says it converges to anything. The defect is now visible in high relief, and it is the distinction — sir, I planted this in Episode One and promised it would earn its keep — between infinitely often and eventually. The flip-flop is close to one infinitely often: visits, endlessly repeated. Convergence must mean something stronger: close eventually — close, and thereafter remaining close. Not a tourist's visits. Residence.

So the repair dictates itself, and notice that it has two knobs. Closeness must be quantified — how close? — call the demanded closeness the tolerance. And permanence must be quantified — close from when onward? — call the position after which the closeness holds the threshold. A limit is a claim about every tolerance, however miserly, being satisfied beyond some threshold. And the entire content of the definition, the part that took twenty-three centuries, is the order in which those two quantifiers arrive.

The Game

Here is the definition, sir, and then the game that makes it thinkable. A sequence — call its terms a sub n — converges to the limit L when the following holds. For every tolerance epsilon, however small, there is a threshold — big N — such that beyond that threshold, every term of the sequence lies within epsilon of the limit. That is the whole thing. Two lines. Memorize the music of it, because that exact sentence, with the nouns swapped, underwrites every theorem this course will ever prove.

Now the game, which is how the definition is actually used, and how I shall speak of it forevermore. Convergence is an adversarial contest between two players. The first player is the skeptic. The skeptic does not believe your sequence converges to L, and the skeptic moves first: he proposes a tolerance — epsilon, any positive amount, and he will make it insultingly small, one millionth, one trillionth, whatever he judges you cannot meet. Your move is to answer with a threshold: a position in the sequence, big N, together with the guarantee that beyond big N, every term — not most, not the well-behaved ones, every last one — lies within the skeptic's tolerance of L. If you can answer every possible challenge — a winning strategy, a threshold manufactured for each and every epsilon — the sequence converges to L, by definition. If the skeptic possesses even one tolerance you cannot meet, one epsilon such that beyond every candidate threshold some term still strays, the claim is dead. Note the etiquette, for everything lives in it: the skeptic moves first. Epsilon is chosen before big N, and big N may therefore depend on epsilon — a stingier tolerance commanding a later threshold — but never the reverse. For every epsilon, there exists a threshold. Reverse the players and you describe a different and useless statement. The order of the quantifiers is the theorem; I shall say this again in later episodes, at moments when whole theories turn on it. If you doubt that the order carries content, reverse it and watch the meaning collapse: there is a threshold beyond which every term lies within every tolerance of L — within every tolerance means at distance zero — and the sentence now says the sequence eventually equals L, term after term after term: a brutally stronger claim, true of almost nothing we care about. One over n converges to zero; it never equals zero. Same words, swapped quantifiers, different universe. The grammar installed in Episode One was not pedantry, sir. It was load-bearing steel.

And a word of notational housekeeping before the first match, because the letters are treacherous aloud: there are two n's in this business. Little n is the running index, the position counter marching one, two, three, forever. Big N is the threshold, the specific milestone your strategy names. Little n runs; big N stands. I shall always say which is which.

Now play. First match: the sequence one over n — one, one half, one third — against the claim it converges to zero. The skeptic opens with tolerance one thousandth. Our answer: threshold one thousand. Beyond position one thousand, every term is one over something exceeding one thousand, hence smaller than one thousandth: within tolerance, all of them, forever. The skeptic tries one trillionth; we answer one trillion. And the general strategy — the winning strategy, stated once for all epsilons — is this: whatever tolerance epsilon the skeptic names, there is a whole number beyond one over epsilon; that is precisely the Archimedean property, Episode Two's first dividend, reporting for duty; take that whole number as big N, and every later term one over little n is smaller than epsilon. Match won, permanently. Notice what the victory consisted of: not a feeling of approach — a recipe, converting any challenge into a certified answer. Convergence proofs are the manufacture of such recipes; you will build a dozen at the desk, and the first few will feel like operating heavy machinery in gloves, and then abruptly they will not.

Second match, for calibration: the constant sequence — seven, seven, seven, forever — converges to seven. The skeptic names his tolerance; we answer: threshold one. Every term beyond position one — every term full stop — sits at distance zero from seven, within any tolerance ever conceived. The skeptic resigns in some disgust. It is a trivial match, but it certifies that the definition endorses the obvious, which a definition must do before it is trusted with the non-obvious.

Third match, and now the roles invert, which is a move you must own as thoroughly as the game itself: the flip-flop, one, zero, one, zero — and we prosecute. To prove divergence, sir, one plays the skeptic, and one must defeat every candidate limit at once. Here is the unanswerable challenge: tolerance one half. Let anyone propose any limit L whatsoever. The values one and zero sit at distance one from each other, so L cannot lie within one half of both — within half of each, the two would sit within one of each other with room to spare on the wrong side; at least one of the two values, one or zero, sits at distance of at least one half from L. And the sequence visits that offending value infinitely often — beyond any threshold anyone names, another visit is always coming. So no threshold ever works, for any L, against tolerance one half: the flip-flop converges to nothing, certified. Mark the shape of the argument — one fixed tolerance, victorious over all comers — because prosecuting divergence is half of analysis, and it is always this shape. And one may prosecute even an innocent sequence, for the right crime: does one over n converge to one? Tolerance one third. From the third position onward every term is at most one third, hence at distance at least two thirds from one — outside tolerance forever; no threshold survives. The sequence converges, sir; just not there. A limit claim names a specific destination, and every destination stands trial separately.

One last item while the paint is wet: can a sequence converge to two different limits? Intuition howls no; the definition must be made to agree, and it does, prettily. If the sequence resided within every tolerance of two distinct destinations, take the tolerance to be half the gap between them: beyond the two thresholds, every term would have to stand within half a gap of both ends of the gap at once, which no number manages. The desk performs this with full paperwork as the chapter's opening proof — uniqueness of limits — and I commend it to you as the gentlest possible first date with epsilon.

And now step back and admire what has quietly happened, because this is the reassurance beat and you have earned it. The definition contains no motion. Nothing approaches; nothing tends; nothing flows. There is a fixed sequence, a fixed number L, and a family of inequalities that hold or fail — arithmetic, standing perfectly still, exactly as Weierstrass demanded. The feeling of approach turns out to be an implementation detail of human cognition; the mathematics is a contract. Indeed, sir, if I may translate briefly into the dialect of your profession: convergence to L is a service-level agreement. Any tolerance the customer demands shall be met beyond a stated threshold, and the proof of convergence is the standing procedure that turns each demand into a compliance certificate. The eighteenth century had the service; what it lacked, until Bolzano and Cauchy and Weierstrass, was the agreement.

One dialect variant completes the grammar, and I install it now because a fourteenth-century gentleman will need it within the hour. A sequence diverges to infinity when: for every bound the skeptic posts, however high, there is a threshold beyond which every term exceeds the bound. The same game with the tolerance replaced by a bar to clear. This is not convergence to a place called infinity — infinity names no destination, only a manner of departure, and the phrase is now safe precisely because it is defined: every bound, eventually and permanently, cleared.

The Algebra of Arrival

A definition earns its keep by powering theorems, and the first theorems are the ones that let us stop playing the game move by move. The algebra of limits: if a sub n arrives at L, and b sub n arrives at M, then the sum sequence arrives at L plus M; the product at L times M; the quotient at L over M, provided M is not zero. Obvious-sounding, endlessly used, and each one a small lesson in epsilon-craft — so let me give you the strategies aloud, and then do something the course will do many times, openly and without apology: name the theorems, hand you the architecture, and exile the bookkeeping to paper, where it belongs.

The sum law's strategy is the oldest trick in the subject, and you should carry it consciously ever after: split the tolerance. The skeptic demands the sum sit within epsilon of L plus M. Very well: bill each culprit half. There is a threshold beyond which a sub n sits within half of epsilon of L; another beyond which b sub n sits within half of epsilon of M; beyond the larger of the two thresholds, the sum's error is at most the two errors combined — two halves, epsilon, done. Half plus half is the whole of the first year of analysis, sir; I am only partly joking. The product is subtler by exactly one idea: errors multiply against the size of the factors, so before squeezing, one factor must first be handcuffed — and the handcuff is a small lemma with a long career: a convergent sequence is bounded. Arrivals do not wander off to infinity; beyond some threshold everything huddles near L, and the finitely many stragglers before it can be rounded up by hand. The desk pins this in four lines and then runs the product proof with the handcuff on. The quotient is the nastiest of the three, for one over b sub n threatens catastrophe wherever b sub n flirts with zero; the fix is to prove the denominator eventually keeps a respectful distance — at least half of M away from zero — and the desk supervises that negotiation in full. Strategy in the ear, masonry on paper: this is the division of labour we agreed to in the first hour, and tonight is its first industrial application.

One more instrument and the toolkit's first drawer is stocked: the squeeze. If a sequence is escorted — trapped between two sequences that both converge to the same limit L — then the prisoner converges to L as well; it has nowhere else to go. The picture is a police escort closing in from both sides, and the proof is one line of the game: beyond the thresholds where both escorts sit within epsilon of L, the prisoner, wedged between them, sits there too. The squeeze's daily use is taming wobble: a sequence like plus or minus one over n — sign misbehaving, magnitude dying — is escorted by minus one over n below and one over n above, both bound for zero; convergence certified without ever engaging the wobble in conversation. Anything bounded, divided by n, dies the same death; the desk drills it. One more law, free but subtle: limits respect order. If every term of one sequence sits at or below the corresponding term of another, and both converge, the limits obey the same inequality — escorts cannot finish behind their prisoners. But mind the flattening, for it costs marks and, later in the course, whole theorems: strict inequality does not survive the limit. One over n is strictly positive at every single term; its limit is zero, which is not. Limits flatten strictly-less into at-most; the desk files the law with the warning attached.

There, sir: the definition, the game, and the first machinery. Now the theorems worth the name — three of them, each a different answer to the question how does one prove arrival without exhibiting the destination in advance? — and the first of the three settles a debt to a gentleman in Zurich.

The Licensed Climb

Zurich, eighteen fifty-eight. A young Dedekind, preparing his first calculus lectures, arrives at the theorem that a bounded, ever-increasing quantity settles toward a value — and discovers, to his lasting embarrassment, that he possesses no proof, and neither does anyone else. You heard this story in Episode Two; it is the story that produced the cuts, the axiom, the whole foundation we now stand on. Tonight, sir, with the axiom in hand and the limit finally defined, we pay Zurich back: the theorem that embarrassed Dedekind falls in four sentences, and they are four sentences you can hold entirely in the ear.

The monotone convergence theorem: every climbing sequence that is bounded above converges. The proof. The terms form a non-empty set with a ceiling; completeness — the bell, sir, its sixth ring — grants that set a least ceiling, a supremum; call it s. I claim the climb converges to s, and the game is won by Episode Two's own vocabulary. The skeptic names a tolerance. Dip beneath the supremum by that tolerance: by the dip-and-overtake clause — the second clause in the very definition of supremum — some term of the sequence overtakes the dipped level. And the sequence climbs: every term after the overtaker is at least as large, while no term ever exceeds s, the ceiling. So beyond the overtaker's position — there is the threshold, delivered — every term is trapped between the dipped level and s: within tolerance of s, permanently. Match won for every epsilon. The climb converges, and it converges precisely to its supremum: the frontier the set was pressing against is the destination the sequence arrives at. Two episodes ago, the supremum was a static noun — the frontier no one stands on. Tonight it acquires its dynamic meaning: the frontier is where the climbers are going. I find the reconciliation of the two pictures deeply satisfying, and I hope its architecture is visible: the theorem is nothing but the definition of supremum read aloud with a stopwatch.

Mark what the theorem buys, because it buys the modern world. First, and dearest: it is a certificate of arrival that never names the destination. To use the game directly, one must know L in advance — the limit appears inside the very tolerance being checked. The monotone convergence theorem requires no such clairvoyance: climbing, bounded, therefore convergent — to something, existence guaranteed, identity negotiable later. This is the season's first existence machine, and completeness is its engine; run the same theorem in the rationals and it dies on the spot — the climbing decimals one, one and four tenths, one and forty-one hundredths, pressing toward the square root of two, are a bounded climb with nowhere to arrive, the exact casualty Episode Two showed you. Second: every decimal expansion in existence is a bounded climb — each further digit adds a scrap, and the whole is ceilinged — so every infinite decimal now officially names a real number. The notation you have used since childhood becomes, tonight, a theorem. In the same breath, Episode Two's picket fence acquires its dynamic restatement: every real number is the limit of a sequence of rationals — density plants a fraction within each tolerance one over n, and the harvested fence-posts converge to their target. Nearness and arrival, the two vocabularies of this course, are hereby one vocabulary. And third, a taste of manufacture: take the compound-interest sequence — one plus one over n, raised to the power n — the desk shows, with Bernoulli's inequality doing the heavy lifting, that it climbs and is bounded, whereupon the theorem summons its limit into existence: a number a little larger than two and seven tenths, which the world calls e. The very constant Hermite would prove transcendental in eighteen seventy-three is born at the desk this week, sir, as the destination of a licensed climb. Jacob Bernoulli first met it pricing compound interest, which I mention so that you may never again feel superior to the seventeenth century. The fine print, dutifully: bounded is essential — the climb one, two, three diverges, though with perfect manners: it diverges to infinity in tonight's licensed dialect, clearing every bar the skeptic posts. Indeed for monotone sequences the dichotomy is total — converge if bounded, diverge to infinity if not, no third option — for a climb is constitutionally incapable of the flip-flop's bigamy. File that tidiness away; it is why analysts convert problems into monotone ones at every opportunity, and why tonight's second half will keep reaching for climbing partial sums.

The Bisection Hunt

Second theorem, and a new character joins the repertory company. The monotone convergence theorem demands discipline of its sequences — climb, or descend, but choose. Most sequences refuse: they wobble, they wander, they visit. What can be salvaged from an arbitrary bounded mess? The answer is one of the load-bearing theorems of the subject, and its proof-technique is a character I hereby christen, because you will meet it again in Episodes Five and Six wearing different hats: the bisection hunt.

First a definition, one of the friendly ones. A subsequence is what remains when you thin a sequence out: keep infinitely many terms, discard the rest, preserve the order — every second term, say, or the terms at the prime positions, or any infinite selection marching rightward. A subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit — thinning a crowd already in residence changes nothing, and the desk notes it in two lines. The interesting direction is the reverse: can a divergent mess contain, hidden inside it, a convergent subsequence? The flip-flop can: discard the zeros and the remaining ones converge — constantly, smugly — to one. Is that luck, or law?

Law. The theorem of Bolzano and Weierstrass: every bounded sequence contains a convergent subsequence. However chaotic the wandering — wobble, drift, malice — if it is confined to a bounded stretch of line, then somewhere within it a convergent subsequence is hiding, guaranteed. And the proof is a hunt with equipment you own. The sequence's terms — infinitely many of them — live in some bounded interval. Halve it. Infinitely many terms cannot be split between two halves with both halves receiving finitely many — at least one half hosts infinitely many of the terms; keep a half that does. Halve again: again one of the halves hosts infinitely many; keep it. Forever. What is being built, sir? Closed intervals, each inside the last, widths halving toward nothing — the Russian dolls, assembled mid-hunt, and inside them, on the seventh ring of the bell, the nested interval theorem's unique survivor: one point, x star, trapped in every doll. Now harvest the subsequence: from the first doll, pick any term of the sequence inside it — the doll hosts infinitely many, so pick one; from the second doll, pick a term inside it whose position comes later than the first pick — infinitely many candidates remain, so pick one; and so on, one term per doll, positions ever rising. The harvested subsequence lives doll by shrinking doll alongside x star: its distance from x star is undercut by the doll widths, which die beneath every tolerance. The subsequence converges to x star. Hunt complete.

Savour the technique, because the technique is the point. The bisection hunt converts a mere infinitude — infinitely many terms, somewhere, in no order — into a location, by repeatedly asking one pigeonhole question: which half is crowded? It is completeness weaponized for search: the dolls do the cornering, the axiom guarantees the quarry. In Episode Five the same hunt will corner a very different kind of prey, and I shall say only that when it does, an entire concept — the season's next great noun — will crystallize around it. Bolzano, incidentally, reached this theorem first, in the very same unread paper of eighteen seventeen that Episode One mourned; Weierstrass, unaware, rebuilt it for his Berlin lectures half a century later, and that time the world was listening. The double name on the theorem is history apologizing to Prague.

Two footnotes seal the theorem's borders. Boundedness is essential: the sequence one, two, three contains no convergent subsequence — any infinite selection from it still marches past every bound; there is nothing to salvage from an escape, only from a confinement. And give the salvage a name: call the values that subsequences converge to the cluster values of a sequence — the levels it revisits within every tolerance, infinitely often. The flip-flop's cluster values are exactly zero and one. The theorem then reads: a bounded sequence has at least one cluster value. And when, shortly, we define the long-run ceiling of a sequence, it will turn out to be precisely the largest cluster value; the desk solders that connection.

Arrival Without a Destination

Third theorem, and the deepest of the three, though it wears the plainest coat. Both machines so far have limitations: the licensed climb demands monotonicity; the bisection hunt salvages only a subsequence. Here is the question that remains, and it is the practical question of the entire subject: given an arbitrary sequence, is there an intrinsic test — a test consulting only the terms themselves, never a candidate limit — that certifies the whole sequence converges? A test for arrival that never mentions the destination?

There is, and you met its inventor's name in Episode Two, on the rival firm's letterhead. Call a sequence a huddling sequence — the books say Cauchy sequence, and so shall I, interchangeably — when its terms eventually crowd one another within any tolerance: for every epsilon the skeptic names, there is a threshold beyond which any two terms of the sequence — any two, arbitrarily far along, compared to each other, not to any limit — sit within epsilon of one another. Not near a destination: near each other. The flock tightening, whether or not anyone can name the field it lands in.

Two claims, and the season turns on the second. First: every convergent sequence huddles. Naturally — terms all within half a tolerance of the limit sit within the whole tolerance of each other; the split-the-tolerance trick again, and the desk files it. Second, the profound converse: in the real numbers, every huddling sequence converges. Huddling suffices. The proof strategy is a relay of tonight's own machinery, and I shall run it for the ear in three legs. Leg one: a huddling sequence is bounded — beyond some threshold all terms crowd within one unit of each other, and the stragglers are finitely many; handcuffs on. Leg two: bounded — so the bisection hunt applies: some subsequence converges, to some x star. Leg three: the huddle now drags the whole flock to x star — far along, every term is within half a tolerance of its huddled brethren, and some brother from the subsequence is within half a tolerance of x star; split the tolerance, and the entire sequence is within epsilon of x star. The bell, sir — the eighth ring, sounding through the bisection hunt inside — and the theorem stands: in the completed line, huddling and converging are the same thing.

I want you to feel why this is the quietly load-bearing result of the hour. Everything, from here to Kolmogorov, needs existence certificates for limits nobody can name in advance — sums of series, integrals, solutions, fixed points. The Cauchy criterion is that certificate: measure the flock against itself, and completeness supplies the field. Cauchy himself, in the great Course of eighteen twenty-one, stated the criterion and treated its sufficiency as evident — he had, of course, no axiom of completeness to prove it with; the eighteen seventy-two warehouse retroactively underwrites his book, which is the sort of accounting irregularity history forgives in the brilliant. And do notice the homecoming: Cantor and Méray built the real numbers out of huddling sequences of rationals — the journey appointed the destination, Episode Two's phrase. Tonight's theorem is that construction's victory lap: in the completed line, every huddle has a home. In the rationals, of course, it does not — the truncations of the square root of two huddle beautifully and arrive nowhere, the same corpse Episode Two showed you from a different angle. Huddling-completeness and least-upper-bound completeness are, over an Archimedean field, two doors into the same house; the desk states the accounting honestly and points to references for the full audit. And bank the criterion's chief application now, for the second half of the hour spends it freely: a series converges precisely when its partial sums huddle, which unpacks to a statement about tails — for every tolerance, a threshold beyond which any stretch of consecutive terms, however long, totals within tolerance. Every convergence test in the trade is at bottom a machine for certifying that tails die, and the huddle is what makes tails-die equivalent to sum-exists without ever naming the sum. Arrival without a destination, industrialized.

The Honest Residue

A short interlude, sir, on what survives when convergence fails — bookkeeping the course will need later, delivered at interlude length. The flip-flop converges to nothing; yet it is not lawless. It has an upper settling level — one — and a lower — zero — and something in us wants those named. They can be. Track, from each position n onward, the supremum of everything from there on out: the ceiling of the remaining tail. As n advances and terms are discarded from consideration, that tail-ceiling can only descend or hold — discarding candidates never raises a supremum — so the tail-ceilings form a descending sequence, and if the original sequence was bounded, the licensed climb, run downhill, summons their limit into existence. That limit is the limit superior: the long-run ceiling, the level the sequence keeps reaching toward forever. Mirror the construction with floors and you have the limit inferior. For the flip-flop: limit superior one, limit inferior zero. And the clean criterion, which the desk has you prove: a bounded sequence converges precisely when its limit superior and limit inferior coincide — when the long-run ceiling and the long-run floor meet, the sequence is pinned between them at exactly one level. When they disagree, their gap is an honest measurement of the failure. Limits may fail, sir; limsup and liminf never do — for bounded sequences they always exist, which makes them the accountant's tool of choice in proofs where convergence is precisely what one is trying to establish and cannot yet assume. File the interlude; Season Two draws on it. And the wires promised in the hunt connect here: the limit superior is exactly the largest cluster value — the highest level the sequence never stops visiting — and the limit inferior the lowest; the desk solders the joint as promised.

The Infinite Sum, Arraigned

Now, sir, the second half of the hour, and the defendant Zeno has been waiting on: infinite addition. What can it possibly mean to add infinitely many numbers? The eighteenth century's answer — keep adding and write down what it looks like — gave us Euler's glories and Grandi's farce in the same breath, answers without definitions, moods. Tonight the definition is one sentence, because we spent the first half of the hour buying it: an infinite series is a sequence wearing a plus sign. To sum the series a one plus a two plus a three and so on, form the partial sums — first term; first two terms; first three — a perfectly ordinary sequence, one number per stage — and ask whether that sequence converges. If the partial sums converge to S, the series sums to S, by definition; if they diverge, the series has no sum, and no amount of wishing supplies one. Every question about infinite addition is hereby a question about sequences, and every machine built tonight applies. That is the entire theory; everything else is consequences.

And one farce closes on the spot. Grandi's series — one, minus one, plus one, minus one, the eighteenth century's favourite parlour trick — has partial sums one, zero, one, zero. It is the flip-flop, sir. Grandi's series has been in the room all evening, wearing a plus sign, and we prosecuted it to conviction in the first act: no limit, no sum, case closed. The value one half that Episode One reported now stands officially exposed as a mood — with the standing footnote of the honesty clause, that modern summation theories do assign it one half lawfully, under different definitions built for different purposes. Under tonight's definition, the definition of analysis, the books are shut.

First consequence, and a debt of the first hour paid in full view: the geometric series, honestly. Take ratio one half — Zeno's own — and sum one half plus one quarter plus one eighth, onward. The partial sums have a closed form the desk derives by the telescoping trick — multiply the partial sum by the ratio, subtract, watch the middle annihilate — and for ratio one half the result says: the partial sum through stage n is exactly one, minus one over two to the n. So the question of summation becomes: does one over two to the n die? It does, and mark who kills it: Bernoulli's inequality — hired in Episode One, wages earned tonight — pins two to the n above n itself, whence one over two to the n is undercut by one over n, and Archimedes finishes as always. The partial sums converge to one. The geometric series with ratio one half sums to one — not as a picture, not as a mood, but as a limit certified by the game. And in general, for any ratio smaller than one in size, the same telescope and the same Bernoulli execution yield the classical formula — first term over one minus the ratio — while for ratios one or larger the terms refuse to die and the partial sums march off or thrash. The desk does every case. With that, sir, a credit formally logged since Episode One — and pointedly dodged in Episode Three, when I built the dust's address system out of dolls precisely to avoid borrowing it — is discharged. The geometric series is licensed. The ledger lightens.

And so, at last, to Elea. The Dichotomy: before crossing the room, cross half; before the remainder, half of that — infinitely many stages. Achilles: reach where the tortoise was; it has moved on; reach the new mark; it has moved again — infinitely many stages. Zeno's engine, in both, is the inference that infinitely many positive quantities must amount to something infinite, or at least to something uncompletable. Tonight that inference is refuted by exhibit. The stage-lengths are geometric; their partial sums converge; the total distance is finite, the total time is finite, and — this is the part the schoolbook answer could not honestly say — we now possess a definition under which those words mean something: the limit of the partial sums, certified against every tolerance the skeptic owns. Achilles overtakes the tortoise at a specific, computable moment, on the far side of infinitely many stages whose totality is a number. Sir: I note for the register that this discharges the second of our standing accounts — Zeno's race, docketed in Episode One, paid in Episode Four with interest — and I permit myself one sentence of scruple, per the honesty clause. What mathematics has settled is Zeno's arithmetic: infinitely many terms may have a finite total, and the notion of total is now rigorous. Whether physical motion truly performs infinitely many acts — what philosophers call the supertask question — is a bill addressed to metaphysics, and I do not pay other departments' invoices. Before the bombshell, one small necessity with a large moral. If a series converges, its terms must die to zero — each term is the difference of two consecutive partial sums, both marching to the same S, so their difference marches to zero; two lines at the desk. The moral is the converse's falsehood, and it is one of the great traps of the subject: terms dying to zero is necessary and nothing like sufficient. The proof of that is six hundred years old, sir, and it is the prettiest thing a fourteenth-century mind ever did to an infinite object.

The Fourteenth-Century Bombshell

The harmonic series, so called for its kinship with the overtones of a vibrating string: one, plus one half, plus one third, plus one quarter, and so on down the reciprocals forever. Its terms die to zero with perfect obedience. Every instinct trained on the geometric series says it must settle — the contributions grow so small, so fast, surely the total tires. It does not. The harmonic series diverges: its partial sums, given time, exceed one hundred, one million, any bound you care to post. And the proof, sir, is due to Nicole Oresme — Parisian scholar, philosopher, translator of Aristotle, eventually a bishop — writing around thirteen fifty, in a set of questions on Euclid's geometry, some three centuries before calculus and five before the definition we built tonight. It is among the prettiest things a medieval mind ever did to an infinite object, and it fits in the ear whole.

Group the terms, says Oresme, and do not group them evenly — group them by doubling. After the opening one, take the single term one half: worth one half. Then the next two terms, one third and one quarter: each is at least one quarter, so together they are worth at least one half. Then the next four terms, one fifth through one eighth: each is at least one eighth — four of them — worth at least one half. Then the next eight terms, each at least one sixteenth: at least one half again. Every doubling block, however deep into the series, contributes at least one half — the terms shrink, but the blocks lengthen in exact compensation — and there are infinitely many blocks. The partial sums therefore climb past every bound, half-step by relentless half-step. No sum. Divergence, certified — and notice, sir, with some awe, that Oresme's argument required no theory of limits whatsoever: it is a comparison of finite partial sums against a finite tally of halves, and its conclusion — the partial sums outgrow every bound — is precisely our modern definition of divergence to infinity, reached from the far side of six centuries. The theorem was rediscovered and reproved down the ages, by the Bernoulli brothers among others, as good theorems are; the priority is the bishop's.

Two morals, one practical and one for the soul. Practical: terms dying to zero is necessary for convergence and nothing resembling sufficient — the harmonic series is the standing counterexample, and half the errors of the innumerate consist of forgetting it. For the soul: the divergence is glacial beyond human intuition. The blocks that each pay one half are doubling in length, so the climb is logarithmically slow — to push the partial sums past a mere three hundred requires more terms, the desk shows you with grim arithmetic, than there are atoms in the observable universe. The harmonic series diverges the way empires fall: certainly, and no one lives to see it. A computer summing it until the sun dies would report convergence and be wrong. Which is, if I may, the entire case for proof over experiment, compressed into one series.

Tests, and Two Kinds of Convergence

With the geometric series licensed and the harmonic series exposed, the working tests of convergence fall out as corollaries, and I shall be brief, as the plan demands — these are tools, and tools belong at the desk. The comparison test: a series of non-negative terms, each trapped beneath the corresponding term of a series already known to converge, must itself converge — its partial sums climb (non-negative terms) and are ceilinged (by the dominating total), and the licensed climb does the rest; the theorem is the monotone convergence theorem in working clothes. The ratio test, drilled into every calculus student and rarely explained: if the ratios of successive terms settle strictly below one, the series converges — because from some point on it is undercut by an honest geometric series, and comparison finishes. The ratio test is the geometric series wearing a disguise, sir; now you know its engine. Both proofs to paper, with the strategies as stated. And one dividend with a familiar name, since comparison plus the licensed climb settles it in a few lines: the sum of the reciprocals of the squares — one, plus one quarter, plus one ninth, Euler's Basel patient from Episode One — converges. Each reciprocal square from the second onward is trapped beneath a telescoping bound: one over the product of n and its predecessor, which is exactly the gap between two consecutive plain reciprocals; and gaps, summed, collapse like a spyglass, ceilinging every partial sum below two. Bounded climb; convergence certified; the desk works the telescope in full. The value — pi squared over six — remains Euler's illegal miracle, its legalization beyond this course; but the convergence, sir, is ours tonight, honestly.

Now a distinction the rest of the hour turns on. Call a series absolutely convergent if the series of the sizes of its terms — every minus sign stripped away — converges. Absolute convergence implies convergence: the strategy is the huddle criterion applied to partial sums — the tail of the stripped series crushes the tail of the original by the triangle inequality — and the desk files it. But the converse fails, and the standing counterexample is the loveliest series in the subject: the alternating harmonic series, one, minus one half, plus one third, minus one quarter, signs forever alternating. Watch its partial sums move: one; down to one half; up to five sixths; down again — each step smaller than the last, overshooting the destination alternately from above and below. The odd-position partial sums descend; the even-position partial sums climb; every odd sits above every even; and the gap between them at each stage is exactly the newest term, which dies to zero. The partial sums are building their own Russian dolls, sir — nested, shrinking, closing on a single number — and the licensed climb, run once uphill and once down, certifies the trap. The alternating harmonic series converges: to a definite constant, a little under seven tenths, whose familiar name I shall supply the moment logarithms are lawfully ours — a small honesty note in the ledger. The test generalizes: any series of alternating signs whose term-sizes descend to zero converges, a criterion tradition attributes to Leibniz; its two-line proof is at the desk with the solution supplied, since the zigzag picture I just narrated is the proof.

But strip the signs from the alternating harmonic series and you are staring at Oresme's patient: the harmonic series, divergent. So here is the phenomenon, named: a series can converge without converging absolutely — convergent, but only by the grace of its minus signs. Call it conditionally convergent, and hear the warning in the word. A conditionally convergent series has infinite positive ambitions and infinite negative ambitions in exquisite cancellation; its books balance, but only in the order presented. It is a truce, sir, not a peace. And tonight's finale is what happens when someone disturbs the seating arrangement.

The Rearrangement Outrage

Let us be precise about the crime before committing it. To rearrange a series is to use exactly the same terms — every one of them, each exactly once — in a different order: a reshuffling of the infinite deck, nothing added, nothing dropped. For finite sums, rearrangement is beneath discussion: commutativity, the oldest privilege of the plus sign, learned at the age of six — three plus five is five plus three, and a grocer may total the basket in any order he pleases. The question of the hour: does the privilege survive infinity?

In eighteen thirty-seven, Dirichlet — our returning character, the man who tamed Fourier's convergence — noticed, and published, something disquieting: rearranging a conditionally convergent series can change its sum. He also proved the consoling half, to which I shall return. But the full, annihilating truth arrived with Riemann, in the very same eighteen fifty-four qualifying essay that built his integral — the essay Episode One told you about, one paper containing two revolutions, published only in eighteen sixty-seven, after his death, edited for the press by his friend Dedekind. The man is everywhere, sir; loyalty was his second theorem. Here is Riemann's rearrangement theorem, and I advise you to sit down even if you are already sitting down. Take any conditionally convergent series. Take any target whatsoever — any real number you care to name. There exists a rearrangement of the series summing exactly to your target. Any target. The same terms, reshuffled, sum to five; reshuffled again, to minus one million; again, to the square root of two. And further rearrangements diverge to plus infinity, or to minus infinity, or thrash forever without settling. One deck of cards; every conceivable hand.

The proof, sir — and this is the wonder I have budgeted for tonight, so I shall spend it walking you through the whole strategy, because the outrage of the statement is only half the pleasure; the other half is how reasonable the mechanism is. Two steps. Step one, the fuel report. Sort the terms of our conditionally convergent series into two piles: the positives and the negatives. I claim both piles, taken alone, are infinite in total — both diverge. They must: if both piles had finite totals, the stripped series would have a finite total and our series would be absolutely convergent, which it is not; if exactly one pile were finite, the other would drag the partial sums off to its own infinity and the series would diverge, which it does not. Conditional convergence, in other words, is exactly the regime of two infinite tanks — unlimited positive fuel, unlimited negative fuel — held in cancellation. Meanwhile the individual terms, being terms of a convergent series, still die dutifully to zero. Unlimited fuel; ever-smaller droplets. You may already see the heist.

Step two, the steering. The skeptic hands us a target — say, five. Begin spending the positive pile, in its original order, term after term, until the running total first climbs past five. This must eventually happen: the positive tank is infinite. Stop there. Now spend from the negative pile, term after term, until the total first drops below five. Must happen: the negative tank is infinite too. Stop. Now positives again, to just above five; now negatives, to just below; and so on, forever, alternating raids on the two tanks. Every term of the original series is eventually spent — each pile is consumed in order, nothing skipped — so this is a genuine rearrangement, the full deck dealt. And the running total? It crosses and recrosses five forever — but attend to the size of the overshoot. We stop at the first crossing, every time; so the total overshoots five by at most the size of the final term spent — and the terms are dying to zero. The swings around the target are throttled by a quantity that shrinks beneath every tolerance the skeptic can name. The partial sums of the rearranged series converge — to five, exactly. Nothing about five was special: any target invites the same steering. And to manufacture divergence, steer for ever-rising stations — past one, then past two, then past three — never settling. That is the entire proof, sir; the desk hands you the index bookkeeping as a starred guided ascent, and you will find no step in it beyond what tonight built.

Now let the meaning land, because this is the moment the infinite stops imitating the finite even in arithmetic, and I have seen it unsettle professionals. Addition — infinite addition — is not commutative. The sum of a conditionally convergent series is not a property of its collection of terms; it is a property of their order. Ask "what do these numbers add up to?" of the alternating harmonic terms, as a bare set of contributions, and the question has no answer — a little under seven tenths is merely what the standard dealing produces; the same cards, dealt one-positive-two-negatives, produce — the desk works this jewel in full, and you may audit every line — exactly half that total. The same terms. Half the sum. Your childhood grocer, totalling an infinite basket, would find the bill depending on the order the items left the shelf. The consolation, Dirichlet's half, restores the border: an absolutely convergent series is rearrangement-proof — every reshuffle, the same sum, and the desk stars the proof for the ambitious. So the privilege of commutativity is not abolished; it is licensed, and the license is absolute convergence. Conditional convergence is the borderlands, where the books balance only by treaty, and every treaty names its seating order.

And now, sir, the deep diagnosis, which is tonight's parting gift and the season's next debt. Why did this happen? Strip the drama and look at the machinery: a sum is a limit — of partial sums, taken in a stated order. Rearrangement changes the order in which an infinite process is executed. We exchanged the order of operations inside a limiting process, and the answer changed. You have seen this crime's silhouette before: Cauchy's false theorem in Episode One — a limit of continuous functions, sworn continuous, refuted by Fourier's jagged assemblies — was an illegal exchange of two limits. Tonight's outrage is another. I therefore open, formally and with ceremony, the account this course has been circling since its first hour. When may two limiting processes be exchanged? Logged tonight as a standing debt — the swap account — with scheduled instalments: Episode Seven, where limits of functions collide with continuity and the monster is built; Episode Eight, where a limit is swapped with an integral and draws blood; and Season Two, whose crown jewels — I say this now so you may hold me to it — are precisely the theorems that license the swap. Half of analysis, sir, is the criminology of illegal exchanges. Tonight you witnessed your first arrest.
