# The Zoo of Convergence, or Littlewood's Three Principles
### The Measure of All Things, Season Two, Episode Six

## The Field Guide

Good day, sir.

John Edensor Littlewood -- Hardy's collaborator, Trinity's resident sceptic, and a man whose working maxims have outlived most other men's theorems -- claimed that the whole of this season's subject reduces to three heuristics, and I shall read them as our field guide tonight. Every measurable set is nearly a finite union of intervals. Every measurable function is nearly continuous. Every convergent sequence of measurable functions is nearly uniformly convergent. Three sentences, sir -- and the word doing all the work in each is nearly. Not approximately, not morally: nearly, in the exact currency this season mints -- off a set of measure as small as you please. Tonight the establishment takes inventory after last week's summit: five modes of convergence now circulate on the premises, and we shall sort the entire menagerie -- which modes imply which, which extract which along subsequences, and which counterexamples keep every arrow honest -- then give Littlewood's second and third principles their formal names, Lusin and Egorov, and close with a structure the twentieth century built from tonight's materials: the space whose points are functions and whose distance is an integral. The first principle, you will recognize, was banked two episodes... four episodes ago, at the approximation manners; tonight completes the trilogy.

## The Five Modes

The cast, introduced in one breath apiece, each with its character.

Uniform convergence you have known since Season One, Episode Seven: one tube, every point inside, the aristocrat's mode -- expensive and unbending. Pointwise convergence, likewise of long acquaintance: every point settles, each at its own pace, no coordination -- the mode that shattered continuity and taught the course to fear. Almost-everywhere convergence, the amnesty's child: pointwise off a null set -- pointwise, as this season prices things, with the clerical noise discarded. Convergence in the mean: the integral of the discrepancy tends to nought -- last week's rider, now promoted to a mode in its own right; the sequence and its limit agree in total mass of disagreement, wherever that disagreement sits. And the newcomer, convergence in measure: for every tolerance, the ground on which the discrepancy exceeds the tolerance shrinks to nothing -- not the size of the disagreement but the acreage of it, sent to zero. The statistician's mode, sir, and your trade's: convergence in probability is this mode wearing next week's... the finale's costume, and every consistency proof you have ever cited lives here.

## The Modes in the Wild

Before the map, a word on why five modes exist at all, since the zoo can look like taxonomy for its own sake. Each mode, sir, is the natural currency of a different working question. Uniform convergence is the numericist's guarantee: one error bound, valid across the whole domain -- the tabulated function trusted everywhere at once, Season One's grid-table certificate. Almost-everywhere convergence is the trajectory-watcher's mode: fix the sample path, follow it forever -- the strong law of large numbers, when the finale states it, will speak this dialect. Convergence in the mean is the auditor's: total expected error, mass of mistake -- the loss your trade actually minimizes, integrated over the data. And convergence in measure -- in probability, on mass-one ground -- is the statistician's: the chance of a noticeable error vanishes, nothing said about totals or trajectories; every consistency theorem in your libraries speaks it. Five questions, five currencies; the map that follows is the exchange-rate table, and the counterexamples are the exchange's posted warnings.

## The Geography

Now the map -- what lies upstream of what -- narrated as geography and drawn properly at the desk, with a proof or a counterexample on every arrow, no exceptions.

Uniform sits at the headwaters: it implies pointwise, hence almost-everywhere, trivially; on finite ground it implies the mean -- the tube's cross-section times a finite width -- and thence in-measure. Downstream, the mean implies measure always, by an argument you should hear once, for it is one line and your trade calls it by name: the ground where the discrepancy exceeds epsilon contributes at least epsilon times its own acreage to the integral, so the acreage is at most the integral over epsilon. Mass bounds acreage -- Chebyshev's inequality in embryo, sir; the finale will christen it properly, and it is the map's most-travelled road. Almost-everywhere implies in-measure on finite ground -- a dividend of Egorov, presently -- and fails without finiteness: the marching bump converges at every point, yet its discrepancy-acreage holds at one forever; the horizon exit again, closed to it last week by lids, open to it tonight because no lid is asked. Walk the arrows once, sir, compass in hand. From uniform: south to pointwise, southeast (on finite ground) to the mean; every road out of uniform is paved. From pointwise: south to almost-everywhere, and no road east -- the spike blocks the way to the mean. From the mean: east to in-measure, always, by Chebyshev's embryo. From almost-everywhere: east to in-measure on finite ground only -- the bump patrols the unbounded frontier. And from in-measure: no road back west to almost-everywhere -- the typewriter stands athwart it -- save the subsequence bridle-path, presently. Every other pairing is a composition of these or a counterexample; the desk's diagram carries the complete table, every cell warranted. And the mean and almost-everywhere, sir, imply each other in neither direction -- the gallery's two prize beasts each break one arrow. The spike converges to zero almost everywhere while its mean discrepancy holds at one: pointwise settlement, mass unsurrendered. The other direction's beast deserves its own section, and a moment of theatre besides, for I have been saving it since the season was planned.

## The Typewriter

Watch, sir -- I shall narrate it slowly, as it deserves.

Take the unit interval. The first function: the indicator of the whole interval -- one block, full width. Then two blocks, each of width one half: the indicator of the left half; then of the right half. Then four blocks of width one quarter, sweeping left to right: first quarter, second, third, fourth. Then eight blocks of width one eighth, sweeping again; then sixteen; and so on forever -- each generation halving the block and sweeping the interval end to end, like a typewriter's carriage: type across the line, return, halve the type, type across again. That is the sequence -- the typewriter -- and I ask two questions of it.

Where does it converge in measure, and in the mean? Everywhere, and emphatically: the n-th block's width -- its supporting acreage, and equally its integral -- is one over two to the k at generation k, and generations advance forever; discrepancy-acreage and discrepancy-mass alike march to zero. By both of the integral-flavoured modes, the typewriter converges to the zero function, and rapidly.

And where does it converge pointwise? Nowhere, sir. Not at one single point of the interval. Fix any point you please: every generation's carriage sweeps the whole interval, so every generation sends exactly one block across your point -- the function reads one there, once per generation, forever; and between visits it reads nought. At your point -- at every point -- the sequence reads nought, nought, one, nought, one, nought, nought, one, without end: infinitely many ones, infinitely many noughts, no settlement, no limit. A sequence converging to zero in measure and in mean, and converging pointwise at no point whatsoever.

Let the wonder beat sound here, for this is the evening's designated moment and the finest convergence counterexample in the literature. The two families of modes, sir, are measuring different things, and the typewriter is the proof that neither family reports to the other. The integral modes watch the mass and the acreage of disagreement: both vanish, and those modes are satisfied -- the disagreement never lingers anywhere. The pointwise modes stand at a fixed address and demand the visits stop: they never do; the carriage always returns. Smallness of the offence and persistence of the offence are different failures -- the typewriter offends infinitely often, each time more briefly; total offence vanishing, offence at every address eternal. Season One taught you that a function's behaviour at points and its behaviour in bulk could diverge; the typewriter is that lesson promoted to sequences, and it is, I submit, a perfect ear-object: you can hear the carriage return.

## The Consolation Prize

The typewriter breaks the arrow from measure to almost-everywhere; here is what survives of it, and it suffices surprisingly often.

Convergence in measure extracts almost-everywhere convergence along a subsequence. The strategy is the season's signature gambit in its purest form. From the in-measure hypothesis, harvest a fast subsequence: a first index where the epsilon-half discrepancy-ground is smaller than one half; a later index where the quarter-ground is under a quarter; a later still where the eighth-ground is under an eighth -- the k-th chosen index confining its two-to-the-k discrepancy to acreage under two to the minus k. Now total the acreages beyond any stage: a geometric tail, as small as you please; so the points visited by infinitely many of the confined discrepancy-grounds form a null set -- trapped under tails of every size. Off that null set, every point is eventually clear of the grounds, and the fast subsequence settles within every tolerance: almost-everywhere convergence, extracted. The typewriter illustrates rather than resists: its generations' first blocks -- widths one, one half, one quarter -- form exactly such a fast subsequence, converging to zero at every point except the left endpoint, a set of one. And I flag, for the finale, the argument's skeleton: summable acreages force finitely many visits almost surely -- the course will meet it there under its proper name, the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, and you will have proved it tonight without being told.

## Egorov's Theorem

Littlewood's third principle, made rigorous -- and note that it is the principle the typewriter appears to mock: every convergent sequence is nearly uniformly convergent. The typewriter converges pointwise nowhere, so the principle owes it nothing; the principle speaks of sequences that do converge, and about those it says something remarkable.

Egorov's theorem, nineteen eleven. On ground of finite measure, if a sequence of measurable functions converges almost everywhere, then for every epsilon there is a citizen set, occupying all but epsilon of the ground, on which the convergence is uniform. Uniform, sir -- the aristocrat's mode, Season One's expensive license -- purchased on almost all the ground for an epsilon of sacrificed acreage. Pointwise convergence, the mode that shattered continuity, is uniform convergence wearing a threadbare patch: cut out a set as small as you please, and the tube closes around everything that remains.

The proof strategy fits in the ear, and it is two of this season's instruments in sequence. For a fixed tolerance -- one over k -- consider the bad tails: the ground where some discrepancy beyond stage n still exceeds the tolerance. As n grows these bad tails shrink, and they shrink to nothing -- almost-everywhere convergence says almost no point stays bad forever -- so the shrinking crankshaft, on finite ground with its finite first floor, sends their measures to zero: some stage confines the k-tolerance offenders to acreage under epsilon over two to the k. Do this for every k and spend the gambit: total sacrificed acreage under epsilon, and on the remainder every tolerance is met uniformly beyond its stage -- which is uniform convergence, certificate in hand. Shrinking crankshaft plus the epsilon-over-two-to-the-k gambit; the desk writes it in full, and marks where finite ground is spent -- the crankshaft's finite floor -- with the marching bump as the standing witness that the clause is real: on the infinite line, the bump converges everywhere and is uniform on no cofinite-measure set; its escape is exactly the crankshaft's caveat at work.

## Lusin's Theorem

Littlewood's second principle: every measurable function is nearly continuous. Formally -- Lusin, nineteen twelve: a measurable function, finite almost everywhere on a frame, admits, for every epsilon, a closed set occupying all but epsilon of the frame on which the function's restriction is continuous. The monsters of two seasons, sir -- the static, Thomae's creature, anything the tribe can weigh -- are continuous functions in disguise, the disguise being a set of acreage epsilon; respectable citizens, almost.

Two footnotes give the theorem its exact edges. Continuity of the restriction, sir, not continuity at the points: the static restricted to the irrationals is identically nought -- continuous as a function on that set -- though the static is continuous at no point of the line; Lusin promises the former, never the latter, and the distinction is worth a pin through it. And the theorem characterizes: a function with Lusin's property -- nearly continuous at every epsilon -- is necessarily measurable, so the principle is an exact portrait of the tribe, not merely a courtesy it extends. The proof is an assembly of the season's parts, and the episode gives the assembly order while the desk gives the references and the sketch, per the plan's own budget: staircase-approximate the function by simple functions; each simple function is nearly continuous -- its storeys' citizens are nearly finite unions of intervals, the first principle, banked at the approximation manners; the staircases converge almost everywhere, so Egorov -- tonight's own theorem -- makes the convergence nearly uniform; and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, Season One's three-legged stool, still load-bearing two seasons on. Nearly, plus nearly, plus Egorov's nearly, the epsilons split by the gambit: three sacrifices, each as small as you please, and the closed certificate emerges from the regularity manners. All three of Littlewood's principles, sir, are one epsilon-management discipline viewed from three angles -- sets, functions, sequences -- and tonight the trilogy stands assembled, each principle citing the others' paperwork.

## The Mean as a Distance

The last movement opens a door the course will not walk through, but the doorway itself is one of the twentieth century's load-bearing arches, and this season built every stone of it.

Consider the integrable functions on a fixed ground, and declare the distance between two of them to be the integral of their absolute discrepancy -- the mean distance. Convergence in the mean is exactly convergence in this distance; the amnesty becomes geometry -- functions equal almost everywhere stand at distance zero, so the space's honest points are the equivalence classes, functions-up-to-clerical-noise, precisely the objects Episode Four said the theory works with; and the triangle inequality is the additivity of the integral applied to a pointwise triangle. A space, sir, whose points are functions and whose geometry is integration: the trade calls it L-one, and its younger siblings -- the L-p family, distances built from p-th powers, your trade's regularizers among them -- are glimpsed at the desk with their nesting on finite ground.

And the theorem that makes the space an instrument rather than a curiosity: it is complete. Every sequence of integrable functions that huddles in the mean -- Cauchy in the mean distance, Season One's dialect -- converges in the mean to an integrable limit. Riesz and Fischer, nineteen oh seven -- two proofs, independent, the same year, and both, fittingly, in papers about Fourier series: the account that opened this course funding, once again, the mathematics that answers it. And the bell, sir -- ring the seventeenth, and I cite the precedent with the ceremony: ring twelve, the first ever inked off the real line, rang for the completeness of the space of continuous functions under the uniform distance; tonight its exact successor -- the completeness of the space of integrable functions under the mean distance -- and the proof is this season's machinery entire, conducted at the desk as the guided centrepiece: extract a fast subsequence by the gambit; stack its discrepancies into a nonnegative series; the climbing license prices the stack integrable; its finiteness almost everywhere hands the subsequence an almost-everywhere limit; and the dominated jewel -- lid supplied by the stack -- closes the huddle onto the limit in the mean. Every jewel of last week, spent once, in order. The count stands at seventeen, flagged for veto as its predecessors were; the axiom of completeness, having built the line, the integral of Season One, and the integral of Season Two, now certifies the space in which twentieth-century analysis actually lives. Hilbert's spectral theory, Fourier's account at its eventual settlement, the function spaces of your trade's optimizers -- all tenants, sir, of the arch completed tonight.

## The Exiles

Two inequalities remain, and I shall treat them exactly as the plan directs -- named, motivated, and exiled to paper by name -- because they are the standing example of this course's ear-and-desk discipline, and I say so, sir, on the record, as the plan instructs me to.

Hölder's inequality: the integral of a product is bounded by the product of conjugate-power norms -- the reason products of integrable things integrate, the engine of every duality your trade's optimization theory runs on. Minkowski's inequality: the triangle inequality for the whole L-p family -- the reason the p-distances are distances at all. Each is motivated in one sentence, as I have just done; each is proved at the desk properly, via Young's inequality with the calculus conducted in full; and neither will be narrated further aloud, because each is precisely the species of epsilon-and-exponent management that the ear cannot hold and was never asked to. Named, motivated, exiled. The discipline of two seasons, sir, in its purest specimen: the honest division of labour is not a compromise; it is the design.
