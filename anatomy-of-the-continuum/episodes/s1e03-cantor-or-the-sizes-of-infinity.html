Cantor, or the Sizes of Infinity

The Anatomy of the Continuum, Season One, Episode Three

The Weighing of the Crowds

Good day, sir.

Last episode closed with a question left deliberately dangling, and I trust it has been swinging gently at the back of your mind ever since. We established that the rationals and the irrationals are perfectly interleaved: between any two members of either society lives a member of the other; neither holds an unbroken stretch of the line, however microscopic. Two populations, mutually permeating, everywhere entangled — and I invited the natural conclusion, that they must be populations of comparable size, before advising you to hold it loosely. Tonight the scale is brought out, the two crowds are weighed, and I may as well tell you now that the reading is one of the great shocks in the history of thought: the scale does not balance. It does not even come close. One of the two societies is, in a sense tonight will make exact, negligible beside the other — and it is not the society you would guess from the confidence of its members.

The instrument of the weighing is due to Georg Cantor, whom you have now twice seen loitering at the edges of our story — dragged into the infinite by Fourier's uniqueness question, met by chance at Interlaken in the summer of eighteen seventy-two, striking up with Dedekind the most consequential correspondence in mathematics. Tonight the correspondence delivers. The theorem at the centre of this hour crossed Europe in an envelope, in December of eighteen seventy-three, before it saw a journal; and the murder weapon, sir — I promised you this — is an item of equipment you inspected personally last week. The Russian dolls are about to earn their keep.

The plan of the hour: first, what same size must honestly mean when counting is impossible — a question settled, charmingly, by shepherds. Then the countable menagerie, and the first shock: the rationals, that impenetrably dense crowd, can be marched single file. Then the two murders: eighteen seventy-four, the reals proved uncountable by the dolls; eighteen ninety-one, the same theorem proved again by four lines so consequential they echo inside every computer ever built — including, sir, in a three-minute detour I have prepared, the precise reason no program can decide what all programs will do. Then the census of numbers and its scandalous finding; the arithmetic of the infinite and the question that broke; the birth of a recurring character made of dust; and a closing word on what all this cost the man who found it.

An hour on the sizes of infinity, plural. Bring your intuition, as I said. It will not be coming back intact.

What Same Size Must Mean

Begin with a scene older than arithmetic. A shepherd who cannot count wishes to know, each evening, whether his flock is whole. He keeps a pouch of pebbles, one pebble per sheep, matched up long ago. At dusk the sheep file past and he moves one pebble per animal. If pebbles and sheep run out together, the flock is whole. He has not counted; he could not say whether he owns forty sheep or sixty; and yet his method is flawless. What the shepherd knows — and what the nineteenth century had to consciously recover — is that comparison is more primitive than counting. Two collections are the same size when they can be matched perfectly, one to one: every member of each paired with exactly one member of the other, none left over, none doubly claimed. Mathematicians call such a perfect matching a bijection; I shall mostly say a perfect dance. Counting itself is just a special case — to count a flock is to dance it with a standard reference crowd, the crowd one, two, three, and so on up to some final number. The pebbles reveal the deep structure: size is dancing, and numbers are merely the names we give the dances that end.

Which raises the door-opening question. For finite crowds, dancing and counting agree, and no one needed a theory. But infinite crowds cannot be counted — the census never terminates. Can they dance? Certainly: pair every whole number with its double, and the whole numbers have danced with the even numbers — one with two, two with four, three with six, perfectly, forever, nobody wallflowered. And there is the first tremor, sir, felt long before Cantor. Galileo, in his final book — published in sixteen thirty-eight, when he was old, blind, and under house arrest — has his characters notice exactly this: every whole number has exactly one square, every square exactly one whole-number root; the numbers and the squares dance perfectly. Yet the squares are visibly a minority — walk up the number line and they thin out ruthlessly, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-five, with ever-longer deserts between. A part of the whole numbers, dancing as an equal with all of the whole numbers. Galileo stared at this, concluded that the words larger and smaller simply do not apply to infinites, and set the matter down. It was the right observation and the wrong conclusion, and the gap between them took two and a half centuries to close. The resolution, when it came, was to stop treating the phenomenon as a paradox and start treating it as a definition. A collection is infinite precisely when it can dance with a proper part of itself — that is Dedekind's definition, sir, our quiet friend from Brunswick again, and it turns Galileo's scandal into the membership card. Finite crowds cannot spare a single member from the dance; infinite crowds can lose whole battalions and pair off regardless. That is not a defect of the infinite. That is what the infinite is. Note the manoeuvre, sir, for it is the second time this course has performed it and it will not be the last: a scandal inverted into a definition — Galileo's paradox made the membership card, exactly as Dedekind made the hole into the number. When mathematics cannot defeat a monster, it hires it and issues a uniform.

You may have encountered the popular staging of this idea: the hotel with infinitely many rooms, all occupied, which nonetheless accommodates a new guest by shifting every occupant up one room — and accommodates infinitely many new guests by sending each occupant to double his room number, vacating all the odd rooms at a stroke. The establishment is traditionally attributed to a lecture of Hilbert's, and I check us in and out briskly, sir, because the premises have been thoroughly overrun by popularizers and the linen is tired. But do note, before we leave, what the hotel is actually demonstrating, because it is tonight's load-bearing fact wearing a bellhop's uniform: adding one to an infinite crowd does not enlarge it; adding a whole infinite crowd of newcomers does not enlarge it. Infinite sizes are magnificently insensitive to insult. Which makes the true business of tonight all the more remarkable — because the natural guess, after the hotel, is that all infinite crowds dance with all others; that infinity is one size, vast and undifferentiated, and the story ends here. That guess held the field, more or less unexamined, for all of human history until December of eighteen seventy-three. It is false. Some infinite crowds cannot dance together, no matter how the pairing is attempted — and proving that a dance is impossible, sir, every dance, every conceivable pairing scheme, is a feat of the same species as Episode One's impossible fraction: a negative, established over an infinity of candidates, by pure structure. You know the species now. You know it can be done.

The Single File

Vocabulary, then, locked for the rest of both seasons. The reference crowd is the whole numbers: one, two, three, and onward. A collection that can dance with the whole numbers is called countable — or, in the fuller phrase, countably infinite. Being countable has a wonderfully concrete meaning: it means the collection can be marched single file. A dance with the whole numbers is nothing but a roster — a first member, a second, a third — an infinite queue in which every single member holds a definite, finite position. The queue never ends, of course; but no member waits forever to be named. Ask for the member at position one million and I produce him; more importantly, hand me any member whatsoever and I can tell you his finite position in the line. Countable means listable, sir, with everyone on the list at a finite address. Hold that picture — the single file, everyone reachable — because tonight's great question is precisely which crowds can be made to queue. One caution on the word, planted now and harvested in a later act: a queue, for tonight's purposes, is a God's-eye roster — it must exist, not be printable. Whether some mechanical procedure could generate the list is a separate and stricter question, and the gap between the two is exactly where a certain saboteur will later live.

The whole numbers themselves, trivially. The even numbers, as the shepherd's doubling dance shows. The squares, by Galileo. What about the integers — the whole numbers now with zero and all the negatives, stretching to infinity in two directions? A naive march from one end never arrives, since there is no end to start from; but nobody said the queue must respect the order of the line. March from the middle, alternating: zero first, then one, then minus one, then two, then minus two, and so on, zigzagging outward. Every integer, positive or negative, is swept up at a finite position — the number minus one million waits roughly two million places, a long wait but a finite one. The integers are countable. Notice the lesson in the trick, because it is the lesson of the whole section: the queue is ours to design. The crowd does not choose the marching order; we do, and a clever order can tame a crowd that a naive order never reaches.

The Zigzag

Now the test that matters — the crowd that, by every instinct cultivated last episode, ought to be unqueueable. The rationals. Recall what density means: between any two fractions, infinitely many more; no fraction has a next-door neighbour; every neighbourhood of every fraction is as crowded as the whole society. How could such a crowd possibly form a single file? A queue has a first member, a second, a third — but the rationals have no smallest positive member, no next fraction after one half, no natural anywhere-to-begin. Density seems to laugh at the very idea of a roster.

Density, sir, is bluffing — and here is the call. Forget, again, the order of the line; arrange the positive fractions instead in an infinite grid, a spreadsheet without edges. First row: everything over one — one over one, two over one, three over one, onward. Second row: everything over two. Third row: everything over three. And so on downward forever. Every positive fraction has a seat somewhere in this grid — the fraction p over q sits in row q, column p — the whole society, seated. Now march the grid. Not along a row, naturally — the first row alone would consume the entire queue. March the diagonals. Start in the corner: one over one. Then the little diagonal of two seats: two over one, one over two. Then the diagonal of three: three over one, two over two, one over three. Then the diagonal of four. Each diagonal is finite — that is the entire trick — so the march sweeps diagonal after diagonal, finishing each, and any seat you name, row q and column p, is reached when its diagonal comes up, at a perfectly finite position in the queue. Housekeeping: some fractions appear at many seats — two over two is one over one in disguise — so as we march, we simply skip every fraction already queued; and to gather in the negatives and zero, we interleave exactly as we did for the integers. The result stands, and I shall let it land plainly: the rationals are countable. The dense society — the crowd with no next-door neighbours, infinitely many members in every crack of the line — can be marched single file, every fraction at a finite address. Density and countability, which every fibre of intuition insists are opposites, turn out to be entirely compatible. Being everywhere, sir, is no obstacle to being listed. Your trade, if I may, has its own dialect for tonight's concept, and the translation may help it stick: a countable crowd is an enumerable one — a crowd for which a generator exists, an endless lazy stream in which any member you care about surfaces at some finite step. The zigzag is nothing but the classic enumeration of a product of two streams, the one every functional programmer eventually reinvents, and the duplicate-skipping is your filter. When tonight's great theorem arrives, then, hear it in dialect: it will assert the existence of a stream that no generator, of any design whatsoever, can produce. Not hard to produce, sir — impossible; and proving impossibility is the business of the dolls.

That is the first shock, and I want to bank its moral before the second, because the moral is the engine of everything until the murder. The grid-and-diagonal march is not a trick about fractions; it is a general instrument. Any infinite family of queues — countably many rows, each row itself a queue — can be merged into one master queue by marching the diagonals. Countably many countable crowds, pooled, are still one countable crowd; the desk states it as a theorem and you will prove it as the first of tonight's problems, with its solution supplied, and one honest footnote about a subtlety of choosing that I flag now and detail there. Feel the power of the instrument, sir, and feel where it drives the mind. The integers queue. The fractions queue. Infinite grids of queues collapse into queues. Every assault we mount ends with the crowd marching single file — and one begins to suspect, as the eighteen seventies began to suspect, that this is simply what infinity is like: that with enough zigzag ingenuity, everything queues; that infinite means countable, full stop, and the hotel has room for the world.

The real numbers are about to end that suspicion permanently.

The First Murder, by Russian Dolls

December, eighteen seventy-three. Cantor, twenty-eight years old, at his provincial post, writes to Dedekind. The question he poses sounds innocent even now: can the real numbers — all of them, the entire continuum — be arranged in a single file? Within the month he has the answer, hammered into shape across the correspondence, and in eighteen seventy-four it appears in print, in a paper whose title mentions only algebraic numbers — a modesty widely read as camouflage, sir, for reasons that will become clear when we discuss what certain colleagues in Berlin thought of this entire subject. The answer is no. The reals cannot be queued. And the proof — the original proof, seventeen years before the famous one — is a hunt, conducted with equipment you already own.

Here is the strategy, whole, in the ear; the desk performs it with full paperwork. We argue as we did for root two: suppose the thing and destroy it. Suppose, then, that someone hands you a single file of real numbers and claims it is complete — every real somewhere in the queue: a first real, a second, a third, and so on. Call them the first suspect, the second suspect, onward. We shall construct, with our own hands, a real number that appears nowhere in their queue — and the construction is a trap-building exercise. Take a closed interval that avoids the first suspect entirely; nothing easier — the first suspect is one point, and the line is generous; indeed, cut any closed interval into its three closed thirds, and the suspect, being a single point, can touch at most two of them, so at least one third is entirely clean of him. Now, inside that clean interval, play the same move against the second suspect: pass to a closed third that avoids him. Then against the third suspect, inside what remains. And so on, forever — each stage a closed interval, nested inside the last, chosen to exclude the next name on their list. Sir: closed, bounded, nested. The Russian dolls, assembled in anger. And last week's theorem — completeness itself, wearing the dolls; I ring the bell, the count stands at four — guarantees the trap is not empty: some real number survives inside every doll. Now interrogate the survivor. Can it be the first suspect? No — it lives in the first doll, which was built to exclude him. The second? No — the second doll excludes him. The nth suspect, for any n whatsoever? No — the nth doll saw to it. The survivor is a real number, and it is nowhere in the queue. The queue was advertised as complete; it is not; and since the argument consumed no particular feature of the list — any alleged complete queue whatsoever hands us the same trap and the same fugitive — no complete queue of the reals can exist. The real numbers are uncountable. There are at least two sizes of infinity, and the continuum is the larger.

Let the consequences land in order. First, last week's dangling question, now answerable in one breath: the rationals are countable — we queued them an act ago — and the reals are not; so the irrationals must be uncountable, for if they too were countable, the reals would be two countable crowds pooled, hence countable by the diagonal-march instrument, which they are not. The two interleaved societies, so symmetric to the eye, so mutually permeating, are of different orders of being. The rationals, dense and dazzling, are a countable film upon an uncountable line. The scale, as promised, crashes. Second, notice what the proof cost and where the power came from. Nothing about the argument is elaborate; the entire lethal force is supplied by one theorem — the dolls — which is to say by completeness, which is to say by the single axiom of last episode. In the rationals, remember, the dolls fail: nested closed rational intervals can strangle down onto a hole and trap nothing. And accordingly this proof, run inside the rationals, dies — as it must, since the rationals do queue. Uncountability is not a fact about crowds in general, sir. It is a fact about the completed line specifically; the fugitive the trap catches is exactly the kind of resident that completeness installed. Cantor's first great theorem is our axiom's fourth bell, and I find the accounting deeply satisfying. One further clause, obtainable free of charge because the hunt is local: nothing obliged us to begin with the whole line. Start the trap inside any interval you please — however microscopic — and the argument runs unchanged, producing a fugitive there. Every interval of the line, down to the smallest, is itself uncountable; the continuum has no thin districts where the crowd runs sparse. File the clause, sir; the census act will spend it.

And third — the historical beat, which is also a corrective, since the folklore has it backwards. This, the dolls, the hunt, is how uncountability was actually first proved. The celebrated argument — the one you may have met in a popular book, the one with the diagonal — came seventeen years later, in eighteen ninety-one, as a second proof of an old theorem: shorter, stranger, and so portable that it escaped mathematics entirely and colonized philosophy and computer science. It is the next act, and it is the set piece of the night.

The Diagonal

Eighteen ninety-one. Cantor, by now the founder of a subject and a battered veteran of its wars, publishes a paper of astonishing brevity in which the uncountability of the continuum is proved again — differently, and, it must be said, immortally. I shall give you the argument in full, from first principles, presuming nothing. It is four moves long. Attend, sir; this is the wonder I budgeted for tonight, and I intend to spend it properly.

Strip the problem to its skeleton. Real numbers, decimal expansions, all of that is dressing — what the argument concerns is endless sequences of two symbols. Picture an infinite record of coin flips: heads, tails, tails, heads, and so on forever, one entry for every position, first, second, third, without end. Cantor's own paper used two letters of the alphabet; we shall use coins. The collection under study is the collection of all such endless flip-records — every conceivable eternal sequence of heads and tails. The claim: this collection cannot be marched single file. No queue contains them all.

Suppose it did. Here is a queue, says the adversary — record one, record two, record three, and so on, every endless flip-sequence allegedly somewhere on the roster. Picture the claim as a table, sir, infinite in both directions: one row per record, one column per position, each cell a head or a tail; every conceivable eternal sequence, says the adversary, appears as some row. Our entire attack will consist of reading one cell per row — the first cell of the first row, the second of the second, the nth of the nth — the cells running down the table's main diagonal; whence the argument's immortal name. Very well. We shall now write down, in front of the adversary, a flip-record of our own, and we shall build it by consulting his queue — one glance per entry, straight down the diagonal. For the first flip of our record: look at the first flip of his first record — and write the opposite. His first record starts heads? Ours starts tails. For our second flip: the second flip of his second record, reversed. For our third: the third flip of his third, reversed. In general, sir — and this is the whole machine — the nth flip of our record is the opposite of the nth flip of his nth record. The recipe is completely explicit; every entry of our record is determined; it is a perfectly lawful, fully specified, endless sequence of heads and tails. So, since his queue allegedly contains all of them, it must contain ours. Where? At position one? No — our record differs from his first record at the very first flip; we built it so. Position two? No — it differs from his second record at the second flip. Position n, for any n the adversary cares to name? No, sir: our record disagrees with his nth record at the nth flip, by construction, always, without exception. Our sequence differs from every sequence on his roster — each one contradicted at the diagonal — and therefore appears nowhere on it. The roster is incomplete. And since we never used anything about which roster it was — hand us any alleged complete queue and the same diagonal recipe manufactures its fugitive on the spot — no complete queue exists. The endless flip-records are uncountable. Four moves: list, diagonal, flip, contradiction.

Sit with it a moment, sir, because the shape of this thing repays staring. Notice, first, its total indifference to content. The eighteen seventy-four proof needed the real line — closed intervals, completeness, the dolls; honest geometry of the continuum. The diagonal needs nothing. No completeness, no order, no arithmetic — only the bare grammar of lists and the ability to say opposite. It is a proof about tables: any table whose rows are endless sequences fails to contain the sequence built by inverting its own diagonal. That is why it travels, and tonight you will watch it emigrate. Notice, second, the peculiar jiu-jitsu of it: the adversary's own list is the raw material of his defeat. The completeness of the roster is precisely what lets us consult row n at column n; the more thoroughly he has listed, the more thoroughly we contradict. Every attempt at a complete queue carries, in its own diagonal, the seed of its refutation — the argument does not so much attack the list as make the list attack itself. There is nothing else quite like it in the armoury, sir; Episode One's parity proof killed one conjecture with one contradiction, but the diagonal is a factory for contradictions, one custom-built fugitive per candidate roster, delivered mechanically, forever. And notice, third, what the bridge back to the line looks like, because I owe you that plank: an endless flip-record is, in all but costume, an address — and later tonight, when a certain dust is born, you will see each flip-record steer a nest of shrinking dolls, left or right at every stage, down onto exactly one point of the line. The uncountable crowd of flip-records will be found standing, entire, inside the line — indeed inside a set you can draw. The desk lays that plank with full care; the picture to keep is that heads-and-tails eternity and the continuum are, size-wise, the same beast.

One more turn of the same key, and then I shall stop gilding — but this one opens the largest door in the subject. Cantor's little eighteen ninety-one paper does not stop at flip-records; it observes that the argument generalizes, and the generalization is now called Cantor's theorem: take any crowd whatsoever — any set — and consider the crowd of all its subcollections, every possible way of selecting some of its members. That second crowd always, provably, outnumbers the first: no set can be danced with the collection of its own subsets; the diagonal manufactures the fugitive subset exactly as it manufactured the fugitive flip-record — the desk has the four lines. And now watch what follows, sir. Start with the whole numbers. The crowd of their subsets is strictly bigger — that crowd, the desk will show you, is precisely the continuum's size. Take subsets again: bigger still. And again: bigger again. There is no top. Infinity is not one size, not two sizes: it is an endless ascending ladder of sizes, each dwarfing the last, manufactured by a four-line argument that fits in the ear of a man out walking. Mathematicians before eighteen seventy-four had one infinity and were nervous about that. Cantor left them infinitely many infinities, rigorously distinguished. I have spent my budgeted wonder, sir, and I regret nothing.

The Same Blade on Your Own Hardware

Now the emigration I promised — three minutes, self-contained, and I suspect, sir, the most personally addressed three minutes of the season. The diagonal argument, I said, is a proof about tables, indifferent to subject matter. In nineteen thirty-six, a young Englishman named Alan Turing built a table whose rows are computer programs, ran Cantor's move on it, and proved a permanent, load-bearing fact about your profession: there are questions about programs that no program can ever decide.

Set the table. A program — this is the only observation needed, and it is Turing's — is a finite text: a finite string of symbols from a finite keyboard. And finite texts can be queued: list them by length, and alphabetically within each length, exactly as the desk has you queue finite strings tonight. So all programs form a single file — program one, program two, program three; a countable crowd, every program at a finite address. Now the table: rows are programs, columns are inputs — and since inputs are finite texts too, think of the entry in row n, column m as the answer to the question does program n, run on input m, ever halt — or does it churn forever? A perfectly definite table of yes and no, infinite in both directions. And the question on trial: could there exist a master program — call it the oracle — which, handed any program and any input, correctly announces halts or runs forever, always, in finite time? Every working engineer has wanted this program by Thursday afternoon, sir. It would end whole categories of grief.

Suppose the oracle exists. We now build, with the oracle as a component, a saboteur — a short, entirely legal program, and I shall specify it exactly. The saboteur takes one input: a number, n. On receiving n, it consults the oracle with the diagonal question: does program n, run on its own index n as input, halt? — row n, column n; the diagonal cell. And then the saboteur does the opposite of whatever the oracle reports. Oracle says program n halts on n? The saboteur deliberately enters an endless loop. Oracle says it runs forever? The saboteur halts at once, smugly. That is the whole program: consult the diagonal, invert it. But the saboteur is itself a program — a finite text — so it sits somewhere in the single file of all programs; it has an index; call it s. And now, sir, the trap closes exactly as it closed on the flip-records: run the saboteur on its own index s. Does it halt? If it halts, then the oracle, being correct, told it that program s halts on s — whereupon, by its own explicit code, the saboteur looped forever. If it loops forever, the oracle said loops — whereupon the saboteur halted. Either way the saboteur does the opposite of what it does. The contradiction is total, and only one supposition stood in the chain: the oracle. No such oracle can exist. The halting problem is undecidable — not unsolved, sir; unsolvable, by any program, on any hardware, in any century. And the plague spreads, by the same infection route, to essentially every interesting question about what programs do rather than how they read: whether one program is equivalent to another, whether a program ever misbehaves on any input, whether the dependency you did not write does anything untoward — each undecidable in general, each proof a re-costumed saboteur. This, sir, is the deep reason testing never became proving, and why your profession's tooling forever approximates, sandboxes, and hedges: the diagonal stands permanently in the road.

Look at what you just watched, because it is not an analogy and I want that plainly on the record: it is the same argument. A countable roster — of programs now, not flip-records. A diagonal — each program confronted with its own index. An inversion — the saboteur flipping the oracle's verdict. A fugitive that the roster cannot contain — here, the behaviour the saboteur exhibits, which no oracle-equipped row can predict. List, diagonal, flip, contradiction: Cantor's four moves, running on silicon. The limits of computation were discovered, sir, with a weapon forged in eighteen ninety-one for weighing infinities — forged, if you trace our season's accounts, in the service of Fourier's heat problem; there is a straight line, and I mean a straight line, from a warm iron rod to the theorem that your build system cannot, in general, be verified by another program. And Turing's is the second member of a family of three: five years earlier, in nineteen thirty-one, Kurt Gödel had run a cousin of the same diagonal through the arithmetic of statements about statements, and proved that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains truths it cannot prove. I name-check the incompleteness theorem tonight and decline to elaborate, sir — it deserves better than a cameo, and it lies off our road. The point on our road is this: Cantor's diagonal is one of the very few ideas in history that fundamentally rearranged three subjects — mathematics, logic, and computation — using four lines. When we admire it, we are not being sentimental. We are reading the invoice.

The Census of Numbers

Back to the line itself, sir, for the finding that made the eighteen seventy-four paper quietly explosive — the corollary Cantor placed in the title while hiding the bomb in the body.

A definition first, spoken with care. Call a number algebraic if it is the root of some polynomial equation with whole-number coefficients — if it answers some finite piece of schoolroom algebra. The club is vast and it contains, I would wager, every number you have ever named by algebraic means. Every fraction belongs — p over q answers a first-degree equation with whole coefficients. Root two belongs — it answers x squared equals two; that it answers no fraction was Episode One's scandal, but it answers algebra perfectly well. The golden ratio belongs — x squared equals x plus one. Every nested radical you can build, every root of every quintic that defeats the solving formulas — still algebraic; the equation exists even where the formula does not. The algebraic numbers are, in effect, everything finitely describable by the oldest language in mathematics. A number that is not algebraic — that answers no whole-coefficient equation of any degree whatsoever — is called transcendental: beyond the reach of algebra entirely.

Do transcendental numbers exist at all? Notice how hard the question is by direct assault: to certify one specimen, you must prove a negative against every polynomial of every degree — infinitely many candidate equations, all shown to miss. It was done, first, by main force: in eighteen forty-four Joseph Liouville constructed a number deliberately engineered to be approximated by fractions too well — suspiciously well — and proved that algebraic numbers cannot tolerate such flattery; his creature was the first certified transcendental, one specimen, laboriously exhibited — and describable in one breath, sir: a decimal that is zero at every position except the first, the second, the sixth, the twenty-fourth, and so on through the factorial positions, where it carries a one; the ones thin out with such violence that fractions flatter the number far too well for any algebraic citizen to bear. In eighteen seventy-three Hermite — with what he described as painful effort — proved the number e, the base of natural growth, transcendental. In eighteen eighty-two Lindemann extended Hermite's method to pi, and thereby closed, with a single theorem, the oldest open problem in geometry: squaring the circle with ruler and compass is impossible, because every ruler-and-compass length is algebraic and pi is not. Two thousand years of circle-squarers refuted in one line; the cranks, I am told, were not deterred. And there, essentially, the specimen cabinet stops. Pi, the number e, Liouville's engineered curiosities, and a modest catalogue of relatives: after which the educated party guest begins to stammer. Humanity can scarcely name a handful of transcendentals.

Now the census, which requires no cabinet at all. The algebraic numbers, I claim, are countable — they queue. The argument is tonight's grid instrument, sir, running at full power: a polynomial with whole-number coefficients is a finite description — a finite string over a finite keyboard — and finite descriptions queue, exactly as programs queued an act ago; each polynomial, moreover, owns at most finitely many roots — a fact of algebra which the desk takes honestly on credit with references, one of only two credits tonight. So the algebraic numbers are a countable file of finite clusters: countable, by the pooling theorem you will prove at the desk. Every number algebra can ever describe fits in a single queue. And the reals do not. Subtract, and hold on to something: the transcendentals are what remain when a countable film is lifted off an uncountable line — and uncountable minus countable is uncountable, by a two-line lemma at the desk. The transcendentals are not rare, sir. They are not even merely infinite. They are the overwhelming totality of the continuum: almost every real number, in the exact size-sense of tonight, transcends algebra altogether — and we can scarcely name three. The numbers we can describe — rationals, radicals, roots, every constant with a name and a biography — form a countable dusting upon a line whose typical resident is anonymous, indescribable, and utterly beyond the reach of any equation. The nameable is the exception, sir. The anonymous is the rule. And if you doubt the word indescribable, tonight has already armed you to make it literal. Fix any language you please — English, mathematics, any notation over a finite alphabet. Its finite texts form a countable queue; we marched exactly such a queue in the halting act. So the numbers that any fixed language can single out by finite description — each text nominating at most one number — form at best a countable crowd, and uncountably many reals answer to no description in that language, nor in any fixed language whatsoever: not merely unnamed, sir, but unnameable, permanently, for want of enough sentences in the universe. (The desk appends a logician's small asterisk to that argument's fine print; the moral survives it intact.) I know of no single theorem that so thoroughly inverts one's picture of what the number line contains.

And mark the method on the invoice, for it is about to become a casus belli. Cantor's eighteen seventy-four paper proves that transcendentals exist — indeed, that every interval, however small, teems with uncountably many of them — while exhibiting not one. No specimen, no construction, no Liouville labour: a census of two crowds, a subtraction, and the conclusion that the unexhibited must not merely exist but predominate. You have met this sensation before, sir — the starred problem of Chapter One, where two candidates prove that an irrational power of an irrational can be rational, and the proof declines to say which candidate works. Existence without exhibition, now at industrial scale. To most of the profession this was a revelation about the power of pure reason. To one man in Berlin it was the corruption of mathematics itself, and his response is the penultimate act of tonight's story.

The Arithmetic of the Infinite, and the Question That Broke

Cantor did not stop at two sizes; he built an arithmetic for them, and I shall give you the taste the plan promises — enough to fix the landscape, no more.

He gave the countable size a name: aleph nought — aleph, the first letter of the Hebrew alphabet, the first infinite number in history to be formally christened. And the striking discovery is how much abuse aleph nought absorbs without changing. Add one guest: aleph nought — the hotel. Add a whole countable crowd: aleph nought — interleave the queues. Multiply, even: a countable grid of countable rows is still one countable queue — the zigzag was precisely this fact in action. Addition and multiplication slide off the infinite like rain off oilcloth; the arithmetic of sizes is magnificently boring at the bottom of the ladder. What finally moves the needle — the only thing, at this level, that does — is the operation the diagonal governs: passing to all subcollections. The subsets of the whole numbers form a strictly larger crowd — Cantor's theorem — and here is the identification that ties tonight into one knot: a subset of the whole numbers is nothing but an endless yes-no record — for each number, in or out — which is to say, a flip-record. The crowd of subsets of the whole numbers is exactly the crowd of endless coin-flip sequences, and the desk will persuade you, with two injections and a starred theorem, that this crowd is precisely the size of the continuum itself. The real line, weighed exactly: two raised to the aleph nought — the size of the whole numbers' powerhouse of subsets. The ladder then climbs by the same rung forever: subsets of the line, larger still; subsets of those, larger again; no summit.

Which leaves a question so natural that Cantor asked it in essentially his first breath, and it destroyed him. Between the bottom rung and the continuum — between aleph nought and two to the aleph nought — is there anything? Does some crowd of reals exist that is strictly bigger than the whole numbers and strictly smaller than the line? Cantor believed the answer is no — that the continuum is the very next size after the countable, no intermediate sizes, no gap — and this belief is the continuum hypothesis. He pursued a proof for decades: announced one, withdrew it, announced the opposite, withdrew that; the problem sat at the exact centre of his subject and would not open. In nineteen hundred, when Hilbert drew up his celebrated list of the twenty-three problems that should occupy the coming century, the continuum hypothesis was problem number one.

The resolution, when the twentieth century delivered it, is unlike anything before it in this course, and I shall spend on it exactly the one honest paragraph I promised, no rabbit hole. In nineteen forty, Kurt Gödel — the diagonal family again — proved that the continuum hypothesis cannot be disproved from the standard axioms of set theory: he constructed a mathematical universe obeying every axiom in which the hypothesis holds. In nineteen sixty-three, Paul Cohen proved it cannot be proved: by a new method called forcing, before which I bow and pass on, he constructed universes obeying every axiom in which it fails. Put the halves together: the standard axioms of mathematics — the ones underwriting everything in this course and everything you compute — are simply silent on the question. Both the hypothesis and its negation are consistent with them, if they are consistent at all. Understand precisely what that does and does not mean, sir. It does not mean the answer is unknown, awaiting a cleverer generation. It means the question, as posed to those axioms, has no answer — the axioms underdetermine the universe of sets, and to settle the matter one must adopt new axioms, about whose truth thoughtful mathematicians disagree to this day, some doubting that truth is even the right word so far from the shore. What would settle it, if anything, is the adoption of new axioms — candidates exist, chiefly concerning infinities vastly larger than any met tonight — and the debate over them is live, technical, and mercifully not our department. Cantor spent his health on a question that his own axioms, unbeknownst to anyone, could never decide. There is the door, sir, clearly marked; we note its existence, and we do not go through. Analysis lives on this side of it, entirely unbothered — a fact worth one grateful sentence before we move on.

The Dust Is Born

Now the promised birth — a recurring character, contracted through both seasons, and the strangest object the course has yet constructed. It costs one closed interval and a pair of scissors.

Take the closed unit interval, zero to one, endpoints included. Remove its open middle third — everything strictly between one third and two thirds, the endpoints one third and two thirds themselves spared. Two closed intervals survive: zero to one third, and two thirds to one. Now do it again to each survivor: remove each one's open middle third, leaving four closed intervals, each one ninth long. Again: eight intervals, each one twenty-seventh long. And so on, forever — at every stage, every surviving interval loses its open middle third. The dust, sir — officially the Cantor set, from an eighteen eighty-three paper — is what survives the entire infinite cutting: the points that are never, at any stage, removed.

Is anything left at all? Certainly: the cutting endpoints. Zero and one are never touched; one third and two thirds were spared by the very first cut and sit at the edges of survivors forever after; likewise one ninth, two ninths, and the whole growing family of boundary points. All such endpoints survive — and they are a countable crowd, sir, since each is born at some finite stage of a march we could queue. So the lazy conclusion offers itself: the dust is just the cutting debris, a countable sprinkle of endpoints, infinite but tame. The lazy conclusion is wrong by an entire order of infinity, and the correct one is tonight's threads braiding shut. To locate a grain of dust, notice, is to give an itinerary. At the first cut, the survivors form a left house and a right house: which did your grain occupy? Left, say. That house's own cut splits it again: left or right? At every stage, exactly one binary decision — and an endless sequence of them: left, right, right, left, forever. An itinerary is an endless two-symbol record. It is a flip-record, sir — the very crowd the diagonal weighed. And every itinerary is honoured by exactly one grain: the chosen houses form a nest of closed intervals, each inside the last, widths shrinking by thirds — beneath any tolerance, since powers of three outgrow every threshold — and that, for the second time tonight, is the Russian dolls in their unique-prisoner configuration. The bell, sir, a fifth time. Each endless itinerary traps precisely one point of the dust; different itineraries part ways at some stage into houses separated by a removed gap, so different itineraries trap different grains. The grains of the dust and the endless flip-records dance perfectly — and the flip-records are uncountable, by the diagonal, proved not two acts ago. The dust is uncountable. That scatter of surviving points is exactly as numerous as the entire continuum — and since the endpoints number only countably many, almost every grain of the dust is not an endpoint at all: the typical grain is an anonymous survivor, named by no stage of the construction, reachable only by an infinite itinerary. The debris is the minority. The dust is mostly ghosts. On paper, incidentally, the itinerary wears an arithmetical costume that the desk fits properly: write numbers in base three, and the survivors of the cutting are exactly those possessing an expansion that avoids the middle digit — every left turn a nought, every right turn a two, the forbidden middle third being precisely where the middle digit lives. The same address book in a different binding, minding the one usual nuisance of numbers that own two costumes.

And now the other jaw of the paradox. For all its uncountable multitude, the dust contains no interval whatsoever — not one unbroken stretch of line, however short. For suppose it contained a stretch of some definite positive width. At every stage n, the dust lies inside the stage's survivors — a scattering of closed intervals each of width one over three to the n — and a connected stretch cannot straddle two survivors, since between any two survivors lies a removed gap, which the stretch would have to contain, absurdly. So the stretch would have to fit inside a single survivor at every stage — its width undercut by one third, one ninth, one twenty-seventh, smaller than every tolerance — and a positive width smaller than every tolerance is no width at all, by the little slack remark that has been quietly closing arguments since Chapter Two. No interval, then. Uncountably many grains — a continuum's worth — yet between any two of them, somewhere, a void; no grain owns a neighbourhood; the set is all points and no fabric. And yet — file this, sir, for it becomes a formal virtue in two episodes — no grain is lonely, either: arbitrarily close to any grain lie other grains, its itinerary-siblings, differing only at late stages. Crowded everywhere in the small; connected nowhere in the large. Uncountable, and dust. And one last property for the connoisseur, free with tonight's purchase: the dust is made of smaller dusts. Look only at the grains inside the left-hand first-stage house, from zero to one third, and you are looking at a perfect one-third-scale replica of the entire dust — the same construction, shrunk — and inside it, replicas again, at every depth, forever. The dust is built of copies of itself all the way down, which is either deeply satisfying or deeply unsettling; I have found the two populations of listener impossible to predict.

The christening, then, and the paperwork. The Cantor set — the dust, as I shall familiarly call it — is hereby a recurring character of this course, and I enter its contracts in the ledger aloud. It returns in Episode Five, where the topological vocabulary we build there will reveal it as a one-set paradox lounge — I shall say no more. It returns again in Season Two, stranger still. And there is a third entry, sir, which I make with the envelope deliberately sealed: you are, at this very moment, tempted to ask a certain question about the dust — the most natural question in the world, the one a child would ask, involving the total extent of what the scissors removed and what remains. I decline to hint at the answer. I decline, in fact, to confirm the question is well posed. It is the opening scandal of Season Two, it is worth the wait, and a gentleman does not read other people's mail even when he wrote it. Three contracts filed. The dust will keep every one.

Paradise and Its Enemies

I owe you, before the accounts, the human ledger — and I shall keep the undertaking made in Episode One: precision about the conduct, restraint about the suffering.

The enemy of the piece is Leopold Kronecker of Berlin: a first-rate mathematician — that must be said plainly, and I say it — algebraist of genuine power, wealthy, formidable, and one of Cantor's own former teachers. Kronecker's creed you heard in Episode One: God made the whole numbers; all else is the work of man. He held that mathematics is the finite manipulation of the finite; that completed infinities are theology in costume; and that an existence proof which exhibits nothing — tonight's census, above all — is not a proof but a confession. I want to be scrupulously fair, sir: as philosophy, the position is serious, it has able descendants to this day among the constructivists, and a course built on the honesty clause is obliged to note that Kronecker was not a fool. He was, however — and here fairness ends — a man who prosecuted a philosophical disagreement as a personal campaign against a younger, weaker colleague. Not all of Berlin stood with him — Weierstrass, to his credit, is recorded as receiving the countability of the algebraic numbers with warm interest and lending his considerable weight to publication. Kronecker wielded his editorial power at the great journal of the day to delay and obstruct Cantor's papers; he stood, immovable, between Cantor and any appointment in Berlin, leaving him marooned for life at a provincial university; and the reported epithets — secondhand, sir, but consistent across the sources — include renegade, charlatan, and corrupter of youth. The position was defensible. The conduct was not. Genius, as I observed once before in another connection, does not excuse it.

Of Cantor's side of those years I shall say three sentences, as promised. His health broke first in eighteen eighty-four, and repeatedly thereafter; the continuum hypothesis and the persecution are both traditionally blamed, though illness, I suspect, keeps its own schedule and needs no villain. He worked on, between collapses, for decades. He died in January of nineteen eighteen, in care, at seventy-two — and that is where a gentleman stops.

What wants remembering instead is the other column of the ledger. Dedekind's friendship, first — the Interlaken thread, the letters that received and sharpened the great proofs, a correspondence conducted with a loyalty the profession around them did not always show. And then the verdict of history, which arrived with unusual speed and unusual unanimity. Within a generation, Cantor's theory of sets had become the accepted foundation of all mathematics — the very axioms whose fine print we discussed two acts ago are its direct descendants, and every object in this course, from Dedekind's cuts to next season's measures, is officially a set, living in Cantor's house. Kronecker died in eighteen ninety-one — the year of the diagonal, as it happens — and his campaign died with him; the finitist philosophy survives, respectably, as a school; the obstruction is remembered as a disgrace. And in nineteen twenty-six, Hilbert — by then the most authoritative voice in the subject — pronounced the sentence that stands as the era's verdict: no one shall expel us from the paradise which Cantor created. It is worth setting beside it the quieter sentence Cantor himself had written back in eighteen eighty-three, in the thick of the campaign, which you will recognize as the season's recurring creed — Dedekind said it of numbers, Cantor now of everything: the essence of mathematics lies precisely in its freedom. The paradise was not found, sir. Like the real numbers themselves, it was built — and the man who built it was billed for it, in full, and paid.

Terms of Engagement

The desk's half of tonight, chapter three, toured at the established brisk pace.

The notation ledger pairs tonight's speech with its symbols: the perfect dance acquires its arrow and its official name; countable and uncountable their definitions; aleph nought its written form; the power set — the crowd of subcollections — its calligraphy; the algebraic numbers their formal membership card. The chapter's proofs in full are tonight's spine, formalized: the zigzag through the rationals, made rigorous with the duplicate-skipping handled honestly; the eighteen seventy-four hunt with complete paperwork — the closed-thirds trick by which each suspect is evicted, and the dolls' verdict cited to Chapter Two, bell and all; the diagonal itself, four lines on two-symbol sequences, exactly as narrated; Cantor's theorem in its full generality — the fugitive subset assembled from the members that decline their own invitations, the desk's most elegant four lines; and the dust's address system, built — I flag this with some pride, sir — using no infinite sums whatsoever, only nested dolls with shrinking widths, so that not a single fact is borrowed against Episode Four; the infinite series you might have expected waits for its license. One credit only is taken, boxed and referenced: that a polynomial of degree n has at most n roots, for the census — and nothing else; even the dust's addressing is built series-free. The uncountable-minus-countable lemma is stated where the census needs it, and its two lines are left to you. The continuum hypothesis receives a sober formal statement and a paragraph of references for the door we are not entering.

The problems. Tier one, Calisthenics: dance drills — verify the integers' zigzag formula does what I claimed; pair the evens, pair two arbitrary intervals by a straight-line formula, and pair a bounded open interval with the entire line by a purely algebraic map, no calculus required; queue the finite binary strings by length and alphabet; compute the first dozen entries of the rational zigzag with duplicates skipped, an exercise in honest bookkeeping. Tier two, The Real Thing, six items: the pooling theorem — countably many countable crowds merge into one — proved in full with the solution supplied, including the honest footnote that selecting a queue for each crowd involves infinitely many acts of choice, a subtlety with a famous future in Season Two; the census completed — the algebraic numbers queued by the height of their equations, solution likewise supplied; the diagonal run directly on decimal expansions, where a certain notorious ambiguity of representation — the nine-repeating disease — must be dodged, and the hint shows the standard vaccination; the theorem that every infinite crowd, of any size, contains a countable file, with the same choosing subtlety flagged again; the uncountable-minus-countable lemma, yours in two lines; and the hotel manoeuvre performed on the line itself — a bijection between the open and the closed unit interval, two extra guests absorbed by an infinite shift. Tier three, For the Ambitious, two stars. First, the theorem of Cantor, Schröder, and Bernstein: if each of two crowds injects into the other — each dances with a part of the other — then they dance outright; the theorem everyone in mathematics uses daily and almost no one has proved — a statement so plausible it feels administrative, and a proof with a genuine idea in it — delivered as a guided ascent in stages, hints only. Second, the identification I asserted in the arithmetic act: the real line and the subsets of the whole numbers are the same size — two injections, one in each direction through binary and ternary expansions, minding the ambiguity, and then the first star closes the loop. Complete it, sir, and you will have personally weighed the continuum.

Solutions policy as standing: tier one complete and terse, tier two selected — the two that finish audio arguments — tier three hints alone. Listen, then work. And the digest, of course, opens the chapter, per our amended arrangement: tonight retold beat by beat, for the shelf.

Until Next Time

The ledger, sir. Newly entered tonight: the dust, with three contracts — Episode Five, Season Two, and one sealed envelope concerning a question I have declined to name. The bell of completeness rang twice this hour — once inside the dolls of eighteen seventy-four, once trapping grains of dust — and the running count stands at five; the axiom is earning its keep. Standing accounts unchanged: Fourier's instalments, the monsters inbound for Episodes Six through Eight, the swap memorandum — and one account quietly gained a second entry tonight: existence without exhibition, opened by a starred problem in Chapter One, now holds the census as well; both fall due together in Season Two, Episode Three, when the axiom of choice presents the family bill. And one account, sir, moves to the top of the pile: Zeno's, the oldest on our books, opened in the first hour and due — at last — next episode.

For next time is the payment: the limit, defined. The hardest small thing in mathematics, the definition that took humanity twenty-two centuries and that undergraduates are expected to absorb in a fortnight — we shall take our time instead, and learn it as an adversarial game: a skeptic proposing tolerances, we answering with thresholds, the contest that becomes the grammar of everything that follows. Achilles will be discharged with the paperwork in order. A fourteenth-century French scholar will prove, beautifully, that a series everyone expects to settle instead climbs forever. The bounded climb from Episode Two — the one that dies in the rationals — will be licensed at last, completeness ringing like a fire alarm. And the hour will end with the season's first true outrage: a convergent series, sir, with a definite sum, which — merely by taking the same terms in a different order — can be made to add up to any number you care to name. Addition, infinitely extended, is not commutative. Bring a seatbelt.

I remain, as ever, in perfect bijection with my duties — every obligation paired, none left over.
