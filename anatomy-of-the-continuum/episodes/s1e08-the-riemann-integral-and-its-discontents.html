The Riemann Integral and Its Discontents

The Anatomy of the Continuum, Season One, Episode Eight

The Summit in View

Good day, sir.

Tonight we arrive. Seven episodes ago this course promised you a rebuilding of the calculus from bedrock, and the promissory schedule has been honoured item by item: numbers made honest in Episode Two, infinity weighed in Three, limits tamed in Four, the line's geography surveyed in Five, continuity earned in Six, the derivative defined and its monsters loosed in Seven. One summit remains, and it is the one the whole classical edifice was scaffolding for: the integral. Area, rendered honest at last.

I shall tell you now, plainly, how the evening runs, because a season finale owes its audience a map. In the first hour of the climb we build the Riemann integral properly — upper sums, lower sums, the machinery of honest area — and the bell of completeness, which has kept a dignified silence through whole stretches of this season, will ring at the very foundation. Twice, in fact; I have counted ahead. At the summit stands the fundamental theorem of calculus, both parts, proved — the reconciliation of the two calculi, the theorem your entire mathematical life has been spending unexamined. And then, sir, because this course does not deal in false bottoms, we read the fine print. The static will walk into the machine and defeat it. Thomae's creature will walk in behind it and pass, and the contrast will open a vocabulary crisis that this season cannot close. A moving spike will draw blood from the swap account. And a quiet Italian will show us that the fundamental theorem itself — tonight's triumph — is holed below the waterline. The season ends on a cliffhanger, and I make no apology: it is the honest ending, and the door it leaves open is the door Season Two walks through.

The summit first. The cracks after. That is the order the history took, and it is the right order dramatically as well.

Area on Trial

Begin with the oldest question in the file. What is the area under a curve?

The ancients answered it for particular curves, and answered it magnificently. You will recall from our first evening the method of exhaustion — Eudoxus's discipline, Archimedes's weapon — polygons marshalled inside a curved region and polygons marshalled outside, the two families squeezed toward one another until the region's area had nowhere left to hide. Archimedes measured the parabola that way, and the circle, and he did it with a rigour that would pass this course's standards essentially unamended. Then the method was lost to Europe for the better part of two thousand years, and when the seventeenth century rediscovered quantitative appetite, it chose speed over rigour — as we heard in Episode One, and as Bishop Berkeley so profitably noticed.

Here is the scandal in one sentence: for a century and a half after Newton and Leibniz, the working definition of the integral was the antiderivative. To integrate a function was to exhibit another function whose derivative it was, and the area interpretation rode along on geometric faith. It worked — heavens, it worked; the eighteenth century computed its way across the sciences on exactly this arrangement. But notice what the arrangement assumes: that every function one meets has an antiderivative to exhibit, and that area means something in the first place. Both assumptions, sir, are loans, and neither had been underwritten.

The man who called the loans was, of all people, Fourier — the standing creditor of this entire course. His scandalous claim of eighteen oh seven, that any profile of heat may be written as a sum of waves, forced the question: what does the integral of an arbitrary function even mean? The coefficients of Fourier's series are integrals; if arbitrary profiles are admitted, arbitrary functions must be integrated; the antiderivative arrangement collapses on the spot, because a jagged, patched-together profile of heat has no antiderivative anyone can exhibit. Cauchy made the first honest answer in eighteen twenty-three, defining the integral of a continuous function as a limit of finite sums and proving the limit exists. And in eighteen fifty-four, a shy young man named Bernhard Riemann, writing the qualifying lecture that German custom demanded — his subject was precisely the representation of functions by trigonometric series, Fourier's question in formal dress — paused in the middle of that enterprise to ask the modern question outright. Not which respectable functions may be integrated, but: what is the exact class of functions for which the integral makes sense at all? Where, precisely, is the boundary of the concept?

Mark the shape of that question, sir. It is the question this season has taught you to ask. Not "does the tool work on the examples I like" but "what is the tool's exact jurisdiction" — the question Cantor asked of counting, the question Weierstrass asked of continuity. And mark also the bookkeeping: Riemann built his integral inside a memoir on Fourier series. The very machinery we erect tonight is an instalment on the Fourier debt — the account opened in our first hour, the largest on the books. The register notes the payment: the integral itself, built in Fourier's service. Debt three is not discharged tonight — it runs to the end of Season Two and in candour beyond — but tonight's entire apparatus is interest paid upon it.

One more name and the history is seated. Riemann's own formulation used tagged sums — sample points chosen inside each slice, a device your numerical trade still runs on daily. Twenty years later, in eighteen seventy-five, Gaston Darboux tidied the whole account into the form we shall actually build: no sample points, no choices, just suprema and infima doing the work. The two formulations agree exactly — the desk states the equivalence, and one direction of it is among tonight's problems. I build Darboux's version because it is cleaner in the ear and because, sir, it wears its dependence on completeness openly, where I can ring bells at it.

Cages and Bricks

Let the function f live on a closed bounded interval from a to b, and let it be bounded there — I shall say in a moment why boundedness is stamped on the ticket at the door. The construction has three moves, and the first is the only one with any cunning in it.

Move one: partition. Chop the interval from a to b into finitely many slices by choosing waypoints — a itself first, then finitely many strictly increasing stations, ending at b. A partition is nothing but a finite itinerary. Coarse partitions have a few fat slices; fine partitions have many thin ones; and one partition refines another when it keeps every waypoint of the first and adds new ones of its own. Chopping finer, note, is the picket fence's trade from Episode Two plied at a new stall — density of stations wherever we please, whenever we please.

Move two: on each slice, ask the function two questions. How high do you reach here, and how low? Not "what value do you take at the left endpoint" — no sampling, no favourites. On each slice, take the supremum of the function's values over that slice, and the infimum.

And there, sir — before anything is summed, before area is so much as mentioned — the bell rings. Ring the thirteenth. On what authority do those two numbers exist? The function's values over a slice form a bounded, nonempty set of reals, and a bounded nonempty set of reals has a least upper bound and a greatest lower bound because the completeness axiom says so, and for no other reason. Over the rationals the same construction dies in its crib — a perfectly respectable function on rational inputs can have values crowding toward a ceiling that simply is not there to be named. Every brick in tonight's building, sir, every single slice-height in every sum we are about to write, is manufactured by the axiom of completeness. Episode Two told you the axiom was the load-bearing wall of the season; tonight it is not the wall, it is the brickworks. That is ring thirteen, and the ceremony is brief because the bell has further engagements this evening. You will notice also why boundedness was stamped on the ticket: an unbounded function has slices with no ceiling at all, no supremum for the axiom to deliver, and the construction cannot start. The unbounded cases are handled by an extension — improper integrals, the desk notes their existence and their dangers — but the honest core is bounded functions on closed bounded intervals, and tonight we stay in the honest core.

Move three: sum. On each slice, erect the rectangle whose height is the slice's supremum, and add up the areas: width of slice times ceiling height, summed across the itinerary. That number is the upper sum for this partition — the cost of caging the function from above with this particular itinerary. Do the same with the infima and you have the lower sum — the yield of packing rectangles under the function from below. For any partition whatsoever, the lower sum cannot exceed the upper sum; on each slice the floor is at most the ceiling, and sums of smaller things are smaller. One family of numbers cages the region from outside; one family fills it from inside. Exhaustion, sir — Eudoxus's discipline, Archimedes's weapon — returned after two millennia wearing modern dress, with the completeness axiom now supplying what Greek geometry had to assume.

The Two Ledgers

Now the second storey, and the evening's decisive definition.

First, a lemma with the flavour of common sense, proved honestly at the desk: refining a partition never hurts. Add a waypoint to an itinerary and the upper sum cannot rise — a ceiling computed over a whole slice is at least the ceilings of its two halves, so splitting a slice can only lower or preserve the cage's cost. Symmetrically, the lower sum cannot fall. Finer chopping tightens both estimates or leaves them be; it never loosens them. And from this, one consequence that carries the entire theory on its back: every lower sum, from every partition whatsoever, sits at or below every upper sum, from every other partition whatsoever. Take any two itineraries, merge their waypoints into a common refinement, and compare all three — the desk walks the chain of inequalities in one line. Every bid from below undercuts every asking price from above. No exceptions, no near misses.

So the stage is set as follows. All the lower sums together form a set of numbers, every one of them bounded above by any upper sum you care to name. All the upper sums form a set bounded below by any lower sum. And now, sir, the bell — ring the fourteenth, and I shall take this one at ceremony pace, because this is the definition itself. Take the supremum of all the lower sums — the best that caging from below can ever achieve, over every finite itinerary there is. Call it the lower integral. Take the infimum of all the upper sums — the sharpest the outside cage can ever be drawn. Call it the upper integral. Those two numbers exist by the completeness axiom and by nothing else — the first storey needed completeness to build each brick, and the second storey needs it again to survey the whole infinite inventory of sums and name the two boundary values. Ring thirteen made the bricks; ring fourteen writes the books. The count stands at fourteen, and Episode Seven's forecast — that the bell would ring more than once before the mortar set — is honoured with the mortar barely mixed. The remaining suprema and infima of the evening are clerical work on these two, and per the standing convention the register counts marquee rings only; the small change is annotated at the desk, uncounted.

The definition, then, and the season has earned the right to make it in one breath. The lower integral is at most the upper integral, always — that is the lemma's consequence restated. When the two ledgers agree — when the best from below meets the sharpest from above in a single number — the function is called Riemann integrable on the interval, and the common value is the integral: the area, now honest. When the ledgers disagree, the function is not integrable: the region between cage and packing never closes, and this machine declines to assign an area at all. Both verdicts will matter tonight. We shall meet functions the machine embraces and — for the first time since the machine was proposed — a function of our close acquaintance that it must turn away.

One working tool before the parade of candidates, because checking agreement of two infinite ledgers head-on is nobody's idea of an evening. The auditor's criterion, proved at the desk from the definitions in a few lines: the function is integrable precisely when, for every tolerance epsilon, however small, there exists a single partition whose upper and lower sums differ by less than epsilon. One itinerary with a small gap convicts the books of agreement — because the lower integral and the upper integral are trapped inside that gap together. It is the courtroom geometry of Episode Five all over again, with the roles reversed: there, one evil bid convicted a cover of waste; here, one thrifty itinerary certifies the whole enterprise. Every integrability proof for the rest of the course is a hunt for that one good partition. The hunt begins now.

First Through the Door

The first candidate is the aristocracy: continuous functions. Every function continuous on the closed interval from a to b is integrable there. And the proof, sir, is a homecoming — nearly every acquisition of the last three episodes presents itself at the door within four sentences.

Watch the certificates being checked. A continuous function on a closed bounded interval — on a compact interval, Episode Five insists on the word — is bounded, by the extreme value theorem; so it holds a ticket, and the machine will even accept it. Better: on each slice of any partition, the supremum and infimum are not merely approached but attained — the ceiling and floor of each slice are honest function values, the extreme value theorem again, working retail this time. Better still, and decisively: by Heine and Cantor's theorem from Episode Six, continuity on a compact interval is automatically uniform continuity. And uniform continuity is exactly the certificate the auditor's criterion wishes to see.

Here is the argument, whole, in the ear. The skeptic names a tolerance epsilon for the gap between the ledgers. We convert it to a budget per unit width: epsilon divided by the length of the interval. Uniform continuity answers with a single response delta — one delta, valid across the entire interval, no renegotiation slice by slice; that is the whole meaning of uniform, purchased in Episode Six precisely so it could be spent tonight. Now choose any itinerary whose every slice is narrower than delta. On each slice, the ceiling minus the floor is a difference of two attained values at points less than delta apart — so it is less than the per-unit budget. Ceiling minus floor, below budget, on every slice at once. Multiply by the widths, sum, and the upper sum minus the lower sum comes in under epsilon. One partition, gap under tolerance; the auditor stamps the file; integrable. Which is what wanted proving, sir — and the season did the work; tonight's proof merely collected.

And now permit me to redeem a wink filed two episodes ago, word for word. When Episode Six taught you uniform continuity, I remarked that when one tabulates a function on a grid — evaluates at mesh points and trusts the gaps — the trust is precisely a uniform-continuity certificate: one mesh size, chosen once, bounding the error everywhere in the table. And I said, if you recall, that Episode Eight's integral would be one enormous table, and would present exactly this certificate at the door. It just did. An integral is a tabulation with the gaps priced honestly; uniform continuity is the instrument that prices every gap with one signature; and your trade's daily habit — sample on a mesh, integrate numerically, trust the interstices — is underwritten by Heine and Cantor and by nothing less. The certificate cleared the door, sir. The wink is redeemed.

The Staircase Pays

The second candidate through the door teaches a different lesson, and a subtler one: continuity, it turns out, was never the point.

Consider the monotone functions — the ascending staircases of Episode Two's picket-fence country, functions that never decrease as one walks rightward, jumps and corners and flats included. Descending versions mirror everything; I narrate the ascent. A monotone function on the closed interval holds a ticket automatically — its values are penned between its value at a and its value at b; boundedness for free, no extreme value theorem required. And it passes the auditor, sir, by an argument so tidy I shall give it to you entire, because the trick deserves to be seen once in slow motion.

Chop the interval into slices of equal width — the plainest itinerary there is. On each slice, an ascending function's floor is its value at the slice's left station and its ceiling is its value at the right station; between stations it can only climb. So on each slice, ceiling minus floor is simply the climb accomplished across that slice. And here is the trick: sum the climbs. The climb across the first slice, plus the climb across the second, plus the climb across the third — the intermediate stations cancel in pairs, each one arriving and departing, and the total is nothing but the final value minus the initial value. The entire staircase, however many steps, however wild their spacing, accomplishes exactly one net ascent: f of b minus f of a. It is Episode Four's telescoping, standing on its feet again — infinitely applicable, paid for once.

So the gap between the ledgers — upper sum minus lower sum — is the sum of the climbs times the common slice width, which is to say: the one fixed total ascent, multiplied by a width we control. The skeptic names epsilon; we chop fine enough that the width times the total ascent dips under it; the auditor stamps the file. Integrable — every monotone function, on every closed interval, without a single word about continuity.

Pause on what has just been admitted through the door, sir, because this is where the season's star gets spent. Episode Six closed with a starred problem — the second of its two — asking you to prove that a monotone function can be discontinuous at only countably many points; the picket fence and Episode Three's counting cooperating across the season, and I promised the consequence would be spent tonight. Here is the expenditure. Build a staircase with a jump at every rational — countably many jumps, dense in the interval, a discontinuity in every neighbourhood of every point — the desk carries the standard construction, jumps of summable sizes parked at an enumeration of the rationals. That object is monstrous by Episode Six's lights: no interval of continuity anywhere. And it integrates without complaint — the telescope neither knows nor cares where the climbs happen, only what they total. Meanwhile Episode Six's step function, prosecuted there in full quantifier dress for its discontinuity, is likewise waved through — piecewise constancy is the degenerate staircase. The moral, and it is tonight's first genuine tremor: integrability tolerates infinitely much discontinuity. The boundary of Riemann's jurisdiction does not run along the continuity line at all. It runs somewhere else — somewhere this season has no name for. Hold that thought through the summit; it returns as the crisis.

Housekeeping, in one breath, before the ascent. The integral is linear — integral of a sum is the sum of integrals, constants slide out; it is monotone — a larger function has a larger integral; it splits over adjacent intervals — integrate to c, then from c onward, and the pieces add; and the absolute value of the integral is at most the integral of the absolute value — the triangle inequality gone to the continuum, Episode Four's oldest friend in new employment. Each of these is a two-line argument on the ledgers, the desk files the lot, and aloud we spend them freely from here forward.

The Reconciliation

And so, sir, the summit. I have narrated this season as a rebuilding, but here at the top I may finally say plainly what was always at stake. The seventeenth century left mathematics two calculi, not one. The differential calculus: slopes, rates, the pen's direction — Episode Seven's country. The integral calculus: areas, accumulations, the region under the curve — tonight's. Two subjects, two machineries, two pedigrees running back to two different Greek problems. That they are secretly one subject — that slopes and areas are inverse trades — is the discovery Newton and Leibniz are actually famous for; not for either calculus alone, but for the bridge. And for a century and a half the bridge was crossed on faith. Tonight, with the integral finally an honest object and the mean value theorem installed as the workhorse it is, the faith becomes theorem. The fundamental theorem of calculus, both parts, earned.

Part one travels from areas toward slopes. Fix the function f, integrable on our interval, and manufacture from it a new function: the accumulation. For each x, let big F of x be the integral of f from a up to x — the running total, the area banked so far, the odometer of the region. The claim of part one: accumulation smooths. First, big F is always continuous — better, its steepness is capped by the size of f, so it is uniformly continuous of the politest kind; banked area cannot lurch, because the deposit over a thin sliver is at most the sliver's width times the function's bound. And second — the heart — wherever the original f is continuous, the accumulation is differentiable there, and its derivative is f itself. The area's rate of growth is the height of the curve. Say it plainly, sir: differentiate the integral and the integrand is returned to you, at every point where the integrand is civil.

The proof strategy fits in the ear entire. What is the difference quotient of big F across a small window at c? Banked area over the window, divided by the window's width — the average height of f across the window. So the question is whether average heights across shrinking windows converge to the height at c. And continuity at c is exactly the guarantee that they do: within any tolerance of f of c for all points close enough to c, hence the average within the same tolerance — an average of numbers near a target is near the target. The desk writes the two-line inequality; the picture is: shrink the window, the average height settles onto the pointwise height. Where f jumps, the averages straddle and no settlement occurs — the desk exhibits the step function's accumulation, a ramp with a corner, differentiable everywhere except precisely at the jump. Accumulation smooths, but it cannot smooth over a lie.

Part two travels the other way, slopes toward areas, and it is the direction your schooling spent. Suppose capital F is differentiable across the interval with derivative f — an antiderivative, in the schoolroom's vocabulary — and suppose, mark the clause, f itself is integrable. Then the integral of f from a to b is F of b minus F of a. Total area equals net change of the antiderivative. Every definite integral you have ever evaluated by finding a primitive and subtracting endpoints was this theorem, deployed on credit; tonight the credit clears.

And the proof, sir, is the mean value theorem cashing the cheque this course wrote it in Episode Seven — I said then it was the workhorse; watch it pull. Take any partition of the interval. Across each slice, apply the mean value theorem to capital F: somewhere inside that slice sits a witness point where F's instantaneous slope equals its average slope across the slice — where f at the witness, times the slice's width, exactly equals F's change across the slice. Now sum over the slices. The right-hand sides telescope — Episode Four's cancellation, working its second shift tonight — into F of b minus F of a, exactly, for every partition. The left-hand sides form a tagged sum for f, with the witnesses as tags; and any tagged sum is trapped between the slice floors and the slice ceilings — between the lower and the upper sum. So the fixed number F of b minus F of a lies between the lower and the upper sum of every partition whatsoever. But f is integrable: the ledgers close on a single value. A number trapped between both ledgers of every partition can only be that value. The net change is the integral. Proved — and observe the machinery bill: mean value theorem per slice, telescoping across slices, ledger-squeeze to close. Episode Seven's workhorse, Episode Four's cancellation, tonight's books. The reconciliation is not one theorem's trick; it is the season, assembled.

Let the wonder have its designated moment, sir — the budget authorizes exactly this one, and I have been saving it. Two questions that share no words: how steep is the curve here, and how much ground has it covered? A seventeenth-century intuition that they are the same question read backwards; a nineteenth-century proof; and between the two, essentially every quantitative achievement of the modern world, riding on the identity of slope and accumulation. Your trade computes gradients so that accumulated loss may be descended; the thermodynamics beneath your machines integrates rates into totals; the bridge is load-bearing in both directions, every hour of every day. It is the single most productive sentence mathematics has ever pronounced, and as of tonight, between us, it is not folklore. It is proved.

The Static at the Gate

Now, sir, the fine print — and the machine's first refusal.

The static returns. Dirichlet's function, born in Episode Six: one on every rational, zero on every irrational; a function that is pure interference, continuous nowhere, the season's first monster. You will recall its provenance — proposed by Dirichlet in eighteen twenty-nine, inside the very memoir on Fourier convergence our first evening described, and proposed for a pointed purpose: as a function beyond the reach of his era's integral. An appointment booked forty-six years before Darboux even tidied the machinery it would defeat. Tonight, act two of its three-act contract: the appointment is kept.

Run the machine and watch it seize. Take any partition of the unit interval — any itinerary whatsoever, coarse or cunning. Consider one slice. However thin it is, it is a genuine interval, and Episode Two's picket fence runs through it twice: the rationals are dense, so the slice contains a rational, where the static's value is one; the irrationals are dense, so it contains an irrational, where the value is zero. Every slice, ceiling one, floor zero. No partition can do better, none can do worse; cunning is unemployed. So every upper sum is the whole interval's width — one — and every lower sum is zero. The upper ledger reads one, forever; the lower ledger reads zero, forever; the books stand a fixed unit apart and will not close for any refinement in the world. The auditor's criterion fails at every tolerance below one. The static is not Riemann integrable — not marginally, not reparably. The machine does not struggle with this function; it registers it as noise and turns it away at the gate.

Dirichlet's booking of eighteen twenty-nine, honoured to the letter, sir. Debt four, act two of three, executed on schedule. And note the staging with care: the static is bounded — it holds a perfectly valid ticket — and it is defeated anyway. Boundedness admits you to the anteroom; it does not seat you. Act three stands where it has stood since Episode Six: Season Two, Episode Four, where a better machine tames this creature in one line. Remember, when we get there, how total tonight's defeat was.

Thomae Passes

And behind the static, its photographic negative walks in — and here, sir, is the result I have been herding you toward for two episodes.

Thomae's function, Episode Six's second monster: zero on every irrational; on every rational, one over the denominator in lowest terms — the loud fractions and the quiet ones, the ruler's engraving. Discontinuous at every rational, continuous at every irrational; discontinuous, that is, on a dense set — a defect in every neighbourhood of every point of the interval, just like the static. Episode Six filed the contrast without explanation and called it a teaser. Redemption follows.

Thomae's function is Riemann integrable, and its integral is zero.

The strategy, aloud, in the loudness dialect the creature taught us. The skeptic names a tolerance epsilon. Divide the fractions into the loud and the quiet at a threshold: call a rational loud if the function's value there — one over its denominator — meets or exceeds half of epsilon. Loudness demands a small denominator; and Episode Six's counting lemma, solved at the desk there, counts the possible culprits: below any loudness threshold there are only finitely many loud fractions in the unit interval. Finitely many, sir — that is the entire battle. Now build the itinerary in two gestures. Around each of the finitely many loud fractions, draw a slice so thin that all the loud slices together total less than half of epsilon in width — finitely many culprits, so the thinness is ours to command. Everywhere else, the function is quiet: values below half of epsilon. Price the upper ledger: the loud slices contribute at most their total width — under half of epsilon — times the function's ceiling of one; the quiet slices contribute at most the whole interval's width times half of epsilon. Upper sum, under epsilon. The lower ledger was never in question: irrationals in every slice pin every floor at zero, so every lower sum is zero exactly. Gap under epsilon; the auditor stamps; integrable, integral zero. The desk writes the bookkeeping in full — it is the season's prettiest deployment of "finitely many exceptions, each individually caged," which the attentive listener will recognize as compactness's household style learned in Episode Five, plied here by hand.

So hold the two verdicts side by side, sir, because no course in my care will hurry past this. The static: discontinuous on a dense set — defeated. Thomae: discontinuous on a dense set — admitted, priced, integrated. Density of defect cannot be what decides. Cardinality fares no better as a diagnosis, and the desk sharpens the point: the staircase admitted an hour ago jumps on a countable dense set, like Thomae; yet the static's defect set — every point of the interval — is uncountable, and one is tempted to blame size; but the desk's notes table a bounded function discontinuous exactly on the dust — Episode Three's uncountable Cantor set — that passes the auditor without difficulty. Uncountably many defects, integrable regardless. Every classifier this season owns — density, countability, topological size — files a wrong or useless verdict on tonight's evidence.

The Vocabulary Crisis

That, sir, is a vocabulary crisis, and I use the term precisely. It is not that we have a conjecture and lack a proof — a familiar, honest condition. It is that we cannot state the theorem. The boundary of Riemann's jurisdiction runs through the territory of tonight's examples along a line for which this season possesses no word. Some discontinuity sets are negligible to the machine; some are fatal; and the property that separates them is not density, not cardinality, not category — not any instrument in the season's cabinet.

I shall do what the honesty clause permits, which is to read you the answer as prophecy, in words we cannot yet define. Here is Lebesgue's criterion, published in nineteen oh two, resolving the exact question Riemann's memoir left open. A bounded function on a closed bounded interval is Riemann integrable precisely when its set of discontinuities has measure zero — when the set of defects, covered by intervals as cunningly as you please, can be made to have total covering length as small as you please. "Measure zero." The phrase has no definition tonight, sir; that is Season Two's opening labour. But run the exhibits through the prophecy and hear how it rings. The continuous functions: defect set empty — nothing to cover; admitted. The staircases: countably many jumps — and countable sets, the prophecy whispers, can be covered by intervals of total length as small as one pleases; Episode Two's picket fence even hints at the trick, one shrinking interval per jump; admitted. Thomae: defects exactly the rationals — countable, again negligible; admitted, and now we know why, rather than merely how. The dust-discontinuous function of the desk's notes: defects an uncountable set that nonetheless — you will prove this in Season Two, and gasp — carries total length zero; admitted, and cardinality convicted of irrelevance. The static: defects everywhere, the full unit of length, nothing negligible about it; refused. Every verdict of the evening, sir, one criterion, no exceptions — and the criterion is unpronounceable in the season's language. A theorem from the future, quoted on credit: the register logs it formally as a debt, proof due when Season Two, Episode Four builds the machinery — the same episode, you will note, in which the static meets its act three. The accounts of this course, sir, have a novelist's sense of convergence.

And one philosophical sentence, because the crisis has earned it: tonight is the first time this season that a question about functions has been answered by measuring a set. The season asked "where is the function continuous"; the answer needed "how much land do the defects occupy." The centre of gravity just moved, sir — from functions to sets, from continuity to length — and it will not move back. That is, in one sentence, why there is a Season Two.

The Spike, or the Second Instalment

Two accounts remain before the audit, and the first is the swap account — opened with ceremony in Episode Four, first instalment paid in Seven, second falling due tonight. When may a limit be exchanged with an integral? The eighteenth century swapped freely and mostly got away with it; Episode One showed you Fourier's assemblies breaking Cauchy's swapped-limits theorem; tonight the crime scene is the integral, and I have brought a demonstration.

The moving spike, sir — watch it run. On the unit interval, for each index n, build a tent: from the origin the function climbs linearly to a peak of height n reached at one over n, descends linearly back to zero by two over n, and lies flat — zero — the rest of the way. Each tent is continuous, piecewise linear, integrable by tonight's aristocratic clause; its integral is the area of a triangle, one half times base times height — one half times two over n times n — which is one. Exactly one, sir, for every n: the tents grow taller precisely as they grow thinner, and the banked area holds fixed like a conservation law. Now send n to infinity and ask what the sequence of functions converges to, pointwise, Episode Seven's dialect. At the origin itself every tent is zero. At any fixed point to the right of the origin, the tents eventually evacuate: once two over n falls below your point, the spike has retreated entirely to your left and the function there reads zero, forever after. Pointwise limit: the zero function, everywhere on the interval. Integral of the limit: zero. Limit of the integrals: one. The swap does not merely fail, sir; it fails by the entire quantity under discussion. All the area escaped — straight up the spike, if you want the picture: mass squeezed leftward and skyward, thinner and taller, until in the limit it has nowhere to stand and vanishes without ever crossing the boundary. Episode Seven's leak and escape, re-offending at the integral; the desk books it as the season's final specimen.

The diagnosis, by now, is reflex: pointwise convergence is the weak currency, and the tents refuse the strong one — their peaks stand at height n, so the uniform distance from the tents to zero is n itself, growing without bound. No tube of any radius ever contains them. And the strong currency, it turns out, is exactly the price of the license. The theorem, sir — instalment two: if a sequence of integrable functions converges uniformly on a closed bounded interval, then the limit function is integrable, and the integral of the limit is the limit of the integrals. Uniform convergence licenses the swap of limit and integral. The strategy narrates in one gesture: uniformity pins the whole sequence, eventually, inside a tube of any requested radius around the limit; a function trapped in a thin tube around an integrable function has its two ledgers trapped nearly together — so the limit passes the auditor; and the areas can differ by at most the tube's cross-section times the interval's width — so the integrals converge. Tube in, tube out; the desk writes the epsilon bookkeeping in full, and marks with relish that the sum it needs — the tube's radius, a supremum over the interval — is Episode Seven's uniform distance doing its second season of service.

The register, with ceremony. Debt five, the swap account: instalment two of three, paid in full — the limit moved past the integral, licensed under uniformity, over the corpse of the moving spike. Paid, sir — and yet I file the payment with a codicil the course will spend two further episodes honouring: the license is stingy. Uniformity is sufficient and brutally expensive — the spike fails it, but so do harmless sequences whose integrals converge beautifully; the desk exhibits one, thin tents whose area also thins away, convergence of integrals achieved with no uniformity in sight. Sufficient, not necessary; a license, not the law. The final instalment — the swap theorems that earn their keep, the crown jewels I promised in Episode Four when the account was opened — is Season Two's fifth episode, and when you see what those theorems ask, which is nearly nothing, you will understand what this season's machinery was too weak to buy. Hold tonight's stinginess in mind as the standard to be beaten.

Volterra, or the Hole Below the Waterline

The last account of the evening, sir, and I have kept it for last because it is aimed at tonight's own triumph.

Recall the fundamental theorem, part two, and attend to its fine print as read: capital F differentiable across the interval with derivative f — and, the clause I asked you to mark, f itself integrable. At the summit that clause looked like lawyer's caution, the sort of hypothesis one states to be tidy and never fears in the field. Surely a function that is somebody's derivative — every point, no exceptions, honest instantaneous slopes throughout — is respectable enough to integrate? Episode Seven taught you that derivatives cannot jump; Darboux showed us they pass through every intermediate value; they are strange, but they are house-trained. Are they not?

In eighteen eighty-one, Vito Volterra — twenty-one years old, sir, a student in Pisa — settled the question with a construction of terrible elegance. There exists a function, differentiable at every single point of the unit interval, with derivative bounded throughout — no spikes, no blow-ups, everything a ticket-holder — whose derivative is not Riemann integrable. The oscillation of that derivative refuses to die down on a set too substantial for the ledgers to absorb: the upper and lower books stand permanently apart, and the auditor's stamp never comes. Now hear what that does to the fundamental theorem. The net change of capital F across the interval is a perfectly definite number — the function sits there, differentiable, blameless. Part two would compute that number as the integral of the derivative. But the integral of the derivative does not exist. The formula is not wrong, sir; it is worse than wrong — it is unpronounceable. There is a derivative, bounded and everywhere defined, whose accumulated change Riemann's machine simply cannot read back. Differentiation took a step the integral cannot retrace. The reconciliation of the two calculi — tonight's summit, the season's peak — has a hole below the waterline, and the hole is not exotic: it is a bounded derivative.

The construction itself is a desk affair — starred, guided, and I shall only describe its silhouette aloud, with pacing as my excuse and honesty as my policy. Take a fattened cousin of Episode Three's dust — a Cantor-style set built with shrinking removals so that, when the removing is done, what remains keeps positive extent; Season Two will make "positive extent" precise, and this fat dust is filed already as an exhibit for it. In each removed gap, plant a carefully trimmed copy of a certain oscillating specimen — and yes, sir, it is exactly the specimen of Episode Seven's loan, x squared times the sine of one over x, the function whose derivative exists everywhere yet oscillates without settling; the register's one loan is serviced a second time tonight, still desk-only, still awaiting the power series for repayment. Trim each copy so its derivative stays bounded but arrives at the gap's endpoints still oscillating at full amplitude. The assembled function is differentiable everywhere; but at every point of the fat dust, the derivative's oscillation stands at fixed size — and the fat dust occupies positive extent, so the ledgers cannot close around it. Bounded derivative; integral refused. The desk gives the blueprint, the references, and the honest admission of which verifications are deferred to Season Two's language.

And the repair? Here the honesty clause takes the floor, sir, because the situation is genuinely delicate, and I shall state it exactly. Season Two's machine — the Lebesgue integral, waiting in the wings — tames tonight's specific monster: every bounded derivative is Lebesgue integrable, and integrates back to the net change; Volterra's creature is domesticated alongside the static, and the fundamental theorem is repaired across the entire bounded estate. But the full reconciliation — every derivative whatsoever, bounded or not, recoverable from its slopes by some integral — lies beyond even Season Two's machinery; there are unbounded derivatives that escape Lebesgue as surely as Volterra's escapes Riemann. An integral exists that closes the account entirely — the desk names it and shelves the reference, for it is a Season Three sort of object, and I am making no announcements about Season Three. Tonight the ledger simply records, without flinching: the peak we conquered this evening has a crack in it, the crack is real, and the best machinery of the next season closes most of it, in spirit nearly all of it, and honestly not quite all of it.
