The Convergence Theorems

The Measure of All Things, Season Two, Episode Five

The Question, Restated

Good day, sir.

If the Lebesgue integral were a product — and I have occasionally marketed it as one — tonight is the feature list that justifies the migration. Everything before was installation.

The question first, restated with the full weight it has accumulated, for no question in this course has a longer file. When does the integral of a limit equal the limit of the integrals? It was flagged in the first season's first evening, among Fourier's casualties. It was opened as a formal account — the swap account — in Season One, Episode Four, at the scene of the rearrangement outrage, with instalments scheduled through the end of this season. Episode Seven of that season paid the first: limits moved past limits, under the uniform license, over the corpse of Cauchy's false theorem. The season finale paid the second: the limit moved past the integral, again under uniformity, over the corpse of the moving spike — and I filed, you may recall, a codicil with the payment: the uniform license is sufficient and brutally expensive; sufficient, not necessary; a license, not the law. I asked you to hold its stinginess in mind as the standard to be beaten. And I quote the account's own opening paper back to you, as promised the night it was signed: Season Two, I said then, has crown jewels — the theorems that license the swap — and I say this now so you may hold me to it. You held; tonight I pay. The stakes, in your trade's plain terms: every time a training loop swaps a limit with an expectation — every convergence proof for an estimator, every exchange of gradient and average, every passage to the limit under an integral of losses — the manoeuvre stands on tonight's theorems or it does not stand at all. Tonight, sir, the account is closed — the third and final instalment, the crown jewels of this season and arguably of the entire course: three theorems that move limits past integrals for a licensing fee so small that Season One would have refused to believe the invoice. Monotone convergence. Fatou's lemma. Dominated convergence. By the hour's end, each will be proved — strategy aloud, paper in full — the additivity credit issued last week will be discharged on schedule, and your trade's daily habit of swapping a limit and an expectation will hold, at last, a theorem instead of a hope.

The Three Escapes

Before the licenses, the crimes — and here the season pays one of its most instructive dividends, because the new machinery does not merely prove the theorems; it explains, exhaustively, what could ever go wrong. Mass, sir, escapes in exactly three ways, and I shall show you each as a picture.

Escape the first: off to the horizon. The marching bump — a block of unit mass sitting on the interval from n to n plus one, marching rightward as n grows. At every fixed point, the bump eventually passes and is gone: the pointwise limit is zero everywhere. But every term carries mass one; the integrals hold at one while the limit integrates to nought. The mass was not destroyed; it emigrated — horizontally, to infinity, one step per term.

Escape the second: up the spike. Season One's finale specimen, returning for its final appearance: height n on ground one over n — the tent thinner and taller with every term, mass fixed at one, pointwise limit zero. The mass climbs, sir: vertical emigration, the ground shrinking under it until in the limit it stands on nothing.

Escape the third: spread thin. Height one over n on the ground from nought to n — mass one, spread ever wider and flatter. The limit is zero — indeed the convergence is uniform, the sag everywhere at most one over n — and yet each term's mass is one. The mass neither marched nor climbed; it diluted, across ground that grew as fast as the height fell.

Note the accounting common to all three: no term destroys mass — each carries its unit faithfully — and yet the limit is destitute. The mass is conserved along the sequence and absent from its destination; it escapes not from the functions but from every fixed observer's window, horizontally past any frame, vertically past any ceiling, or laterally past any width. Three pictures, sir: the bump, the spike, the spread. Horizontal escape, vertical escape, dilution. And the theorem-shaped fact of the evening is this: that is the complete list. There is no fourth escape. Every failure of the swap, however dressed, is one of these three in costume — and each of tonight's licenses is engineered to bar specific exits. Note, before we leave the gallery, what the spread proves about Season One's prize instrument: its convergence is uniform, and the swap fails anyway. On unbounded ground, even the expensive license does not suffice — the finale's interchange theorem leaned on the interval's finite width, quietly, and the spread is what happens when nothing does. Uniformity was never the true currency, sir. Domination is — and we shall now buy it properly.

The Climbing License

The first theorem bars every exit at once, by the simplest possible means: it forbids descent.

The monotone convergence theorem. Let a sequence of nonnegative measurable functions climb — each term at least its predecessor, pointwise — toward a limit function. Then the integral of the limit is the limit of the integrals. No domination, no uniformity, no finiteness — the limit and the integrals may all be infinite, and the theorem holds regardless, the two sides infinite together or finite together and equal. Climbing mass cannot escape: the bump would have to abandon ground it holds — descent; the spike would have to lower its skirts — descent; the spread likewise. Monotonicity bars all three exits with one clause.

The proof strategy, aloud, in two halves. One half is free: each climbing term sits below the limit, so each integral sits below the limit's integral — monotonicity of the integral, last week's manners — and the climbing integrals, a monotone sequence of numbers, converge by Season One's oldest license to something at most the limit's integral. The substance is the other half: the climbing integrals reach every under-staircase of the limit. Here is the idea, and it is the season's signature move wearing evening dress. Fix an under-staircase of the limit and offer it a discount: choose a rate c, strictly below one, and ask only that the climbing sequence overtake the discounted staircase — c times the storeys, rather than the storeys entire. Where does the n-th term suffice? On some citizen set; and as n grows these sufficiency sets swell — the climb is monotone — toward everything: at any point, the limit strictly exceeds the discounted storey, so some term already does. Now the crankshaft turns — continuity of measure along swelling chains, proved in this season's first evening for exactly this moment: each storey's ground is exhausted from within, the simple integral follows its grounds, and the climbing integrals dominate c times the staircase's full price. Let the discount tend to full price; the climbing integrals dominate every under-staircase; hence the supremum itself — which is, by ring sixteen, the integral of the limit. The desk writes every epsilon; the ear retains: discount, swell, exhaust, and let the discount expire. Beppo Levi's theorem, the trade also calls it, nineteen oh six; the season's engine, first crankshaft dividend at full torque. The clauses travel with the usual amnesty — climbing almost everywhere toward a limit almost everywhere suffices, null exceptions patched at the desk — and the sign clause is load-bearing, not clerical: a climbing sequence of signed functions can start at minus infinity's doorstep and climb forever without its integrals following; the desk's descending-tower specimen, read upside down, is the two-line witness.

One application before the corollaries, sir, chosen because Season One's finale had to refuse it. Integrate one over the square root of x on the interval from nought to one — unbounded near the origin, no ticket for Riemann's machine proper, an improper integral by the old regime's grace. The new regime needs no grace: truncate at one over n — the truncations climb, each bounded and honest — the truncated integrals compute by the schoolroom rule to two minus two over the square root of n, climbing to two; and the monotone license passes the limit: the integral is two, exactly, as an honest Lebesgue integral, no impropriety about it. The absolutely convergent improper integrals of Season One are absorbed wholesale by this one manoeuvre — truncate, climb, license — and the desk retires the relevant half of last week's boundary post on precisely this argument.

The Credit, Discharged

And the machine now completes itself, sir, on schedule — the one-week maturity, honoured to the day.

Last week I issued, plainly flagged, the construction's final credit: additivity of the integral — that the integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals — proved only on the bounded estate, stated on credit beyond it. Watch the discharge. Take two nonnegative measurable functions; take their dyadic staircases, climbing as the staircase theorem provides; the sums of the staircases climb toward the sum of the functions; simple functions add exactly — last week's storey bookkeeping; and the monotone convergence theorem passes the addition through the limit. The integral of the sum is the sum of the integrals, on all nonnegative measurable functions whatsoever, and thence — gains and losses, the usual split — on all integrable ones. Debt fourteen: discharged, first corollary of the first crown jewel, precisely as the register scheduled.

And a second corollary follows so fast it barely touches the ground, resolving a question this course has carried since the rearrangement outrage: series and integrals swap freely when the terms are nonnegative. A series of nonnegative measurable functions is a climbing sequence of partial sums; additivity prices each partial sum; the monotone theorem passes the limit: the integral of the sum of a nonnegative series is the sum of the integrals, always — divergence included, both sides infinite together. No conditions, sir, beyond the sign. The rearrangement theorem taught you that signed infinite arithmetic is treacherous; tonight's corollary is the other shore: nonnegative infinite arithmetic commutes with everything in the building. The trade calls the pair — swap freely when nonnegative; check absolutely then swap when signed — the standard operating procedure, and Episode Seven will canonize it for double integrals under Tonelli's and Fubini's names. It begins here.

The One-Way Valve

The second jewel is an inequality, and I shall spend a moment making its direction unforgettable, because half its citations in the wild are citations of its direction alone.

Fatou's lemma. For any sequence of nonnegative measurable functions — no monotonicity, no domination, nothing but the sign — the integral of the limit inferior is at most the limit inferior of the integrals. In the vernacular, sir: mass can be lost in the limit, never created. The three escapes are all losses — the bump's mass leaves, the spike's mass leaves, the spread's mass leaves; in every case the limit holds less than the terms carried. Fatou says that is the only direction the books can err: whatever survives to the limit was always there, term by term, in the liminf's honest accounting. A one-way valve on mass.

The mnemonic, before the proof, since the direction is the lemma's soul: the integrals remember mass that the limit has forgotten. The spike's integrals remember the unit that climbed away; its limit knows nothing. So the integrals' liminf stands at or above the limit's integral — never below. If you misremember the direction, sir, the spike corrects you in five seconds; I recommend the spike.

The proof rides the first jewel in four sentences — narrated entire, since the desk needs barely more. The infima of the tails — the running worst cases — form a climbing sequence, climbing by construction toward the limit inferior. Each tail-infimum sits below its own term, so its integral sits below that term's integral, hence below the tail's worst integral. Monotone convergence passes the climb on the left; the right side's tail-infima are the limit inferior of the integrals by definition. Valve proved. And the spike certifies the inequality strict: limit zero, integrals one — mass genuinely lost, the valve genuinely one-way. The desk adds the standing caution with a two-line specimen: no mirror statement for limsup holds without further hypotheses, and the sign condition is not decorative.

The Crown Jewel

And now the theorem the season was built to reach, sir — the one your trade runs on daily, the one whose absence Season One could only paper over with uniformity's premium.

The dominated convergence theorem. Let a sequence of measurable functions converge pointwise — almost everywhere suffices, the amnesty extending every clause tonight — to a limit. Suppose the sequence is dominated: a single integrable function, one lid, sits above every term's absolute value, every term, everywhere. Then the limit is integrable, the integrals converge to the limit's integral — and the swap is licensed.

Hear the license's economy against the escapes. The bump cannot march under a lid: an integrable lid over every translate would carry mass along the whole horizon — infinite mass, not integrable. The spike cannot climb: a lid over every spike majorizes one over x near the origin — infinite mass again. The spread cannot dilute: a lid over every spreading term covers a fixed plateau on every frame out to infinity — infinite once more; the desk computes all three in a line apiece. One hypothesis, sir — one integrable ceiling — and all three exits are bricked shut at once. That is what domination means: the mass has nowhere to go, because the lid is a fixed, finite roof over the entire procession, and mass that cannot move must converge. Compare the price against Season One's: uniformity demanded control of every discrepancy at every point simultaneously, and still needed finite ground; the lid asks one function, checked once, and covers infinite ground without comment. What those theorems ask, sir, is nearly nothing — and now you see what this season's machinery was too weak to buy until tonight.

The proof, and it is the evening's most elegant three minutes: the crown jewel is Fatou's valve, applied twice, once from each side. The lid plus each term is nonnegative — the lid absorbs the negative parts, that is its office; Fatou's valve on that sequence, after the additivity of the credit's discharge cancels the lid's finite integral from both sides — lawful, the lid being integrable — yields: the limit's integral is at most the liminf of the integrals. The lid minus each term is likewise nonnegative; the valve again, the lid cancelling again, and the inequality arrives reversed: the limsup of the integrals is at most the limit's integral. Liminf above, limsup below, both pinned to the same number: the integrals converge, to exactly the limit's integral. Two applications of a one-way valve, facing opposite directions, make a two-way license. The desk writes the cancellation bookkeeping in full and notes where the lid's integrability is spent — twice, once per cancellation, neither optional.

Two riders complete the jewel's papers, both desk-proved tonight. The convergence is stronger than stated: not only do the integrals converge — the integral of the absolute discrepancy converges to nought; run the double valve on the discrepancies themselves, dominated by twice the lid, and the limit-in-the-mean drops out, a phrase Episode Six will formalize into a geometry. And the lid's own integral bounds everything in sight uniformly, so the limit inherits integrability with room to spare — the desk's bookkeeping makes both riders two lines each.

Let the wonder beat sound here, sir, as the plan designates, and let it be the quiet kind. There is no new machinery in that proof. The staircases built the integral; the crankshaft turned the climb; the climb proved the valve; the valve, doubled, proved the jewel. Four evenings of this season, stacked in a single column, and at the top — the theorem that every working analyst, every probabilist, every engineer who has ever differentiated under an integral sign or swapped an expectation with a limit, uses more often than any other single result of twentieth-century mathematics. It is the season's whole argument, sir, compressed into one licensing clause: measure the ground honestly, and the limits move free.

The Bounded Corollary

One special case deserves its own name before the applications, because it is the form your trade meets daily and the form that retires a Season One theorem with honours. On finite ground — the unit interval, any frame, any probability space — a constant is integrable; so if a pointwise-convergent sequence is uniformly bounded — one number capping every term everywhere — the constant is a lid, and the jewel applies: the bounded convergence theorem, domination's pocket edition. Probability lives on ground of measure one, sir, so this corollary is the workhorse of Episode Eight's entire subject: bounded random quantities swap limits and expectations without further ceremony. And note its reach backward: Season One's finale proved the uniform-convergence interchange theorem with a page of tube bookkeeping; tonight it is two lines — uniform convergence on the finite interval eventually bounds the sequence by the limit's bound plus one, a constant lid, done. The finale's theorem was the pocket edition's pocket edition, sir; the season's machinery re-proves its ancestor in passing, which is how one knows the machinery is the right one.

What the Lid Buys

Three applications with teeth, in ascending order of your likely affection.

First: series of signed terms. Check the absolute series integrates — Tonelli-for-series, the nonnegative freedom of the credit's corollary — and the lid is the absolute partial sums' majorant; dominated convergence swaps sum and integral. The standard operating procedure, now fully licensed on the line.

Second: continuity of integrals in a parameter. An integral depending on a dial — a temperature, a time, a hyperparameter — is continuous in the dial wherever the integrand is continuous in it and dominated uniformly in it: take any sequence of dial settings converging to a target, and the lid marches the limit through. One integrable roof, checked once; continuity everywhere under it.

Third, the celebrated one: differentiation under the integral sign. If the integrand's partial derivative in the dial exists and is itself dominated — one integrable lid over all the difference quotients' witnesses, which the mean value theorem of Season One supplies from a lid on the derivative — then the derivative of the integral is the integral of the derivative. The desk states the hypotheses with full precision and proves the theorem by exactly the sequence-plus-lid manoeuvre above. This, sir, is the trick your trade attributes to Feynman — he learned it, by his own cheerful account, from a calculus text nobody else had read, and wielded it as a private superpower; it is licensed tonight as a two-line corollary of the crown jewel, and the desk sets one specimen integral for sport, with the honesty clause noting its logarithm still rides on Season One's credit. Feynman's superpower, sir, was this season's Episode Five, read early.

The Parade of Counterexamples

The licenses earn their fine print, per the plan's own mandate: each hypothesis violated, once, in public.

Monotone convergence without monotonicity: the bump — nonnegative, pointwise to zero, integrals stuck at one; the climb was the load-bearing clause. Monotone convergence climbing from the wrong side: descending sequences with infinite starts — one over x above n, say, descending to zero with every integral infinite — the theorem's mirror needs a finite first floor, exactly as the crankshaft's shrinking chains did, and the desk files the kinship. Fatou without the sign: allow negatives and mass can be created — the negative spike, minus n on ground one over n, has integrals minus one and liminf zero; the valve leaks backward without its nonnegativity gasket. Dominated convergence without the lid: all three escapes, each individually fatal, as computed. And the spread once more, for emphasis: uniform convergence, no lid, swap fails — uniformity neither implies domination nor substitutes for it. Five violations, five failures, no theorem wounded: the hypotheses, sir, are not lawyer's caution; each is a wall against a named escape.

The Account, Closed

The register, sir, and tonight it closes the oldest active account in the establishment.

Debt five: the swap account. Opened, Season One, Episode Four, at the rearrangement outrage, with the words: when may two limiting processes be exchanged? Instalment one, Season One, Episode Seven: limits past limits, the uniform license, Cauchy's corpse as exhibit. Instalment two, the season finale: limit past integral, uniformity again, the moving spike convicted, the stinginess codicil filed with the payment. Instalment three, tonight: the crown jewels — climbing mass swaps free; nonnegative mass swaps free; dominated mass swaps free; and the fine print is three pictures long. The account is closed, sir — not paid off and forgotten, but closed the way a good account closes: converted into standing law. I read the closing comparison into the record. Season One's license: uniform control at every point, finite ground, and even then the theorems came one at a time. This season's: one integrable lid — or mere monotonicity, or mere nonnegativity — and the swaps hold on any measurable ground in the building, infinite included. The stinginess codicil is discharged with prejudice. Two seasons, sir, from outrage to law; the register notes that no account has travelled further or arrived better.

The bell: silent tonight, and the silence is the elegant kind — the count holds at sixteen. Every theorem of the evening ran on ring sixteen's supremum, the crankshaft's swelling chains, and the staircases — instruments long since paid for. When a season's crown jewels ring nothing new, sir, the season's architecture is finished; what remains is furniture.

Terms of Engagement

The desk's half of the evening — chapter five of the second volume, the volume's core chapter, as the plan bills it — toured in the standing manner.

The notation ledger seats the liminf and limsup of function sequences, the tail-infima, the lid, and the standard-operating-procedure schema.

Proofs in full, the season's tightest file: the monotone convergence theorem with the discount argument displayed — the sufficiency sets, the crankshaft's turn, the storey-by-storey exhaustion, the discount's expiry — every epsilon written; the additivity discharge, boxed, with the credit's paper formally retired; the series corollary, nonnegative interchange in full; Fatou by the climbing tail-infima, four sentences at the desk too; the caution specimens — no limsup mirror, no unsigned valve; the dominated convergence theorem with the double-valve bookkeeping and both expenditures of the lid's integrability marked; the almost-everywhere versions of all three, the amnesty patching each null exception; continuity in a parameter; differentiation under the integral sign with hypotheses stated exactly and the mean-value bridge from derivative-lid to quotient-lid made explicit; and the counterexample zoo formalized — the bump, the spike, the spread, the negative spike, the descending tower — each priced against each theorem in a table the desk is rather proud of. For sport: the promised specimen — the integral from nought to one of, x to the power t minus one, over the logarithm of x — evaluated by differentiation under the sign to the logarithm of one plus t, domination checked honestly, the logarithm's credit line cited.

Assigned, with the standing policies. Tier one, Calisthenics, eight: the three escapes priced by hand at three named indices; monotone-theorem warm-ups on staircase sequences; a valve drill — compute both sides of Fatou for the bump and the spike; lid-hunting — find dominating functions where they exist for five sequences and certify the failures where they do not; the credit's discharge replayed on a two-function specimen; a series-swap drill with nonnegative terms; an almost-everywhere patch drill; and the descending-tower audit. Tier two, The Real Thing, six: the discount argument written to the letter — solved in full, the audio's promise; the double-valve proof of dominated convergence with the cancellation bookkeeping — solved in full, likewise; the limsup mirror's exact hypotheses — state, prove, and break; differentiation under the integral sign assembled from the crown jewel with the mean-value bridge; the spread dissected — uniform convergence, no lid, and precisely which line of the finale's interchange theorem fails on infinite ground; and the sport integral, domination audited line by line. Tier three, For the Ambitious, two stars: the generalized dominated theorem — lids allowed to vary and converge in the mean — with references; and Fatou's valve run on measures rather than functions — the swelling-chain and valve statements unified through indicator sequences, a first taste of the abstract theory the finale will gesture at. Solutions per standing policy: tier one terse and complete; tier two, the discount and the double valve — the pair completing spoken arguments; the rest hinted; tier three, hints alone. The digest opens the chapter as always.

Until Next Time

The ledger's summary lines, sir. Closed: debt five, the swap account, two seasons from outrage to law — the establishment's longest-travelled account, retired with a comparison table in its file. Discharged: debt fourteen, the additivity credit, on its one-week schedule, first corollary of the first jewel. Silent: the bell, at sixteen, on finished architecture. Standing: Fourier's account — tonight's jewels are precisely the instruments its remaining service requires, and the account enters its final phase; and one small new promise, filed quietly: the standard operating procedure, canonized for double integrals, two weeks hence.

For next time: the field guide. Five modes of convergence now circulate in the establishment — pointwise, almost everywhere, uniform, in the mean, and a newcomer called convergence in measure — and Episode Six sorts the whole menagerie: which modes imply which, which extract which along subsequences, and which counterexamples keep the arrows honest. The star exhibit is the typewriter — a sequence of blocks sweeping the unit interval in ever-finer carriage returns, converging in measure and in the mean while converging pointwise at not one single point — the finest convergence counterexample in the literature, and one I have been saving since the season was planned. Above it all, Littlewood's three principles — every measurable set is nearly a finite union of intervals; every measurable function is nearly continuous; every convergent sequence is nearly uniformly convergent — with the word nearly bearing, at last, its exact measure-theoretic weight, and the season's approximation manners graduating into named theorems: Egorov's and Lusin's. A tidying episode, sir, after tonight's summit — the establishment takes inventory before the final ascent.

I remain, sir — dominatedly, beneath one integrable lid of my own choosing — your obedient servant.

Good day, sir.
