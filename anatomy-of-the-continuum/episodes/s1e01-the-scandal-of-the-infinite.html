The Scandal of the Infinite

The Anatomy of the Continuum, Season One, Episode One

An Overture, and the Terms of Our Arrangement

Good day, sir.

We begin today a course of study in the mathematics of the continuum — which is to say, of the real numbers; which is to say, of the straight line that every schoolchild draws with a ruler and believes, on no evidence whatsoever, that he understands. I intend, over the eight episodes of this season, to disturb that belief thoroughly, and then to replace it with something incomparably better: an understanding built from the foundations upward, every stone inspected before it is laid. The subject is called real analysis, and it has the singular virtue that in it, rigour and wonder are not rivals. They are the same thing, seen from two sides.

A second season waits beyond this one, in which we shall ask an even more innocent question — how long is a set of points? — and discover that the honest answer occupied some of the finest minds of the early twentieth century, cost mathematics the comfortable belief that every set has a size, and ended by revealing that probability itself, the daily bread of your profession, sir, had been a branch of this subject all along, wearing a costume. But that is the second season. One scandal at a time.

A word, first, on the terms of our arrangement, for this course is a partnership with a strict division of labour. These episodes are the ear's half: motivation, history, definitions rendered honestly in words, the strategy and architecture of proofs, and the framing of the work to be done elsewhere. The desk's half is a printed companion — I have taken the liberty of titling it the Desk Volume — one chapter to each episode, containing the precise notation, the formal statements, the complete proofs, and the problems, in three tiers of ascending ambition. The ear is a magnificent instrument, sir, but it cannot hold a computation with three moving parts, and I do not propose to insult it by trying. When an argument belongs on paper, I shall say so plainly, tell you what the paper will accomplish, and proceed. In return, nothing appears in the volume that these episodes have not motivated; the paper never springs a stranger on you. Neither half stands alone. Listen first; then, at your leisure, repair to the desk. Toward the end of the hour I shall walk you through what the first chapter holds, so that you arrive knowing the terrain.

One further article of the contract, and it is the one I hold most dear: honesty. When this course simplifies, it will say so. When a proof is deferred, it will say where the full argument lives, and when a claim is beyond the course entirely, it will say that too. A course of study, like a gentleman's gentleman, is worthless the moment it cannot be believed. And since promises will be made — the subject is too rich for a single hour, and I shall be obliged, repeatedly, to say the words we shall prove this later — I have opened a ledger. Every such promise is a debt, entered with its due date, and I intend to read the account aloud from time to time as the debts are issued and paid. No obligation will be quietly forgotten. If circumstances force a change of plan, you will hear of it from me directly.

Today's episode is the story of why the subject exists at all. It is a story in three crises, spread across two and a half millennia: a scandal in antiquity, discovered by a religious brotherhood and, if the legends are to be believed, suppressed by them; a machine, built in the seventeenth century, that worked miracles for two hundred years while nobody on earth could say why; and a claim about the flow of heat, made in the early nineteenth century, so productive and so alarming that mathematics had no choice but to tear itself down to the footings and rebuild. Along the way you will receive the first proof of the course — delivered whole, in the ear, because it is one of the few great proofs that fits there — and you will hear the season's syllabus presented as what it actually is, which is a drama with recurring characters, several of them monsters.

I shall ask for roughly an hour of your attention, sir, and I shall try to deserve it.

The Cult of Number

Our story begins in the Greek south of Italy, in the sixth century before the common era, with a community that was equal parts research school and religious order. Its founder, Pythagoras of Samos, is a figure so encrusted with legend that historians handle every sentence about him with tongs; the theorem that bears his name is demonstrably older than the man, tabulated by Babylonian scribes a thousand years before his birth. But the brotherhood he founded at Croton was real enough, and it held a doctrine that concerns us directly. The doctrine was this: all is number.

By number, the Pythagoreans meant the whole numbers — one, two, three, and their brethren — and the ratios between them. And they had, to be fair to them, excellent evidence. The most celebrated piece of it was musical. Take a stretched string and pluck it; it sounds a note. Stop the string at exactly half its length and pluck again: the note leaps up by an octave, the most perfect consonance the ear knows. Divide the string in the ratio of two to three, and you obtain the interval musicians call the fifth — the second most perfect. The discovery that harmony, of all things — the least material, most spiritual experience available to the senses — was governed by the ratios of small whole numbers, struck the brotherhood with the force of revelation. If the whole numbers ruled music, why not everything? The heavens, they concluded, were a tuned instrument; the planets in their courses sounded a music of the spheres, inaudible only because we have heard it since birth.

Now, a creed of that magnificence requires a mathematical foundation, and the foundation was the doctrine of commensurability. Two lengths are called commensurable when some common unit measures both of them exactly — when there exists a little measuring rod, perhaps very little indeed, that fits into the first length a whole number of times and into the second length a whole number of times, with nothing left over in either case. If such a rod exists, then the two lengths stand to one another as one whole number to another; their ratio is a fraction. The Pythagorean faith, stated precisely, was that any two lengths whatsoever are commensurable. Choose the unit small enough, the thinking ran, and it will measure anything. The world, surveyed finely enough, is whole numbers all the way down.

It is a beautiful creed, sir. It is also false, and the brotherhood itself discovered the falsehood — which is the sort of misfortune that gives research a bad name among the devout.

The discovery is attached, by tradition, to the name of Hippasus of Metapontum, a member of the order in the fifth century before the common era. The stories about what happened next are lurid and contradictory. In one version, Hippasus divulged the existence of incommensurable magnitudes to outsiders, and the gods, taking a professional interest in the brotherhood's secrets, drowned him at sea for the impiety. In another, the drowning was arranged by his colleagues, which at least has the merit of administrative plausibility. In a third, his offence was not the incommensurables at all but the divulging of a geometric construction — the sphere assembled from twelve pentagonal faces — that the order held sacred. I must be plain with you, sir: the drowning is legend. The sources are late, they disagree, and the tale has visibly improved in the telling, as tales do when they are good. Some scholars hold, on respectable evidence, that the first incommensurable magnitude was found not in the square but in the pentagon — the diagonal of a regular pentagon against its side — which would be a pleasing irony, the order's own sacred emblem harbouring the heresy. The history, in short, is fog.

The theorem is not fog. The theorem is as solid as anything our species possesses, and it says this: there exist two lengths — perfectly ordinary, constructible lengths, which you could draw in the dust with a stick — that no common unit measures. Not a small unit, not a microscopic unit, not any unit at all. The doctrine of commensurability is not approximately true, or true with exceptions. It is false, and provably false, and the proof was found some twenty-four centuries ago.

The two lengths in question are the side of a square and its diagonal. Let us go and look at them.

The Diagonal

Draw a square, sir — in the mind's eye will do — with each side one unit long. One what does not matter: one hand-span, one furlong; call it simply one. Now draw the diagonal, corner to opposite corner. The question on the table is ancient and childlike: how long is the diagonal, in the units of the side?

The Greeks could not answer with a number, but they could answer with a construction, and it is a construction so pretty that Plato has Socrates coax it, step by step, out of an unschooled slave boy in one of the dialogues — the point being that the knowledge was somehow in the boy all along, waiting to be led out. It goes as follows. Take your unit square and draw both of its diagonals; they cross at the centre and cut the square into four equal right-angled triangles, like a sandwich cut twice. Now build a new, larger square, tilted like a diamond, whose four sides are four copies of the diagonal. If you arrange the pieces — and I encourage you to do this on paper this evening; it is the work of a minute — you find that the tilted square is composed of exactly four of those triangles, while the original square was composed of exactly two. The square built on the diagonal is precisely twice the square built on the side.

Twice the area, sir. And here the elder theorem — the one wrongly credited to Pythagoras but at home in his brotherhood — says the same thing in general: the square on the longest side of a right triangle is the sum of the squares on the other two. One plus one is two. So whatever the diagonal's length may be, its square is two.

Let us give the creature its modern name. The length whose square is two is called the square root of two — root two, if you will permit me the familiar form hereafter, for we shall be seeing a great deal of one another. In decimal dress it begins one point four one four and continues in a manner I shall not attempt aloud. The Babylonians, ever practical, had a sexagesimal approximation of astonishing accuracy — good to about one part in a million — pressed into a clay tablet a millennium before the Greeks. Approximation was never the difficulty. The difficulty was the Pythagorean question: is root two a ratio? Is the diagonal commensurable with the side? Is there a fraction — some whole number p over some whole number q, perhaps with a numerator and denominator of grotesque size, but whole numbers nonetheless — whose square is exactly two?

Every measurement ever taken says the matter cannot be settled by measuring. A ruler reads to three digits, a laser to twelve; no instrument reads to infinitely many, and a fraction with a large enough denominator can hide below any instrument's resolution. If the question is to be settled at all, it must be settled by pure reason — and it must be settled in the negative by pure reason, which is a far stranger demand. To show a fraction exists, one may simply produce it. To show that no fraction whatsoever, among the infinitely many candidates, has a square equal to two — that requires a new kind of weapon entirely.

The Greeks possessed the weapon. It is time you saw it fired.

The First Impossible Thing

What follows is the first proof of this course, and I shall give it whole, because it is short, because it is perfect, and because it is the moment — as near as our species has a record of one — when humanity first proved a negative about numbers. I ask for your full attention for the next few minutes. There is no hurry. If a step blurs, the entire argument, dressed in formal clothes, awaits you in the first chapter of the Desk Volume; nothing depends on your catching every syllable at first pass. But I think you will catch it, sir. It was built to be caught.

The strategy is proof by contradiction — reduction to absurdity, as the schoolbooks render the old Latin. We wish to show that root two is not a fraction. Very well: let us suppose, for the sake of argument, that it is one, and follow the supposition wherever it leads. If it leads us, by impeccable steps, into a flat impossibility — a statement that cannot be true on any terms — then the supposition itself must have been rotten, and root two is no fraction. One assumes the thing one despises, sir, and escorts it politely to its own destruction.

Suppose, then, that root two is a ratio of whole numbers: p over q, where p and q are positive whole numbers. We are entitled to one piece of housekeeping before we begin: we may take the fraction in lowest terms — that is, with all common factors already cancelled, so that no whole number greater than one divides both p and q. Any fraction can be brought to lowest terms by cancelling until nothing is left to cancel. I note in passing — because I undertook to be honest with you — that even this innocent-looking step conceals a small theorem: how do we know the cancelling ever finishes? It does, and the reason is a principle called well-ordering, and the two-line argument is in the Desk Volume, where you may inspect it. For now: p over q, in lowest terms. Hold on to that phrase, lowest terms. The entire proof balances upon it.

Now. If root two equals p over q, then squaring both sides tells us that two equals p squared over q squared. Multiply both sides by q squared and we have it in the cleanest possible form: p squared equals two times q squared.

Look at that equation, sir. The right-hand side is two times a whole number. So p squared is an even number.

What does that tell us about p itself? Here we need a small lemma — a servant-lemma, hired for one task: if p squared is even, then p is even. And the neatest way to see it is to prove the contrapositive, a manoeuvre I confess openly because you will use it constantly: instead of proving that an even square forces an even root, we prove that an odd number always has an odd square — which is the same claim wearing its coat inside out. If a statement is true, so is its contrapositive, and the reverse; logic guarantees they stand or fall together. So: suppose p is odd. Every odd number is twice something plus one — that is what odd means. Square it, and multiply it out as the schoolroom taught you: twice-something-plus-one, times itself, gives four times the something squared, plus four times the something, plus one. The first two pieces are visibly even — they are multiples of four — so the total is an even quantity plus one. Odd. There it is: odd numbers have odd squares, always, no exceptions. Contrapositive granted: since p squared is even, p cannot be odd. P is even.

So p is twice some whole number; give the half a name and call it r, so that p equals two r. Substitute that into our clean equation. P squared is two r, squared — which is four r squared. So four r squared equals two q squared. Divide both sides by two: two r squared equals q squared.

But look at what the equation has become, sir. It says q squared is two times a whole number. Q squared is even. And our servant-lemma, still in the room, performs the same office again: q is even.

And now the trap closes. P is even. Q is even. Then two divides both of them — but we took the fraction in lowest terms, with no common factor greater than one. The supposition that root two is a fraction has walked us, in perhaps a dozen honest steps, into a statement that is false on its face: a fraction in lowest terms whose top and bottom are both even. There is no such object. The supposition is destroyed, and with it the Pythagorean creed. Root two is not a ratio of whole numbers. The diagonal and the side of the square are incommensurable: no unit, however small, measures both. Some lengths are simply not fractions, and the world is not whole numbers all the way down.

Notice, sir — before the glow fades — what the proof did not do. It did not examine any fractions. It did not compute. It touched, in one motion, every fraction that exists or could exist: the candidate with a denominator of ten, the candidate with a denominator of ten to the millionth power, candidates no computer could store — all of them dispatched at once, by pure structure. No measurement, no instrument, no search, however long, could ever have established this; a search can fail forever without proving failure was necessary. And a certain kind of mind will notice something else: what we have here is an impossibility proof — a demonstration that no object of a certain description exists, delivered not by inspection but by showing the description is self-destroying. If that species of argument feels familiar from your own trade, sir, from the celebrated impossibilities of computation, I shall only say: it should, the family resemblance is real, and in two episodes' time I shall make the kinship exact, on your own hardware. For now, savour the original. Two and a half thousand years old, and not a bolt loose.

Aristotle, writing a century and more after the discovery, reaches for precisely this argument when his treatise on reasoning requires a specimen of proof through impossibility — the diagonal is incommensurable, he says, because if you suppose otherwise, odd numbers come out equal to even ones. It was already the classic. It has never stopped being the classic.

And since the weapon is in our hands and still warm, permit me a two-minute encore from the same armoury, because it may be the only other proof in mathematics of comparable age and comparable perfection: Euclid's theorem that the primes never run out. Suppose you have gathered any finite collection of prime numbers whatsoever, and you fear it may be all of them. Multiply your entire collection together, and to the product add one. This new number, when divided by any prime on your list, leaves a remainder of exactly one — the division cannot come out even. But every whole number greater than one has at least one prime divisor — a fact whose short proof rests, once again, on well-ordering, and is filed in the Desk Volume beside its cousins. So the new number has a prime divisor, and that prime is a stranger to your list. Note the fine print, sir, for it is the most commonly misremembered clause in mathematics: the new number need not be prime itself; it merely harbours a prime you did not have. No finite list of primes is ever complete. The primes are infinite, and it takes four sentences to know it forever.

Two proofs, then, sir, in one sitting — the impossibility and the infinitude — and between them they mark the moment mathematics became mathematics: the moment it stopped being a catalogue of measurements and calculations, and became the art of establishing, by finite reason, truths about the infinite that no amount of looking could ever certify. That is the wonder I promised you this hour, delivered. I shall not gild it. But do notice, before we move on, that the triumph arrived wearing mourning. The first great proof in history is a proof that the reigning theory of the universe was wrong — a demolition, performed by the faithful, upon their own temple. The Greeks looked at what they had proved, and flinched.

The Retreat into Geometry

What does a civilization do when it discovers that its concept of number is too small for the world — that the diagonal of a square, an object you can draw, corresponds to no number it recognizes? One option, the brave one, is to enlarge the concept of number: to declare that there shall be new numbers, irrational ones, and to say precisely what such a thing is and how to compute with it. That option was taken, sir — in the eighteen seventies of our era, some twenty-three centuries after the crisis. Rather a long time between the diagnosis and the cure, you will agree. It is one of the longest outstanding tickets in intellectual history, and the closing of it is the business of our next episode.

The Greeks took the other option. They retreated — though it must be said at once that no retreat in history was ever conducted with such genius. If lengths cannot all be numbers, they reasoned, then let us stop pretending; let geometry, not arithmetic, be the true language of magnitude. A length is a length; one does not ask for its decimal expansion, one asks how it stands in proportion to other lengths. And the man who made that honest was Eudoxus, in the fourth century before the common era — in my estimation, and not only mine, the finest mathematician of antiquity after Archimedes, and the author of two ideas so far ahead of their time that the modern era had to reinvent both.

The first idea is his theory of proportion, preserved as the fifth book of Euclid's Elements. The problem: how can one say that two ratios of magnitudes are equal — that the diagonal stands to the side as this line stands to that one — when the ratios in question correspond to no fraction? Eudoxus's answer is a stroke of genius disguised as pedantry. Compare the ratio against every fraction, he says. Take any whole numbers m and n you please, and ask of the first ratio: is it larger than m over n, equal to it, or smaller? The fraction m over n acts as a measuring probe. Two ratios shall be called equal precisely when every probe reports the same of both — when every fraction that undershoots the one undershoots the other, and every fraction that overshoots the one overshoots the other. A magnitude, in other words, is to be known by the company it keeps among the fractions: by the exact manner in which it splits the world of fractions into those below it and those above.

File that phrase away, sir — known by the company it keeps among the fractions. Twenty-two centuries after Eudoxus, in eighteen fifty-eight, a German mathematician named Richard Dedekind, dissatisfied with his own calculus lectures, will pick up precisely this idea, turn it over in his hands, and realize that what Eudoxus used as a test for equality can be used as a definition of number itself. The hole in the rationals where root two ought to be — the very cut that divides the fractions into two camps — will become root two. We shall be present at that audacity in the next episode; Dedekind is on our syllabus, and Eudoxus is the reason he will look, on arrival, strangely familiar. It is the longest assist in the history of thought.

The second Eudoxan idea is the method of exhaustion, and it is nothing less than the rigorous half of the calculus, arriving two millennia before the other half. Here is the flavour. Suppose you wish to know the area of a curved region — a circle, say, or a slice of a parabola. Curves resist rulers. But you can inscribe within the region a polygon — all straight edges, whose area you can compute honestly — and then a finer polygon, and finer still, consuming ever more of the region, exhausting it. The areas of the polygons form an increasing procession of known quantities, creeping up on the unknown. The Greeks then finished the argument with an austere double stroke: to prove the curved area equals such-and-such, show that assuming it larger leads to contradiction, and assuming it smaller leads to contradiction as well. Two impossibilities, and the value is cornered. No infinite process is ever declared complete; infinity is approached, never touched — the Greeks, after Zeno, of whom more shortly, handled the infinite as one handles a loaded firearm.

The virtuoso of the method was Archimedes of Syracuse, and I permit myself one example, because it may be the single most astonishing computation of the ancient world. Take a parabola — the curve of a thrown stone — and slice it with a straight line, producing a curved segment like a drawn bow. Archimedes proved that the area of that segment is exactly four thirds the area of the largest triangle you can inscribe in it. Exactly four thirds, sir. Not approximately; the double-contradiction closes both doors. He did it by filling the segment with an infinite cascade of triangles — the big one, then two smaller in the gaps, then four smaller still — each generation contributing a quarter of the one before, and he needed, in effect, to sum that endless procession of quarters, and did, with complete rigour, in the third century before the common era. And in a private treatise addressed to his friend Eratosthenes, he confessed the workshop secret: he first found such results by a mechanical method — imagining the shapes as physical objects, weighing them against each other on an idealized balance, infinitesimal slice by infinitesimal slice — and only afterward built the exhaustion proof, because, he said plainly, the mechanical method demonstrates nothing, though it shows where the truth lies. Discovery by one method, certification by another, and total clarity about which was which: there is the honesty clause of this course, sir, practised twenty-two centuries before I adopted it. That treatise, the Method, was lost for most of recorded history and recovered only in nineteen hundred six, in Constantinople, hiding in plain sight beneath a book of prayers — the parchment had been scraped and overwritten by a medieval copyist, and the mathematics was read at last beneath the liturgy. Civilization came within one energetic monk of never knowing how Archimedes actually thought.

So the retreat into geometry was no rout; it produced proportion theory and exhaustion, tools of complete rigour that would not be surpassed until the nineteenth century. But a retreat it remained, and the bill for it ran long. Number and magnitude, arithmetic and geometry, were now estranged — and so the algebra of the continuum, the thing you and I will spend this season building, sat unbuilt. For two thousand years, sir, if you wished to speak precisely about continuous quantity, you were obliged to do it in the dialect of triangles.

Zeno, Taken Seriously

Before we leave antiquity, there is a gentleman waiting in the anteroom, and he has been waiting, with perfect composure, for about twenty-five centuries. Zeno of Elea, a philosopher of the fifth century before the common era, composed a book of paradoxes concerning motion and the continuum. The book is lost; we know its arguments chiefly through Aristotle, who quotes them in order to refute them — a service, I would observe, roughly equivalent to preserving a rival's finest speeches in the act of heckling. Fashion has treated Zeno as a purveyor of parlour tricks, quibbles for undergraduates to dissolve. Fashion, sir, is mistaken. The paradoxes are serious questions about what a continuum is, and this course regards them as an unpaid invoice. Let me read you three items from it.

The first is called the Dichotomy. To walk across the room to the far wall, you must first cover half the distance. Then half of what remains. Then half of what remains after that — and however many stages you complete, a remainder survives, forever halved and never abolished. The journey decomposes into infinitely many stages, each requiring some time, however brief. How does one complete infinitely many acts, one after another, in a finite afternoon? And yet you cross rooms daily, sir, without visible strain.

The second is Achilles and the tortoise, the celebrity of the collection. Achilles, fleetest of the Greeks, grants the tortoise a head start and gives chase. By the time he reaches the tortoise's starting point, the animal has crawled somewhat ahead. By the time he covers that new gap, it has advanced again — less, but not nothing. Whenever Achilles arrives where the tortoise was, the tortoise is somewhere it wasn't. The pursuit resolves into infinitely many episodes of catching-up, in every one of which the tortoise leads. How, then, does Achilles ever draw level? And yet the fast overtake the slow on any racecourse in the world.

Now, the modern reflex — and I suspect it is twitching in you already, sir — is to answer with a sum. The stages of the Dichotomy take a half, plus a quarter, plus an eighth of the total time, and so on forever; and that endless sum, we are taught, comes to exactly one. The infinitely many stages fit, with nothing to spare, inside one finite crossing. Achilles's catching-up episodes likewise sum to a finite time, at the expiry of which he is level, and thereafter the tortoise sees only heels. This answer is correct. I wish to be entirely fair to it: it is correct. But observe what it presumes. What is the sum of infinitely many numbers? You cannot add them one at a time and finish — that is precisely Zeno's point turned back upon the arithmetic. To say the endless sum equals one is to say something quite subtle: that the running totals — a half, then three quarters, then seven eighths — approach one, come within any tolerance of it and stay there, and that we have agreed to call that approaching having a sum. The answer to Zeno, in other words, leans its whole weight on the concept of a limit — and the concept of a limit is the very thing under interrogation, the thing neither Zeno nor anyone else for twenty-two centuries after him could define. The geometric series does not dissolve the paradox, sir. It relocates it, into the foundations of a subject that did not yet exist. An answer that presumes the thing in question is not yet an answer. It is an intention to answer.

We shall make good on the intention. In episode four of this season, the limit of a sequence will be defined with complete precision — it is, I warn you now, the hardest small thing in mathematics, and we shall take our time — and the sum of an infinite series will be built upon it, honestly, and Achilles will be discharged from his labours with the paperwork in order. Into the ledger it goes: Zeno's account, due in episode four.

The third paradox cuts deeper still, and I shall leave it deliberately unresolved today. The Arrow. Consider an arrow in flight, and consider it at one single instant — a durationless now. In that instant, the arrow occupies a region of space exactly its own size; it has, within the instant, no time in which to move. It is, at that instant, at rest — indistinguishable, frozen there, from an arrow hovering motionless at the same spot. But the flight is nothing but its instants. If the arrow is at rest in every one of them, whence the motion? Where does the flying live, if it lives in no instant? Do not let the antique furniture fool you, sir: this is a precise question, and it is aimed at the heart of the calculus we are about to watch being invented. What is the velocity of a thing at an instant? Velocity is distance covered per time elapsed — and in a single instant, no distance is covered and no time elapses. Zero over zero. The whole of the differential calculus is an attempt to make sense of exactly that phrase, and the honest making-sense-of-it is the derivative, which receives its rigorous definition in episode seven. The Arrow is entered in the ledger beside its sibling. Two and a half thousand years, sir, between the filing of a question and its lawful answer. One can only admire the gentleman's patience.

The Machine That Worked

We now vault across a great deal of history — the Alexandrians, the Indian and Islamic mathematicians who kept the flame and improved the arithmetic, the medieval schoolmen; history will forgive the vault, or at any rate I shall apologize to her later — and arrive in England in the plague years of sixteen sixty-five and sixty-six. Cambridge University has closed its doors against the pestilence, and a twenty-three-year-old fellow of Trinity College has withdrawn to his mother's farm in Lincolnshire with nothing but time. Over roughly eighteen months, Isaac Newton invents the mathematics of change — he calls it the method of fluxions — works out the foundations of a theory of gravitation, and decomposes white light with a prism, any one of which would have secured his immortality. He then, being Newton, publishes none of it, and spends much of the next decade on alchemy and scriptural chronology. Genius, sir, is not obliged to be reasonable.

A decade later, independently, the German philosopher and diplomat Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz builds the same engine in different livery, and Leibniz — a man who thought in notation the way musicians think in keys — dresses it in the symbols the world still uses: his elongated letter for summation, his d for a difference infinitely small. Newton conceived quantities as flowing in time, their velocities his fluxions; Leibniz conceived curves as polygons with infinitely many infinitely short sides. Two costumes, one machine. Leibniz publishes first, in sixteen eighty-four, in a paper so compressed that even the Bernoullis — the formidable Swiss family who became the calculus's first power users — described reading it as an enigma rather than an explanation. Newton's system reaches print later, embedded in his Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy of sixteen eighty-seven, arguably the most consequential book ever published in the sciences.

What does the machine do? Two things, sir, which reveal themselves — this is the machine's deepest secret — to be one thing run in opposite directions. The first: given the whole history of a changing quantity, find its instantaneous rate of change. Not average speed over an hour — the speed now, at this instant; the steepness of a curve at a point; Zeno's forbidden quantity. The second: given the rate at every instant, recover the accumulated total — the area under a curve, the distance from the speeds, the whole from the rates. That these two problems undo each other — that steepness-finding and area-finding are inverse operations, so that every hard-won area formula is a rate formula read backward — is the fundamental theorem of the calculus. Newton and Leibniz both saw it; neither could prove it in any sense we would today accept; we shall state it honestly and prove it in episode eight, as the summit of the season. In the meantime the machine ran, and what it did to natural philosophy can scarcely be overstated. Kepler had spent years of labour wringing three planetary laws out of decades of observations. With the calculus, Newton derived the ellipses of the planets from one law of attraction — derived Kepler, sir, the way one derives a corollary — and went on to the tides, the flattening of the earth, the wobble of its axis, the paths of comets. It was not a good tool. It was the greatest instrument of understanding our species had ever built, and every subsequent machine of consequence, up to and including the ones you train, sir, has the calculus somewhere in its ancestry.

And nobody — I choose the words with care — nobody on earth knew why it worked. Let me show you the crack in the foundation, because you can see the whole of it in one small computation, done Newton's way. We ask: at what rate does the square of a quantity grow? Take x, and take x squared, and let us nudge x — increase it by some small amount. Newton wrote the nudge with a little letter of his own; I shall simply call it the nudge, and you will see in the Desk Volume why the name is safer than the letter. The new value of the square is x plus the nudge, all squared; multiplied out, that is x squared, plus twice x times the nudge, plus the nudge squared. Subtract the original x squared: the growth is twice x times the nudge, plus the nudge squared. Divide by the nudge to get the rate of growth per unit of nudge: twice x, plus the nudge. And now — watch the hands closely, sir — now the nudge, having served, is declared negligible and discarded, and the answer is proclaimed: the square grows at the rate twice x.

The answer is right. The procedure is scandalous. To divide by the nudge, the nudge had to be a nonzero quantity — division by zero being no division at all. To vanish from the final answer, the nudge had to be zero. Within four lines it was both, as convenience dictated. Is the nudge something, or nothing? If something, the answer twice x is merely approximate, polluted by a leftover nudge. If nothing, the division upstream was fraud. The practitioners, pressed, spoke of quantities infinitely small — smaller than any assignable magnitude yet not zero — evanescent increments, ghostlike beings that existed just long enough to be divided by and then decently expired. Leibniz, the philosopher, offered several stories over the years and settled at last on the admirably frank position that infinitesimals were convenient fictions — scaffolding that could in principle be replaced by honest exhaustion-style arguments, though nobody, including Leibniz, ever replaced it. Newton, in the Principia, gestured toward limits — first and last ratios of quantities just coming into or going out of existence — language tantalizingly close to the truth and maddeningly short of a definition. The machine worked. The manual was mysticism.

I cannot leave the seventeenth century, sir, without two footnotes of a more human colour. The first: Newton and Leibniz, and their partisan nations, descended into the ugliest priority dispute in the history of science, complete with accusations of plagiarism, anonymous pamphlets, and stacked committees. The Royal Society empanelled a commission to adjudicate, and found for Newton — its own president. The commission's report, it later emerged, was drafted by Newton himself. I offer no comment, sir, beyond observing that peer review has had its imperfections from the start. The second footnote concerns the first calculus textbook ever published, in sixteen ninety-six, under the name of the Marquis de L'Hôpital — a French nobleman of genuine ability and greater income. The rule for evaluating zero-over-zero expressions that every student learns as L'Hôpital's rule was in fact discovered by his tutor, Johann Bernoulli, who had contracted — for a generous monthly retainer — to communicate his mathematical discoveries to the Marquis for the Marquis's own use. The theorem, in short, was purchased, sir; there is no more dignified word. When the book proved a triumph, Bernoulli complained loudly of the arrangement he had happily signed. I confine myself to noting that in mathematics, unlike tailoring, the receipt does not transfer the fit.

The Bishop Files His Report

The eighteenth century inherited the machine, and the machine kept working — indeed it conquered. And precisely because it conquered, the foundational crack drew flies. The definitive complaint arrived in seventeen thirty-four, from a quarter mathematics did not expect: George Berkeley — philosopher of immaterialism, Bishop of Cloyne in Ireland, and a controversialist of the first water — published a pamphlet titled The Analyst, addressed, as its subtitle announced, to an infidel mathematician. Tradition, on good circumstantial grounds, identifies the infidel as Edmond Halley, the astronomer of comet fame and a cheerful freethinker who was said to have talked a dying man out of his religion. Berkeley's grievance was not with the calculus as such; it was with mathematicians who mocked faith for resting on mysteries while their own celebrated science rested, at its very foundation, on mysteries at least as dark. His pamphlet undertakes to press that charge home in detail — and the detail, sir, is devastating.

Berkeley walks through the fluxional computations — essentially the one you and I performed a moment ago — and asks, with lethal politeness, the question we asked: is the increment something or nothing? He anatomizes the double game: the nudge is nonzero when divided by, zero when discarded, and, quote, all which seems a most inconsistent way of arguing. Of the fluxions themselves — the velocities of evanescent increments — he asks what manner of beings they are: not finite quantities, not quantities infinitely small, not yet nothing. And then he delivers the most famous phrase ever aimed at mathematics: may we not call them the ghosts of departed quantities? He twists the knife with equal elegance in the theological direction: a man who can digest a second or third fluxion, he suggests, need not, methinks, be squeamish about any point in divinity. And — this is the part that raises the pamphlet from polemic to criticism of the first rank — Berkeley grants, indeed insists, that the calculus gets right answers, and offers a diagnosis: the standard procedures commit two errors, of opposite sign and equal size, which cancel. You arrive at the truth, he says, though not by science: by virtue of a twofold mistake. In several classical cases his compensating-errors analysis can be made to stand up. The man did not merely find the bug, sir. He proposed a mechanism for why the tests were passing anyway.

You will permit me a professional opinion, formed over long service: The Analyst is the finest hostile code review in history. Every substantive objection in it was correct. The response of the profession was, in the traditional manner, to attack the reviewer's motives, publish rebuttals that misunderstood the objection, and continue shipping. Some of the rebuttals — Maclaurin's, notably — were serious work that improved matters at the margins; none reached the root. The ticket Berkeley opened in seventeen thirty-four remained open, sir, for over a century. It was not closed until Weierstrass, whom you will meet within the hour. Let no one tell you that review is thankless work; it is merely slow to be thanked.

The Age of Nerve

Here, sir, the story takes a turn that a moralist would not have scripted. The foundations had been publicly demolished and everybody knew it. The sensible course was to stop and repair them. What the eighteenth century did instead was accelerate — and the age of unsound foundations became the most gloriously productive age mathematics had yet seen. The lesson is uncomfortable and true: rigour is not always the engine of discovery; sometimes it is the audit that comes after the fortune is made.

The age has a presiding genius, and his name is Leonhard Euler — a Swiss, the pupil of the Bernoullis, and quite possibly the most prolific mathematician who has ever lived. His collected works run to some eight dozen large volumes and the editing, begun in nineteen hundred eleven, occupied the better part of a century. The astronomer Arago said of him — the remark is famous, and I let it stand on Arago's authority — that he calculated as men breathe, as eagles sustain themselves in the air. He lost the sight of one eye in his thirties, and of both late in life, whereupon — he is said to have remarked — he would now have fewer distractions; his output, sustained by a preternatural memory and a household of amanuenses, did not fall. He wrote on everything — mechanics, optics, shipbuilding, lotteries, music — and he wielded the infinite with a nerve, sir, that no one before or since has quite matched.

One exhibit will serve, and it is the most celebrated: the Basel problem, named for the Swiss city, home of the Bernoullis, where the question had defeated the best for ninety years. Add up the reciprocals of the square numbers: one, plus a quarter, plus a ninth, plus a sixteenth, and so on forever. The sum visibly settles toward something in the neighbourhood of one and two thirds — but toward what, exactly? In seventeen thirty-five, Euler, not yet thirty, announced the answer, and the answer is one of the strangest single facts in mathematics: the sum is pi squared over six. Pi, sir. The circle constant, materializing uninvited from the arithmetic of square numbers, with not a circle in sight. How did he find it? By an argument of magnificent illegality. The sine curve, he reasoned, vanishes at whole-number multiples of half-turns — so treat it as if it were a polynomial of infinite degree and factor it by its roots, one factor per crossing, exactly as one factors a quadratic. Then multiply the infinite product back out and compare coefficients with the known series for the sine. Out falls pi squared over six. Nothing in that argument was licensed. Polynomials are finite objects; nobody had established that a function is entitled to be reassembled from its zeros — indeed for general functions it is false. Euler knew the argument was exposed; he checked the value numerically to many digits — the accountant verifying the miracle — and in later years supplied genuinely rigorous derivations. And the factorization itself, the crime, was eventually made lawful: a century and a half later Weierstrass — that name again, sir; he is this story's locksmith — established exactly when such infinite factorizations are valid. Euler's instinct was so good that mathematics rebuilt itself to make him retroactively honest. That, and not carelessness, is why we forgive the age its sins: its sins kept turning out to be theorems.

But not always, sir. Not always — and the age knew it, and mostly declined to look. Consider the innocent-seeming series: one, minus one, plus one, minus one, and so on forever. Stop after an odd number of terms and the total is one; after an even number, zero. The running totals never settle; by any honest reckoning the series has no sum. Yet the age wanted it to have one. Substitute into a certain well-known formula and the value one half drops out; the Italian priest Guido Grandi, who studied the series at the turn of the eighteenth century, is said to have seen in the equation of nothing with one half a mathematical image of the world's creation from nothing. Leibniz endorsed the half on probabilistic grounds — the total is one or zero with equal frequency, so split the difference — reasoning, sir, that I can only describe as the arithmetic of a gentleman at the roulette table. I must, in fairness, complete the story: there exist in the modern era rigorous theories of summation — definitions, theorems, the full apparatus — under which one half is precisely and provably the right value to assign that series, and such methods do honest work in physics. But they are theories, sir, with stated definitions; the eighteenth century had the answers without the definitions, and to have answers without definitions is to have moods. By eighteen twenty-six the reckoning was overdue, and the young Norwegian Niels Henrik Abel wrote in a letter that divergent series were the invention of the devil, and that it was shameful to base any demonstration upon them — the cry, sir, of a rigorous soul born into a lax age, and a sign that the age was ending.

One more witness, and we may move to the catastrophe that forced the issue. Joseph-Louis Lagrange — the greatest analyst of the generation after Euler — was troubled enough by the foundations to attempt, in seventeen ninety-seven, a complete reconstruction: banish infinitesimals, banish limits, banish geometry; found the calculus purely on the algebra of infinite power series. The attempt failed — it assumed, roughly, that every function is the sum of its own Taylor series, which is false, though the decisive counterexample lay a generation in the future — but the failure is instructive twice over. It shows the discomfort had reached the summit of the profession; and it shows the summit of the profession still believed all functions were, at bottom, tame — expressible by formulas, expandable in series, well-bred. That belief, sir, was about to be dismantled by a study of, of all things, the flow of heat through iron.

The Heat of the Matter

The third crisis of our story has a protagonist with a biography no novelist would dare submit. Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier, orphaned son of a tailor, schooled by monks, narrowly escaped the guillotine during the Terror — arrested twice, saved in part by the fall of Robespierre — then joined Napoleon Bonaparte's expedition to Egypt as a scientific savant, ran half the administration of the occupied country, and returned to be appointed by Napoleon as prefect of an Alpine department, governing from Grenoble, where he drained swamps, built roads, and, in the hours left over, revolutionized mathematics. He is said to have kept his rooms tropically overheated, having contracted in Egypt the conviction that heat was health. The conviction was prophetic, sir, if not medically then professionally: his masterpiece is the theory of heat, and his weapon in building it detonated under mathematics itself. The memoir arrived at the Institute in Paris in eighteen hundred seven; the definitive book, The Analytical Theory of Heat, in eighteen twenty-two.

Let me build his problem for you from nothing, because it narrates beautifully and because this course presumes no physics whatsoever. Picture a thin iron rod, insulated along its length, whose two ends are held at the temperature of ice — call that temperature zero. At the stroke of noon the rod carries some arbitrary pattern of heat along its length — hot here, cool there; imagine the temperature graphed above the rod as a curve, a profile, a skyline. The question: how does that profile evolve as time passes?

Two facts of common experience govern everything; grant me these and we owe nature nothing further. First: heat flows from hotter toward colder, and it flows faster where the temperature changes more steeply along the rod — a fierce difference across a short span drives a strong flow, a gentle gradation drives a trickle. Second: the temperature at a spot rises when more heat flows into it than out of it, and falls in the contrary case — a ledger of arrivals and departures, nothing more. Put the two together and watch what they imply. At the crest of a bump in the profile, heat is flowing away down both slopes and nothing warmer stands above to resupply it: crests must fall. At the bottom of a dip, heat pours in from both sides: troughs must fill. The profile, in short, relaxes — sharp features soften, the skyline slumps toward the flat line of ice at the ends. Everyday experience, rendered as geometry: the curvature of the profile drives its change.

Fourier now asks the mathematician's question: are there special profiles that evolve simply — shapes that do not shuffle and deform, but merely fade in place? There are, and you know their shape from a plucked string: the sine arch. A single soft arch spanning the rod from end to end, zero at both ice-bound ends, decays without changing form — the whole arch subsides uniformly, like a tent settling. So does the wave of two arches — one up, one down — and the wave of three, and so on through the whole family of ever-wigglier waves that fit the rod with zeros at the ends. And they fade at wonderfully different rates, and the rule is the essence of the physics: the rate goes as the square of the number of arches. The two-arch wave dies four times as fast as the single arch; the three-arch wave, nine times as fast; a wave of ten arches is extinguished a hundred times faster than the fundamental. Why? Because more arches in the same rod means steeper slopes and tighter curvature — fiercer flows over shorter distances. Fine wiggles are hot gossip, sir: intensely exchanged, and gone by morning. Broad features are the slow news of the rod, and they linger. Hold that principle; you will meet it in every corner of applied mathematics for the rest of your days, not least in why the crude features of a signal survive every blur while the detail perishes first.

Now comes the leap — the sentence for which Fourier is immortal and for which his century very nearly disowned him. If each wave in the family evolves so simply, then any profile built by adding waves together — so much of the first arch, plus so much of the two-arch wave, plus so much of the third — evolves just as simply: each ingredient fades at its own rate, and the sum of the fading ingredients is the fading sum. Splendid, if your profile happens to be a chord of sine waves. And Fourier says: every profile is. Any initial temperature distribution whatsoever — smooth or jagged, drawn by formula or drawn freehand, a profile with corners, a profile that leaps in a broken step from cold to hot — any of them, he claims, can be written as a sum, generally an infinite sum, of those pure waves. And he does not stop at the claim, sir; he supplies the recipe. To find how much of the seventh wave your profile contains, multiply your profile by that seventh wave, point by point along the rod, and take the average — an integral. The wave in question resonates with its own kind in the profile and averages everything else away, like a tuning fork that answers only its own note in a roomful of noise. Decompose the profile into its waves by the recipe; let each wave fade at its own square-law rate; reassemble at any later hour you please: the evolution of heat, solved — not for one profile, for all of them, forever. It is one of the most powerful single ideas in the history of the exact sciences, and its descendants are in your telephone, your image models, and very possibly, sir, in whatever you shipped last quarter.

And it set off every alarm in mathematics at once. Hear the alarms as the professionals heard them. First: the recipe delivers infinitely many ingredients, so the reassembly is an infinite sum — not of numbers now, but of functions, of whole curves. What does it mean to add up infinitely many curves? Does the sum arrive anywhere? Nobody could say; the definition did not exist. Second, and worse: each wave in the family is perfectly smooth — the gentlest object in geometry. Fourier claims a sum of them can equal a profile with a corner, or a broken step. Smoothness piled on smoothness yielding a jag? Every instinct of the age — Lagrange's articulate instinct above all — said a sum inherits the good breeding of its terms; a sum of the smooth is smooth. That instinct, sir, is wrong, and the corpse of it is buried under the whole of modern analysis — but the funeral takes most of this season, and the general question of when a property survives an infinite summation will pursue us to the very end of season two. Third: the recipe itself demands the integral — the average — of an arbitrary function. For the polite formula-functions of Euler's day, integration was understood well enough; but Fourier is claiming dominion over any profile whatsoever, and what, pray, is the integral of an arbitrary function? What even is a function, if it need not be given by a formula? And fourth, quietly, the question that would prove deepest of all: could two genuinely different profiles dissolve into the very same waves? If so the decomposition is ambiguous and the whole edifice trembles. Uniqueness, sir. Remember uniqueness; it has a destiny.

The old guard did what old guards do. Lagrange, the grandest survivor of the great age, objected in committee to the eighteen hundred seven memoir — on grounds of rigour, and by the standards of rigour then available to him, sir, I am bound to say his objection was not the folly it is sometimes painted; the manuscript was denied publication. The Institute then, with the exquisite passive aggression of learned bodies, set the propagation of heat as its grand prize problem; Fourier submitted, and in eighteen twelve the committee — Lagrange among the judges — awarded him the prize while attaching a censure to it: the manner in which the author arrives at his equations, they wrote in substance, leaves something to be desired in rigour and even in generality. A prize with a reprimand pinned to its ribbon. Fourier pocketed both, waited out the politics, and in eighteen twenty-two, by then permanent secretary of the very Academy that had censured him, published the Analytical Theory of Heat, censure unaddressed, claims intact. I confess a certain admiration for the sheer nerve of it, sir — though as a professional matter I must side, and this entire course sides, with the committee. The claims required proof. The proofs required a new mathematics.

And the new mathematics came, and here is the astonishing accounting of it, the reason this section is the hinge of the whole hour: nearly everything this course will build, sir, was built to answer Fourier. Trace the lines with me. In eighteen twenty-nine the young Dirichlet — the first mathematician, it was said, to truly master Fourier's book — produced the first honest convergence theorem: for any function of respectable habits — finitely many wiggles, finitely many jumps — the wave-sum does converge, and at each point of the rod it converges to the right value; at a jump, delightfully, it lands exactly midway between the two sides, splitting the difference like a diplomat. That theorem is a landmark twice over: once for what it proved, and once for how — it is among the first theorems in history about all functions of a described class rather than about a formula in hand. And in the very same paper, almost as an aside, Dirichlet exhibited a function so utterly deranged that his theorem — anyone's theorem, any integral then conceivable — could not touch it: a function I decline to describe today, sir, because it deserves, and will receive, a proper theatrical entrance in episode six. Consider it the first monster formally promised. Note it in the ledger.

The line continues. In eighteen fifty-four, Bernhard Riemann, preparing his qualifying essay at Göttingen — an essay on trigonometric series, on Fourier's waves — found he could not even pose the convergence questions precisely without first answering the prior scandal: what is the integral of an arbitrary function? His answer is the Riemann integral, the very one you learned as the limit of rectangle-sums, sir; it was born as a lemma in the service of Fourier, and this season crowns it in episode eight — and then, in the same episode, shows you the cracks in the crown, for the season ends with the Riemann integral failing, publicly, at the hands of Dirichlet's monster. And one line more, the longest: some fifteen years on, a young analyst at a provincial German university took up the fourth alarm — uniqueness; whether two different profiles could share all their waves — proved they cannot for well-behaved convergence, and then pressed at the edges: what if the convergence misbehaves on a few exceptional points? On a scattered dust of points? How much exception can uniqueness survive? He found he needed, and did not have, a theory of sets of points — of which infinite dusts are negligible and which are substantial. His name was Georg Cantor, sir, and that is how set theory — the theory of the infinite itself, the mathematics that episode three of this season is made of — entered the world: through the tradesman's entrance, carrying Fourier's luggage.

So tally the invoice of the claim of eighteen hundred seven: the modern concept of a function; the honest theory of convergence; the Riemann integral; set theory; and, in the fullness of season two, the Lebesgue integral, which was invented in no small part because Fourier's recipe deserved an integral worthy of it. One physical insolence, sir — any profile, as a chord of waves — and mathematics spent a century growing large enough to contain it. We shall be collecting instalments on this debt for sixteen episodes; I record it in the ledger as the largest single account we hold.

The Great Rebuild

The rebuild, when it came, was the work of the nineteenth century, and it proceeded — as rebuilds do, sir — unevenly, with heroes out of sequence.

The first hero arrived before the demand did. In eighteen seventeen, in Prague, a Catholic priest and philosopher named Bernard Bolzano published a pamphlet whose title announced a purely analytic proof of the theorem that a continuous curve passing from below a line to above it must somewhere cross it. Purely analytic, sir: no appeal to pictures, no talk of motion — the intermediate value theorem earned from the definitions alone, decades before the profession understood that it needed earning. Bolzano defined continuity essentially as we define it today, put his finger on the completeness of the real line as the load-bearing fact, and was read by almost no one; provincial, politically suspect to the imperial authorities, and published in the wrong journals, he haunts this subject as its great anticipator. History, I am afraid, keeps untidy files. We shall salute him properly when his theorem takes the stage in episode six.

The rebuild in earnest begins with Augustin-Louis Cauchy, engineer, royalist, and professor at the Polytechnic School in Paris, whose Course of Analysis of eighteen twenty-one is the founding textbook of rigour. Cauchy took the word limit — which had drifted through a century of prefaces as an apology — and made it the working foundation: the derivative defined as a limit of quotients, the integral as a limit of sums, the sum of a series as the limit of its partial totals, convergence subjected to tests that could be checked rather than felt. The nudge, in Cauchy's hands, is neither something nor nothing; it is a variable, and the question is what its quotient approaches. This is the right idea, sir, and Cauchy built the modern skyline upon it. And yet — I must keep the books honest — his own definitions retained a final softness, a residue of motion and approach not yet reduced to arithmetic, and the softness bit him. In that same great Course, Cauchy proved — his word, sir, not mine — that a convergent sum of continuous functions is itself continuous. Fourier's broken profiles, assembled from perfectly smooth waves, stood in the street outside as a standing contradiction. Something was wrong: with the theorem, with the proof, or with the very grammar of convergent. Where precisely the leak lies is a delicacy I reserve for episode seven — it is one of the most instructive wrong proofs ever published — and the general question it opens, of when two limiting processes may be exchanged, will be entered formally in the ledger in episode four and will pursue us, I promise you, to the final episode of season two. Young Abel, he of the divergent-series anathema, noticed the contradiction in eighteen twenty-six and remarked with courteous Norwegian understatement that the theorem appeared to suffer exceptions.

The softness was cured by the man I have been trailing all hour. Karl Weierstrass spent fourteen years as a schoolmaster in provincial Prussia, teaching mathematics to boys — along with, the records confirm, penmanship and gymnastics — and doing research alone, at night, unknown. In eighteen fifty-four a paper on a hard problem in the theory of functions appeared from this nobody and stunned the profession; within two years he had an honorary doctorate and a post in Berlin, where for three decades he delivered the lectures that taught Europe what proof means. Weierstrass's reform sounds like nothing and changed everything: he removed time from analysis. No more flowing, no more approaching, no more quantities becoming small — verbs, all of them, smuggling motion into a subject that had been burned by motion since Zeno. In their place: the static grammar of tolerance and threshold. To say a sequence has a limit is to say — and here, sir, is the sentence this whole season will teach you to love — that for every tolerance, however small, there is a threshold beyond which every term of the sequence lies within that tolerance of the limit. A challenge, and an answer; an inequality that holds or fails; arithmetic, standing still. Epsilon, the traditional name of the tolerance, is the most feared letter in the undergraduate alphabet, and by episode four you will regard it, I hope, as one regards a butler: strict, and entirely on your side. With that grammar, Cauchy's edifice was at last mortared tight — and the strangest consequence, which the schoolmaster himself supplied in eighteen seventy-two, is that perfect rigour did not banish Fourier's monsters. It licensed worse ones. Hold that thought for one more section.

Two final flourishes complete the rebuild, both waiting on our syllabus. Weierstrass's programme was called the arithmetization of analysis — the continuum reduced to statements about numbers and inequalities — but it stood on an unexamined floor: what is a real number? What, precisely, is the object root two, beyond a hole where a fraction ought to be? In eighteen fifty-eight, in Zurich, Richard Dedekind, obliged to lecture on the calculus and privately appalled that he could not justify its first principles to his own satisfaction, found the answer — Eudoxus's answer, sir, held up to the light and read as a definition — and he thought the moment important enough that he recorded the very day it came to him. That story is our next episode entire. And the floor beneath that floor — the infinite sets out of which Dedekind's numbers are built — was surveyed by Georg Cantor, Fourier's accidental heir, who in eighteen seventy-four proved that infinity comes in genuinely different sizes, and in eighteen ninety-one found the four-line argument, perhaps the most consequential four lines in mathematics, that we shall detonate together in episode three. I note, sir, with the disapproval of a well-run household, that Cantor's reward for all this was two decades of obstruction from Leopold Kronecker of Berlin — a first-rate mathematician who famously declared that God made the whole numbers and all else is the work of man, and who treated Cantor's infinities as corruption to be kept from print and Cantor himself as an infection to be kept from Berlin. Genius does not excuse conduct. The affair belongs to episode three, and I shall handle it there with the restraint it is owed.

So stands the rebuild: Bolzano the unread prophet; Cauchy the architect; Weierstrass the engineer of rigour; Dedekind the founder of the floor; Cantor the surveyor of the infinite. Between them, roughly between eighteen seventeen and eighteen ninety-one, the calculus of ghosts became analysis — and Berkeley's ticket, one hundred and forty years open, was closed at last, with a resolution the good bishop would have relished: the infinitesimals were abolished entirely. Nothing in modern analysis is infinitely small, sir. There are only finite tolerances, met by finite thresholds, arranged in ranks without end.

The Season Ahead, and the Monsters Promised

Let me now lay the season before you as what it is — a drama in eight acts, of which tonight was the first — and let me be candid that the cast includes monsters, several of them already under contract.

Episode two: what is a number, actually? The rationals will be shown to be dense — between any two of them, a third — and yet riddled with holes, a paradox of crowding and absence; and Dedekind will resolve it by the most audacious definition in mathematics: the hole itself, the cut in the line of fractions, declared to be the number. Absence, promoted to object. We shall also meet completeness, the single axiom that separates the real line from the rationals, and I give you fair warning: every major theorem of this season is that axiom wearing a different coat.

Episode three: Cantor, and the sizes of infinity. The rationals — dense, remember — will prove to be countable, no more numerous than the whole numbers; the real line will prove uncountable, a strictly larger infinite; and the proof, the diagonal argument, is a jewel I shall place in your hands entire, along with its astonishing echo in the theory of computation — the same blade, sir, that shows some problems no program can decide. You will also witness the birth of our first contracted monster: a dust of points, built by deleting middle thirds forever, that manages to be as numerous as the entire line while containing no interval whatsoever. It will return in episode five stranger than it left, and again in season two, strangest of all. And you will hear of the question Cantor could not answer — whether any infinity lies strictly between the whole numbers and the line — and of the twentieth-century verdict of Gödel and Cohen that the question is undecidable from the standard axioms of mathematics: not unanswered, sir; unanswerable from where we stand.

Episode four: the limit, defined at last — the hardest small thing in mathematics, and the payment of Zeno's account. You will learn it as an adversarial game, a skeptic proposing tolerances and we answering with thresholds, and the game will become the grammar of everything after. You will also hear a proof from around thirteen fifty — Nicole Oresme, a French scholar of startling modernity — that the harmonic series diverges; and the episode will close with a genuine shock: certain convergent series, reordered — merely reordered, sir, the same terms in a different sequence — can be made to sum to any value you care to name. Addition of infinitely many terms is not commutative. I shall enjoy your face, figuratively speaking, when Riemann does that.

Episode five: the hidden architecture of the line. Three of this season's great theorems demand the same odd hypothesis, closed and bounded, and we shall discover what that hypothesis really is: compactness, the next best thing to being finite, and the subject of a theorem bearing the names of Heine and Borel. We shall also state, and merely state, a theorem of Baire — patience, sir; it is a slow-burning fuse.

Episode six: continuity, earned. The schoolroom picture — a curve drawn without lifting the pen — will be replaced by the real definition, and then the gallery opens. Dirichlet's monster, the one promised half an hour ago, makes its formal entrance: a function that is pure static, continuous nowhere. Beside it, a subtler exhibit of one Thomae: a function continuous at every irrational point and discontinuous at every rational one — yes, sir, you heard the asymmetry correctly — and the still subtler fact that the mirror image is impossible: no function anywhere is continuous exactly on the rationals. That impossibility we shall state, savour, and file as a debt, for its proof requires machinery from the far shore of season two. The ledger will show it as our longest-dated bond.

Episode seven: the derivative, and the catastrophe. In eighteen hundred six, Ampère published a proof that every continuous function is differentiable save at scattered bad points; textbooks taught it for decades; and it is false in the most spectacular way ever discovered. Weierstrass, in eighteen seventy-two, constructed a function continuous at every point of the line and differentiable at none — a coastline of saw-teeth upon saw-teeth at every magnification, a curve that is all corner. Hermite, no coward, confessed in a letter to Stieltjes to turning away with fright and horror from this lamentable plague of functions. And then Baire's fuse, lit two episodes earlier, detonates: in the precise sense his theorem provides, the monsters are not the exceptions, sir — the monsters are the typical case, and the smooth curves of the textbooks are the vanishing minority. Intuition will need to be escorted from the building.

Episode eight, the season finale: the Riemann integral in its glory — Darboux's honest bookkeeping of upper and lower sums, and the fundamental theorem of calculus, the reconciliation of slope and area, the summit of the classical subject and the one unclouded triumph I can promise you. And then the fine print. Dirichlet's monster returns and defeats the integral utterly. A function of Volterra's design — differentiable everywhere, derivative bounded — proves to have a derivative the integral cannot digest: the fundamental theorem itself, holed below the waterline. And at the last, a criterion will be stated as pure prophecy — the Riemann-integrable functions are exactly those continuous almost everywhere — containing two words, almost everywhere, that Season One cannot even define. On that confession the season ends, with a French civil servant reaching for his pen.

Beyond it, sir, season two: the innocent question how long is a set, and everything it costs and buys. The rationals — dense, infinite, everywhere — will be shown to have total length zero. Vitali will exhibit, in nineteen hundred five, a set of points possessing no length whatsoever — not zero, sir; none, the question itself breaking — and the instrument of that construction, the axiom of choice, isolated by Zermelo in nineteen hundred four, will present its philosophical bill, which includes the notorious theorem of Banach and Tarski whereby one solid ball is carved into five pieces and reassembled into two balls, each the size of the original: a result that sounds like fraud and is in fact a sober theorem about pieces so pathological they have no volume to conserve. We shall meet Lebesgue, the hero of the second season, whose thesis of nineteen hundred two rebuilt the integral by sorting values instead of slicing domains; Carathéodory, whose criterion for measurability resembles a misprint and turns out to be the master key; the three convergence theorems that are the entire point of the construction, among them Fatou's lemma, whose moral — mass may vanish in a limit, but never appear unbidden — you will find applicable well beyond mathematics; Egorov and Lusin, who prove that even the monsters are, off a set of negligible size, respectable citizens; Hölder and Minkowski, whose celebrated inequalities I shall name aloud, praise sincerely, and exile to paper with full military honours; Riesz and Fischer, who complete one of Fourier's oldest promissory notes; Radon and Nikodym, whose derivative — the derivative of one measure with respect to another — turns out to underwrite conditional expectation and, at the very last, the divergence of Kullback and Leibler, an object I believe you have minimized professionally for some years, sir, without being told what it was. And presiding over the finale, Kolmogorov, who in nineteen thirty-three, in some sixty pages, declared that probability — dice, noise, markets, models — had been measure theory all along, wearing a costume. Forgive the parade of names; introductions are best made early, and you will know every one of these gentlemen before we are done.

Terms of Engagement

Before I release you, sir, the promised tour of the desk's half of tonight's work — chapter one of the Desk Volume — so that you arrive knowing the terrain.

The chapter opens, as every chapter will, with a notation ledger: a small table pairing tonight's spoken phrases with their written symbols — the nudge with the letter Newton used for it, which I have avoided pronouncing for the excellent reason that spoken aloud it is indistinguishable from a zero; the phrase root two with its radical sign; the quantifier words with their inverted letters. Two conventions are set down there that I flag now for your veto: the natural numbers, in this course, begin at one; and certain facts — the sum of the geometric series, the Basel value — are used tonight on credit, marked as such, with the debt recorded and the paying episode named.

There follows a short grammar of quantifiers — for every and there exists, the two words bearing most of the weight of modern mathematics — with particular attention to the order in which they arrive. Every pot has a lid and there is a lid that fits every pot employ the same four words, sir, and assert profoundly different degrees of optimism about kitchenware; a page of drills will fix the distinction in the wrist, along with the two patterns — a claim holding eventually versus holding infinitely often — that episode four will lean upon.

Then the proof toolkit. Contradiction, tonight's weapon, laid out formally. The contrapositive, which our servant-lemma used, with the logic of why it is legitimate. Induction — the ladder of the whole numbers, native country to a computer scientist — demonstrated on two specimens: a schoolboy summation, and an inequality of Bernoulli which looks like nothing whatever and will be found carrying luggage in half the proofs of this season. The two confessions of the evening are discharged in full: that lowest terms exist rests on the principle of well-ordering — every non-empty collection of positive whole numbers has a least member, a fact so obvious it takes discipline to notice one is using it — and the same principle yields the lemma that every whole number beyond one has a prime divisor, the missing bolt in Euclid's argument.

The theorems of the night then appear in formal dress: the irrationality of root two, exactly as narrated; Euclid on the primes; and the generalization you have surely already conjectured — the square root of a whole number is rational only when the number is a perfect square, so that root three, root five, root six and all their kin are irrational together. Its tidiest proof leans on the fundamental theorem of arithmetic — uniqueness of prime factorization — which the chapter states on credit and does not prove, with directions to where the honest article may be had; the honesty clause, sir, applies to the desk as to the ear. You will find there also a nod to Theodorus of Cyrene, who in the generation after Hippasus proved the irrationality of the roots case by case up to seventeen — where, tradition says, he stopped, for reasons scholars still argue about at conferences.

And then the problems, in the three tiers that will be standing policy. Tier one, Calisthenics: eight short exercises — negation drills, a contrapositive of your own, root three by tonight's method, the two inductions, the fact that no smallest positive rational exists — designed to be gotten right, because confidence is a mathematical instrument. Tier two, The Real Thing: the arithmetic of the irrationals, with its traps — the sum of two irrationals, sir, can be perfectly rational, and knowing when is the point; a two-line jewel proving the logarithm of three in base two irrational, by the observation that a power of two cannot equal a power of three — even cannot be odd; root two plus root three; a set of quantifier games in which you will formalize, and then correctly negate, a famous unsolved conjecture of Goldbach; and a herd of horses which I shall prove, by flawless-looking induction, to be all of one colour — your task being the autopsy of the proof. Tier three, For the Ambitious, starred and unapologetic: a second and swifter murder of root two by the method of infinite descent, a favourite manoeuvre of Fermat, in which the supposed fraction is made to generate a strictly smaller copy of itself forever, which no positive quantity can survive; and a small gem concerning root two raised to the power root two, which proves that an irrational raised to an irrational can land on a rational — by exhibiting two candidates, proving one of them must work, and being constitutionally unable to say which. A proof that exhibits nothing, sir. File the sensation; it returns in season two, episode three, when the axiom of choice presents exactly that character flaw at industrial scale.

Solutions: complete for tier one, terse as a telegram; provided for selected tier two problems — the ones completing arguments sketched aloud; for tier three, hints only, sealed in a separate section. The policy is deliberate and I state it once: a course of self-study needs closure on the essentials, but complete solution manuals rot discipline. Work first; hints second; solutions as audit, never as route.

Listen, then work. The order matters, sir. The ear was tonight's; the desk is yours.
