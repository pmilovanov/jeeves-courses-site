# Fubini, or the Crime of Swapping Integrals
### The Measure of All Things, Season Two, Episode Seven

## The Manoeuvre

Good day, sir.

There is a manoeuvre so habitual in the quantitative trades that most practitioners no longer notice performing it. A quantity depends on two variables; one integrates over both; and one chooses -- without ceremony, without a second thought -- the order of the itinerary: sweep x first and then t, or t first and then x, confident the answer cannot care about the bookkeeping. Every double sum in your libraries, every expectation of an expectation, every marginalization in every probabilistic model you have ever shipped, is this manoeuvre. Tonight, sir, the establishment determines exactly when it is a licensed operation and when it is a crime with consequences -- and I use the word crime advisedly, because tonight's exhibits include a function, elegant as you please, whose two itineraries both complete successfully and return different answers. Not divergence, not breakdown: two finite numbers, computed correctly, disagreeing. The desk will compute both to the digit, and the diagnosis will be an old acquaintance wearing new dress.

The programme: first the plane itself, built honestly -- the season has measured the line; tonight the product construction measures the plane from two copies of it, and the dimension license of Episode Two is cashed in full, from both sides. Then the two commandments, a matched pair: Tonelli's theorem -- for nonnegative functions, swap freely, always, even to infinity; and Fubini's theorem -- for integrable functions, swap after checking the absolute integral. Then the crime scene, with the fine print about sigma-finiteness that even careful practitioners forget exists. And at the close, the dividend the plan bills as the bridge to how probabilists think: the layer cake -- an integral recomputed as the area under the distribution of its heights -- with which the two seasons' notions of integration shake hands one final time before the finale. To work.

## The Plane, Built from the Line

The plane must be measured before it can host integrals, and there are two honest routes to its measure. Route one you have owned since Episode Two: run the whole season's construction directly in two dimensions -- rectangles for intervals, countable rectangle covers, the same infimum, Carathéodory's same criterion; the dimension license, verbatim. Route two is tonight's, and it is the structurally interesting one: build the plane's measure as a product of two lines, because the product construction is what generalizes -- to the product of any two measure spaces whatsoever, your trade's joint distributions included, where no ambient geometry exists to run route one.

The product construction in three breaths. The measurable rectangles: products of a citizen of the first line with a citizen of the second -- the atoms of two-dimensional bookkeeping, priced at length times length, the schoolroom's area rule promoted to a definition. The product sigma-algebra: the tribe the rectangles generate -- the smallest sigma-algebra containing them, existence by the intersection-from-above construction of this season's first evening, working its third season of service. And the product measure: extend the rectangle pricing to the whole tribe -- by Carathéodory's machinery run on rectangle covers, exactly as the line ran on interval covers; the theorem of nineteen fourteen was proved once and serves forever, which was always its advertisement. Uniqueness comes from the same well: two contract-keepers agreeing on rectangles agree on the tribe, by the frame-squeeze of Episode Two transplanted. And the two routes agree, sir -- the direct plane measure and the product measure coincide on their common jurisdiction -- the desk proving agreement on rectangles and letting uniqueness carry the rest. One plane, one measure, two derivations; the books close.

One vocabulary item completes the toolkit: sections. Fix a height y, and any set in the plane casts a shadow at that height -- the x-section, the set of x whose pair lies in the set; dually the y-sections. A function of two variables, frozen at one height, becomes a function of one -- the section functions. The x-knife and the y-knife, sir: the two ways of carving a planar object into linear slices, and the entire subject of iterated integration is the accounting relationship between a planar whole and its two families of slices. The foundational lemma -- every section of a product-measurable set is measurable on its line; every section of a product-measurable function is measurable -- is proved at the desk, with a candour I flag now: its full proof wants an induction over the tribe's generation that the trade calls a monotone-class argument, and the plan directs me to flag that machinery honestly rather than smuggle it. The desk states the monotone-class lemma, proves the accessible half, and cites the standard shelves for the remainder -- the season's one borrowed beam, clearly labelled.

## Tonelli's Theorem

The first commandment, sir -- Leonida Tonelli, nineteen oh nine -- and it is the nonnegative freedom of Episode Five grown up to two dimensions.

For a nonnegative measurable function on the product: the double integral -- the merchant's count run on the plane's measure directly -- equals the iterated integral, x first then y, and equals the iterated integral, y first then x. All three, always, with no hypothesis beyond the sign -- and "always" includes infinity: if any one of the three is infinite, all three are, in honest agreement. Nonnegativity is the universal license. Mass that cannot cancel cannot conspire: sweep it in any order and the total is the total.

The proof is this season's greatest-hits reel, and I narrate its architecture with some pride of ownership. Stage one: rectangles -- for the indicator of a rectangle, all three integrals read length times length by inspection. Stage two: from rectangles to the tribe -- the monotone-class beam carries indicator agreement from the generating atoms to every product-measurable set; here sits the borrowed machinery, spent exactly once. Stage three: from indicators to everything -- the staircase of the range lifts indicator agreement to simple functions by linearity, and the climbing license lifts simple agreement to all nonnegative measurable functions, staircases ascending under all three integrals at once, monotone convergence passing the limit through each. Rectangles, tribe, staircases, climb: every load-bearing wall is an instrument this season built, and the theorem that licenses your marginalizations is, at bottom, the observation that all three ways of counting agree on rectangles and that everything else is a limit of rectangles.

## Fubini's Theorem

The second commandment -- Guido Fubini, nineteen oh seven -- handles signs, and its statement carries the check that gives the standard operating procedure its shape.

For an integrable function on the product -- integrable, meaning the absolute value's double integral is finite -- the two iterated integrals exist, agree, and equal the double integral. The inner integrals, I note for precision, exist for almost every value of the outer variable -- a null set of bad slices is tolerated and absorbed, the amnesty working its passage even here. The proof, once Tonelli stands, is bookkeeping of a familiar shape: split into gains and losses, apply Tonelli to each -- both finite, by the integrability check -- and subtract, every term lawful.

And so the doctrine, sir, stated as doctrine -- the standard operating procedure, promised in Episode Five, canonized tonight. To swap a double integral: if the integrand is nonnegative, swap freely -- Tonelli, no questions asked. If the integrand carries signs, first run Tonelli on its absolute value -- a nonnegative computation, itself swappable at will -- and if that absolute total is finite, swap the original freely -- Fubini; if infinite, do not swap -- you are outside the license, and the crime scene ahead shows what the territory looks like. One checks with Tonelli and spends with Fubini. That procedure, sir, executed reflexively for a century by everyone who computes, is the entire content of the matched pair -- and note the family resemblance to Season One's series doctrine: absolute convergence licensed rearrangement; conditional convergence forbade it; the plane's integrals obey the same constitution, because they are the same phenomenon -- an uncountable rearrangement question, wearing area's clothes.

## The Crime Scene

Now the exhibit, sir, and I shall describe it geometrically before the desk computes it, because the geometry is the diagnosis.

On the quarter-plane -- x and y from nought to infinity -- build a staircase of blocks along the diagonal: on each unit square sitting on the diagonal -- corners at whole numbers, the k-th square spanning k to k plus one in both coordinates -- place mass plus one, spread uniformly; and give each such block a shadow: mass minus one on the square immediately to its right -- same storey, next column, just below the diagonal. A staircase of positive blocks, each casting a negative shadow one column rightward. That is the whole function: plus-one blocks on the diagonal, minus-one blocks just under it, nought elsewhere -- bounded, measurable, elementary as furniture.

Run the y-itinerary first: fix a column -- a vertical line at position x -- and total what the column sees. A column through the k-th unit of x, for k at least one, passes through exactly two blocks: its own storey's positive block on the diagonal, and the shadow cast into it by the storey below -- one plus, one minus, total nought. But the very first column -- x below one -- meets the ground floor's positive block and no shadow at all: the ground floor casts its shadow into the second column, and no storey exists below to cast one back. Column totals: one on the first unit of x, nought forever after. Integrate over x: one.

Now the x-itinerary: fix a row -- a horizontal line at height y -- and sweep across. The row through the k-th storey meets that storey's positive block on the diagonal and that same storey's shadow, parked one column to the right -- both present for every storey, the ground floor included, since every block casts its shadow sideways rather than downward. One plus, one minus: every row totals nought, without exception. Integrate over y: nought.

One itinerary returns one; the other returns nought. Both computations are finite, elementary, and correct -- the desk performs each to the digit, block by block -- and they disagree. No error has been made, sir; the manoeuvre itself is the error. Run the check the doctrine demands and the diagnosis appears at once: the absolute value of this function is a one on every block, positive and shadow alike -- an infinite staircase of unit masses -- and its double integral is infinite. The integrability check fails; Fubini's license was never issued; and the two itineraries are two conditionally convergent rearrangements of the same signed mass, each converging to its own answer -- the Riemann rearrangement theorem of Season One, Episode Four, staged on the quarter-plane with blocks for terms. The crime was never exotic, sir. It is the oldest crime in the course, in two dimensions.

## The Fine Print

One clause remains, easy to forget and cheap to state. Both commandments assume the two measure spaces are sigma-finite -- coverable by countably many pieces of finite measure, the clerical property the line has enjoyed since Episode Two's frames and the season filed as fine print with an appointment for tonight. Drop it and even Tonelli fails: the standard witness pairs the unit interval under ordinary length with the unit interval under counting measure -- every set priced by its number of points, an honest measure with no countable finite cover -- and examines the indicator of the diagonal. One itinerary integrates to nought, the other to one; nonnegative, measurable, and disagreeing, because the counting side is too vast for rectangle bookkeeping to approximate. The desk files the computation in three lines. The moral for practice is brief: every space your trade will ever integrate over is sigma-finite, and the clause costs you nothing -- but the theorems' statements carry it, and now you know which exhibit put it there.

## The Layer Cake

The dividend, sir -- the plan calls it the bridge to how probabilists think, and it has been my favourite application of Tonelli since I learned the trade.

Take a nonnegative measurable function on any sigma-finite ground. For each height t above nought, ask: how much ground does the function exceed t upon? -- the acreage of the pile above t, a decreasing function of the height; the season's oldest question, asked at every altitude at once. The layer-cake representation: the integral of the function equals the integral, over all heights, of those acreages. Total mass equals the accumulated area of the horizontal slices -- the cake priced layer by layer rather than column by column.

The proof is Tonelli performing a somersault, and it fits in the ear entire. Consider, on the product of the ground with the half-line of heights, the indicator of the region under the graph -- one when the height is below the function's value, nought otherwise. Integrate heights first, ground second: at each point of the ground, the inner integral measures the heights beneath the function's value there -- which is the value itself; the outer integral then totals the function: the left side. Integrate ground first, heights second: at each height, the inner integral measures the ground where the function exceeds the height -- the acreage of the pile; the outer integral accumulates the acreages: the right side. One region, two itineraries, both nonnegative -- Tonelli licenses the equality without conditions, and the identity drops out. The subgraph, sliced vertically, is the integral; sliced horizontally, it is the distribution of heights. Your trade, sir, lives on the horizontal slicing: every tail-probability computation, every expected value written as an integral of survival probabilities, every quantile argument, is the layer cake -- the finale will write expectation equals the integral of the tail probabilities, and it will be tonight's identity wearing its proper uniform. The merchant of Episode Four sorted the till by denomination; the layer cake prices the till by cumulative solvency; and the two seasons' pictures of an integral -- area under a curve, mass sorted by value -- meet in the subgraph, which is both at once. The wonder beat of the evening, sir, spends itself on that handshake.
