What Is a Number, Actually?

The Anatomy of the Continuum, Season One, Episode Two

The Account Falls Due

Good day, sir.

When we parted, the ledger showed five accounts, and the first of them falls due this very hour. Last episode ended with a demolition: the diagonal of the unit square has no fraction for its length — we proved it, watertight, twenty-four centuries after the Greeks did — and so either the diagonal has no length at all, which is absurd, since there it lies, drawable in the dust; or else there are lengths that are not fractions, numbers beyond the rationals, and somebody owes us a definition. What is such a number? Not a decimal that never ends — that is a costume, not an anatomy. Not a point on a line — that is a picture, and we have seen this subject burned by pictures. The question stood open, I remarked, for some twenty-three centuries, which even by the standards of intellectual history is a leisurely turnaround; and it was settled at last by a quiet man in Zurich who thought the answer important enough to write down the date it came to him.

Tonight we settle it with him. The plan of the hour: first, a fair inventory of the rationals — what the fractions do superbly, for they are a more impressive society than their failure at the diagonal suggests. Second, the strangest fact in tonight's inventory: the rationals are dense — between any two of them, a third — and yet riddled with holes; everywhere crowded, and full of gaps; and we shall face squarely the question of how both can be true at once, and what a hole in a crowd could even mean. Third, the new vocabulary that lets us point at a hole with precision — bounds, and the best of bounds — and then the single axiom, one sentence, that separates the real numbers from the rationals and carries, I promise you, every major theorem of this season on its back. Fourth, the set piece: Dedekind's construction, the audacity itself. And then the dividends: three theorems that fall out of the axiom like interest on a deposit, one of which formally exorcises Bishop Berkeley's ghosts.

An hour on the question every schoolchild would consider beneath asking. That, sir, is generally the sign one is near the foundations.

The Society of Fractions

Let us begin by being fair to the rationals, because tonight ends with their demotion, and a gentleman does not demote a faithful servant without first reading the letter of reference.

The rationals — the fractions, positive and negative, whole numbers included — form what mathematicians call a field, and the word deserves one minute of honest definition, for it will recur. A field is a number system in which the four operations of the schoolroom behave exactly as the schoolroom promised: you may add, subtract, multiply, and divide — by anything except zero — without ever leaving the system, and the familiar laws hold: order of addition irrelevant, multiplication distributing over sums, every number owning a negative, every nonzero number owning a reciprocal. The fractions pass every clause. Divide one fraction by another and a fraction returns; the society is closed, self-sufficient, complete under commerce. The whole numbers, note, are not a field — divide three by two and you have already emigrated. The fractions are the smallest society containing the whole numbers in which division is a birthright, and that is precisely why humanity built them.

More: the rationals are an ordered field. Any two fractions can be compared — one is smaller, or they are equal — and the ordering respects the arithmetic: add the same amount to both sides of an inequality and it survives; multiply both sides by a positive quantity and it survives. Order and arithmetic, cooperating. This too will be on the specification sheet when we commission the real numbers.

And now the property that makes the rationals genuinely uncanny, the one I ask you to sit with. Take any two distinct fractions, as close together as you dare — a millionth apart, a millionth of a millionth. Their average is a fraction, and it lies strictly between them. Between any two rationals, however close, there is a third. And therefore a fourth and a fifth — between the first and the newcomer, average again — and so on without end: between any two fractions live infinitely many more. The property is called density, and I want you to appreciate how strange it makes the society. No fraction has a next-door neighbour. There is no fraction just after one half; whatever candidate you nominate, infinitely many fractions crowd between. Zoom in upon the rational line forever and it never resolves into separated points; every neighbourhood of every fraction, at every magnification, is as crowded as the whole. Compare the whole numbers, marching in file, each knowing its successor: the rationals have abolished succession entirely. A society, sir, with no such thing as adjacent.

Grant the fractions one more virtue and the reference letter is complete: they are enough for every measurement that will ever be taken. Any length, any weight, any voltage, to any finite precision whatsoever — a fraction suffices, indeed a decimal fraction. No laboratory instrument has ever output an irrational number; none ever will. The rationals are dense in the world. If mathematics were only the science of measurement, tonight's episode would be unnecessary.

So there is the letter of reference: a self-sufficient arithmetic, an order that cooperates, a crowd so thick it has no gaps visible from inside, and adequacy for every practical purpose. And yet. You watched the proof, sir. Somewhere in that impenetrable crowd, exactly where the diagonal of the unit square demands a number — there is nobody home.

The Anatomy of a Hole

Here is the difficulty with the phrase the rationals have holes: a hole is an absence, and you cannot point at an absence — least of all from inside the crowd, using only members of the crowd as your pointing apparatus. Last episode we located the hole from outside, geometrically: build a square, draw its diagonal, observe that no fraction measures it. But the whole ambition of the rebuild, you will recall, is arithmetic standing on its own feet, no pictures, no appeals to lengths. So tonight's first serious question is this: can the rationals themselves be made to confess the hole? Using nothing but fractions and the ordering among them, can we exhibit the gap — describe a precise location at which a number ought to stand, and demonstrate the post unfilled?

We can, and the manoeuvre is the technical heart of the hour. Attend closely, sir; this is the moment tonight's new vocabulary is born.

Consider the collection of every positive fraction whose square falls short of two. One belongs — one squared is one, short of two. So does one and two fifths — its square is one and twenty-four twenty-fifths, still short. One and forty-one hundredths: still short. The collection is a perfectly lawful citizen of the rational world: I have described it using fractions, squaring, and comparison, nothing else. Call it, for the hour, the shortfall club — every positive fraction whose square falls short of two.

Two observations, both elementary. First, the club is not empty; one is a member. Second, the club does not sprawl without limit up the number line: no member reaches as high as two, since any fraction of size two or more has square four or more, disqualifying it outright. The entire club lives below two. We say the club is bounded above, and we call two an upper bound: a value that no member exceeds. Upper bounds are cheap, notice — if two is one, so is three, so is seventeen, so is any fraction above two. The club has a ceiling; indeed it has an infinite wardrobe of ceilings, each higher than the last and each equally useless. The interesting question about ceilings is never whether they exist. It is how low they go.

So: press the ceiling downward. Two works. Does one and a half? A member would need square below two; one and a half squared is two and a quarter; and — one small lemma the desk will confirm — if a positive fraction's square exceeds two, every larger fraction's square exceeds it too, so nothing in the club can exceed one and a half. Yes: one and a half is an upper bound, a lower ceiling. Press again: one and forty-two hundredths? Its square is a shade over two — a ceiling still. One and forty-one hundredths? Its square falls short of two — so it is a member of the club, and a member overtopped by other members, hence no ceiling at all. You see the game, sir. The ceilings can be pressed lower and lower; the members climb higher and higher; and both parties are converging on the same fateful territory — the place where squares stop falling short of two and start exceeding it. The place, in other words, where a fraction with square exactly two would stand, if such a thing existed. We proved last week that it does not.

And now the confession we came for. I claim the shortfall club has ceilings without end, but no lowest ceiling — no best, no final, no least upper bound among the fractions. The argument has two halves, and each is a small squeeze; I shall give you the strategy and the desk the arithmetic. Take any rational ceiling. Its square cannot equal two — last week's theorem. Could its square fall short of two? No: then it would be a club member, and members are never ceilings — every member has other members above it; the club, remember, inherits the rationals' horror of a last element, and the desk verifies that a member's slight enlargement stays a member. So every rational ceiling has square exceeding two. Very well: take such a ceiling, square strictly exceeding two — and here is the fatal half — there is always a slightly lower fraction whose square still exceeds two. The excess above two gives room to shave; a small, entirely explicit adjustment — the desk performs it with a rather elegant fraction that does both halves of tonight's squeeze in one motion — produces a smaller ceiling. Always. Every rational ceiling has a rational ceiling strictly beneath it. The descent never ends and never arrives.

Sit with what has just been established, sir, because it is the precise anatomy of a hole, drawn from inside the crowd. A club of fractions, climbing; a wardrobe of ceilings, descending; the two parties approaching one another forever, with nothing between them — and no member of either party is the boundary. The club has no highest member. The ceilings have no lowest member. Somewhere between the climbers and the descenders there is a station at which a number ought to stand — the number that would be the least of all ceilings, the exact frontier — and among the rationals the post is unfilled. No geometry, no diagonal, no picture: fractions alone, testifying to their own incompleteness. That, and precisely that, is what a hole in a dense crowd means.

And let me twist the knife the width of one remark: density makes it stranger, not milder. Approach the empty station from below and club members greet you all the way in — closer than any tolerance you name. Approach from above and ceilings greet you likewise. From inside the rationals, the neighbourhood of the hole is indistinguishable from the neighbourhood of an honest number: crowded, bustling, infinitely populated on both flanks. The hole is not a visible clearing, sir. It is a missing resident in a city with no vacant streets — detectable only by the club-and-ceiling audit we have just performed. Dense, and yet gapped: both true at once, and now you have seen exactly how.

Bounds, and the Best of Them

The audit we just performed deserves permanent vocabulary, for it is about to become the load-bearing language of the entire course. Let me lay the terms out slowly and lock the phrasing; these templates, once learned, will be spoken the same way every time they occur, this season and next.

A collection of numbers is bounded above if some value stands at or above every member — such a value is an upper bound, a ceiling. Bounded below and lower bound — a floor — mirror it downward. A collection can be bounded above and not below, as the negative whole numbers are; below and not above, as the club of positive fractions is; both, as tonight's shortfall club is; or neither, as the rationals entire.

Now the crown. Among all the ceilings of a bounded collection, the one that matters — when it exists — is the lowest: the least upper bound, called by its Latin-flavoured name the supremum. Say the word aloud once, sir: supremum. It will be spoken perhaps a thousand times before we finish, and it is the single most important definition of the season. What must a value be, to be the supremum of a collection? Two clauses, and I shall always give them in the same order. Clause one: it is a ceiling — no member of the collection exceeds it. Clause two: nothing lower is a ceiling — dip beneath it by any margin, however slight, and some member of the collection rises past you. A ceiling, and the last word in ceilings: press it down by a hair and the club pushes back. The mirror notion — the greatest lower bound, the highest floor — is the infimum, and every word tonight about suprema flips for infima with the signs reversed.

Three quick specimens, to calibrate the instrument — each teaches a distinct lesson, and all three are in the desk chapter with relatives for practice.

Specimen one: the closed interval from zero to one — everything from zero to one, endpoints included. Ceilings: one, and anything above. Lowest ceiling: one. The supremum is one, and one belongs to the collection — the supremum here is simply the maximum, the largest member, wearing formal dress. When a collection has a largest member, supremum and maximum coincide, and the new word earns nothing.

Specimen two, where the new word earns everything: the open interval from zero to one — endpoints now excluded. What is the largest member? There is none, sir: for any member, the average of it and one is a larger member; the collection climbs forever toward a summit it never occupies. Maximum: nonexistent. And yet the ceilings are exactly as before — one, and everything above — and the lowest ceiling is exactly one. The supremum is one: a perfectly definite value, not a member of the collection, hovering at the frontier like a landlord who owns the building without living in it. This is the entire point of the concept. Maxima demand a champion inside the collection and often go begging. Suprema ask only for a frontier, and a frontier can exist whether or not anyone stands on it. The supremum is the honest generalization of largest to collections that climb without cresting — which, in analysis, is most of the collections worth studying.

Specimen three, the one to carry forward: the collection of fractions one minus one over n — a half, two thirds, three quarters, four fifths, marching upward forever. No largest member; each is overtaken by the next. Supremum: one — a ceiling, since every member falls short of one; and the least ceiling, since dip below one by any margin and the march overtakes you, as the desk confirms with a whole number chosen by a method that will star in tonight's third act. Notice what the supremum is doing in this specimen, sir, and file it: the collection approaches something, and the supremum names the something without a single word about approaching, motion, or time. Weierstrass's programme from last episode — the abolition of verbs — is already at work. Where the eighteenth century said the sequence tends to one, tonight's vocabulary says: the supremum of its values is one. A noun has replaced a journey. Come episode four, when limits are defined in earnest, you will find the supremum already standing there, holding the door. The mirror specimen, for symmetry: the fractions one over n — one, a half, a third, a quarter, marching downward forever — have infimum zero, unattained; and the desk's proof that zero is truly the greatest of the floors employs a property to be unveiled in tonight's third act, a circularity of employment I note with approval. The staff here are versatile.

And now, sir, reread tonight's hole in the new vocabulary, because it compresses to a single devastating sentence. The shortfall club is a non-empty collection of rationals, bounded above — and it has no supremum in the rationals. That is the confession, notarized. The rationals contain climbing, bounded crowds whose frontier posts are unmanned. Whatever the real numbers are going to be, their defining virtue — the entire job description — is now perfectly clear: in the reals, every such frontier post must be occupied. Always. Without exception. Let us go and write that into law.

The Single Axiom

Here it is, then — the sentence this season stands on. I shall read it the way one reads the operative clause of a will.

Every non-empty collection of real numbers that is bounded above has a least upper bound, among the real numbers.

That is the axiom of completeness, and I want to be scrupulously clear about its legal status, because the word axiom is used loosely in the world and precisely here. We are not claiming to have observed this property in nature. We are not deducing it from something deeper — not tonight, at any rate. We are writing a specification. The real numbers, whatever they turn out to be, are hereby commissioned as: an ordered field — all the arithmetic and order virtues of the fractions, every clause of the reference letter — with one additional guarantee the fractions conspicuously lack: no unmanned frontier posts. Every bounded climb has a supremum. Every hole of the kind we anatomized tonight is, by decree, filled.

One axiom. It looks so slight, sir. It mentions no diagonal, no calculus, no curve; it is a single sentence about ceilings. Yet I give you my word as a keeper of accounts, and I enter it into the ledger as a standing obligation to be audited at the season's close: every major theorem of this season is this sentence wearing a different coat. When a bounded increasing sequence converges in episode four — completeness. When every sequence trapped in an interval has a convergent thread within it — completeness, by way of a hunt we shall name later. When a continuous function on a closed interval attains its maximum in episode six, and when it cannot leap from negative to positive without crossing zero — completeness, twice, in two different costumes. The fundamental theorem of calculus in episode eight rests on the mean value theorem, which rests on the extreme value theorem, which rests — you follow the plumbing, sir. Pull this one axiom out and the entire season collapses into the rationals, where essentially every one of those theorems dies. One concrete casualty to fix the idea: inside the rationals, the climbing decimals — one, then one and four tenths, then one and forty-one hundredths, each a fraction, the whole climb ceilinged by two — arrive nowhere; the station they climb toward is precisely tonight's hole. A bounded climb with no destination: the monotone convergence theorem, the first star of episode four, is false in the rationals, and a desk problem tonight has you construct the matching failure of tonight's third dividend. Completeness is not one repair among several, sir; it is the difference between a subject and a ruin. I shall ring a small bell, figuratively, at each appearance. You may keep count; the ledger certainly shall.

Two honest footnotes before we meet the man who made the axiom respectable. First: an axiom is a promissory note, and promissory notes raise the question of solvency. Who says anything satisfies this specification? Writing does not exist upon a wish is a lesson mathematics has learned expensively. The answer — the construction of an actual object meeting every clause — is precisely Dedekind's deed, and it is next. Second: suppose something does satisfy the specification; might several inequivalent somethings? Might there be rival real lines, all complete, all ordered fields, genuinely different? The answer is no — any two complete ordered fields are identical in all but the names of their elements, so the axiom pins down its object uniquely — but the proof of that uniqueness is bookkeeping of a length I decline to inflict on either of us; the desk states it honestly as a fact on credit and points to where the audit is performed in full. What matters tonight is the division of labour: the axiom is the specification; Dedekind is the manufacturer; uniqueness is the certificate that there is only one product on the market.

The Man Who Dated His Idea

Richard Dedekind was born in Brunswick in eighteen thirty-one, into a family of professors and lawyers, and he has the quiet distinction of having been the last doctoral student of Gauss — the old lion signed off on him in eighteen fifty-two, near the end. He was, by every account, the least theatrical of the great mathematicians: modest, methodical, devoted to his sisters, suspicious of haste. He spent nearly his whole career not in Berlin or Göttingen but back home in Brunswick, teaching at the technical high school, declining grander chairs, outliving all his famous correspondents, and dying in nineteen sixteen at eighty-four — so long after his great work that a mathematical almanac once printed his death date years in advance; he is said to have written to the editor that the day in question had in fact been passed, in excellent health, in stimulating conversation with his friend Georg Cantor — a friendship to which I shall return within the half hour. Remember the temperament, sir: what follows is the most audacious act in the foundations of mathematics, and it was committed by the most cautious man in the profession.

In eighteen fifty-eight, aged twenty-seven, Dedekind took up a post at the Polytechnic in Zurich, and for the first time in his life had to teach the differential calculus to beginners. And the experience embarrassed him. Not the students — himself. At the crux of the subject — the theorem that a quantity which climbs steadily but is barred from climbing past some bound must settle toward a definite value; you will recognize tonight's vocabulary circling overhead — he found that he possessed no proof. Nobody did. One waved at the picture: the climbing points, the barrier, the intuition that they must pile up somewhere. He later wrote of it plainly: the resort to geometric evidence in a first calculus course he considered useful, even indispensable, on pedagogical grounds — but no one, he said, would claim it was scientific. Most men who notice such a thing note it, deplore it at dinner, and carry on. Dedekind stopped and asked the question underneath: what fact about the straight line is the picture silently invoking? What is continuity — the unbrokenness of the line — when one is forbidden to gesture? Everyone believed the line gapless; no one could say what gapless meant.

He found the answer within weeks, and here is the detail I promised you at the close of last episode, the detail I confess I find rather moving. His own preface records the day: the twenty-fourth of November, eighteen fifty-eight. A Wednesday, for what it is worth. He knew precisely what he had, and precisely how much it mattered, and then — this is the temperament, sir — he put it in a drawer for fourteen years, mentioning it to a friend or two, teaching from it privately, publishing nothing. Only in eighteen seventy-two, when rival constructions began appearing in print and the priority he had never bothered to claim was about to evaporate, did he issue the little pamphlet — barely thirty pages — titled, in our anglicized convention, Continuity and Irrational Numbers. It is one of the shortest foundational documents in the history of thought, and among the most consequential per page. What was in the drawer, sir, is the set piece of tonight's episode.

The Audacity

Dedekind's opening move is to ask what makes the geometric line feel continuous, and his answer is a masterpiece of looking at the obvious until it confesses. Take the line, and take a point upon it. The point does something so banal that no one before him thought it worth stating: it divides the line into two pieces — a left portion and a right portion, every location falling into one or the other, everything in the left portion lying left of everything in the right. A point is, among other things, a way of cutting the line in two. Now Dedekind inverts it. Forget the point; keep the cut. Suppose I hand you only the division — here is the left camp, here is the right camp, every location assigned, every left member below every right member — a complete severing of the line, with the knife itself hidden. Question: can you always find the point where the knife fell? And Dedekind saw that the continuity of the line — the very gaplessness everyone believed and no one could state — is exactly the assertion that the answer is yes. Every honest severing of the line is performed at some point. No cut falls on nothing. That is what unbroken means, sir, said without a single picture: the line is the thing you cannot cut without hitting somebody.

Now run the same knife through the rationals, and watch the belief fail — for we have already done the experiment; we did it half an hour ago and called it the anatomy of a hole. Sever the fractions as follows: into the left camp, every fraction that is negative or zero, and every positive fraction whose square falls short of two — the shortfall club and its hangers-on; into the right camp, everything else — the positive fractions whose square meets or exceeds two. Check the paperwork: every fraction is assigned to exactly one camp; every left member is below every right member — the desk verifies the comparison — so this is a lawful, complete severing of the rational line. And where did the knife fall? On nobody. The left camp has no greatest member — the climbers climb forever. The right camp has no least member — the ceilings descend forever. We audited both facts. Here is a cut of the rationals performed at no rational whatsoever: two camps pressed against one another across an empty frontier. The rationals can be severed without hitting anybody, and that — now stated with complete precision, in the language of camps and members and order, no geometry anywhere — is what it means for the rationals to be incomplete.

So the diagnosis is finished, and any ordinary genius would proceed as follows: postulate that new numbers exist, one for each empty frontier, and get on with the calculus. What Dedekind did instead is the audacity, and I ask you to slow down with me and take it at walking pace, because it is a thing worth witnessing rather than merely hearing about.

Where the old mathematics says: this cut falls upon no number — Dedekind replies: then the cut shall be the number. That pair of camps — that very act of severing, the left crowd and the right crowd pressed against their empty frontier — that object, itself, is hereby a number. The cut of the shortfall club is not a description of the square root of two, not an address where it would live if it existed, not a pair of pincers closing in on it. It is the square root of two. There is nothing behind the curtain, sir; the curtain is the actor. And with one gesture the entire missing population arrives at once: define a real number to be a cut of the rationals. The cuts that fall on a fraction — where one camp has a champion at the frontier — those are the old numbers, the rationals themselves, re-dressed; each fraction is matched with the cut it performs, and the desk confirms the match preserves everything. The cuts that fall on nobody — the empty frontiers, the holes themselves — those are precisely the new citizens: the irrationals. Every hole in the rationals has been converted, by definition, into a resident. The absence is the object. The vacancy has been appointed to fill itself.

I shall allow myself the hour's one open moment, sir, because I undertook to spend it here and there is nowhere better in the episode. Consider what has actually happened. For twenty-three centuries the square root of two was a scandal precisely because no one could say what it was — the Greeks could construct it and could not number it; the moderns could approximate it to a thousand places and could not define it. Every approach treated the thing as somewhere out there, glimpsed through fractions but never caught. Dedekind's insight is that the glimpsing was already the catching. The infinite crowd of fractions pressing at the gap from both sides — the complete dossier of everything below and everything above — is not evidence about the number. It is all there ever was to the number; the dossier, complete, is the thing itself. A number, in the end, is exactly the way it sits among the fractions — Eudoxus's old test, the company a magnitude keeps, promoted after twenty-two centuries from a criterion into a birth certificate. And notice the change in what mathematical existence means, because the profession itself needed a generation to digest it. The new numbers are not discovered glinting in some Platonic riverbed; they are manufactured — lawfully, explicitly, out of prior materials, the way a corporation is manufactured out of paper and intent. Dedekind said it himself, in a phrase that scandalized some contemporaries and delights me: numbers are free creations of the human mind. Man, having found holes in his number line, sat down and minted the missing currency — and the minting was a definition. That is the audacity, sir. Not a bold conjecture, not a heroic computation: an act of lawful creation. It remains, I would submit, the single most instructive event in the foundations of mathematics — the demonstration that when reality declines to supply an object your theorems require, precision itself will serve as a foundry.

You may ask — a listener of your habits will already have asked — why the foundry required such exotic stock. Why not simply decree a real number to be an unending decimal expansion, since that is how the world pictures them anyway? One can, sir; the construction goes through, and the desk notes where. But attempt to add two unending decimals and you meet the difficulty: carrying. The carry in addition propagates from right to left, and an unending decimal has no rightmost digit to start from; every operation becomes a negotiation with infinitely remote places. Worse, the definition smuggles in a loyalty to the number ten — a biographical accident of our species' hands. Dedekind's cuts carry no base, no digits, no carrying: only order. The picturesque definition and the workable one, sir, are rarely the same definition; it is among the standing lessons of foundations.

Very well: the currency is minted; now the audit, for a definition acquires its rights by doing work. Three inspections, in ascending order of consequence — strategy tonight, arithmetic at the desk.

First, order. Given two cuts, which is smaller? Look at their left camps: the smaller number's left camp sits inside the larger's — containment of camps is the ordering, and the desk checks it behaves: any two cuts comparable, the comparison cooperating with everything else. Second, arithmetic. To add two cuts, add their left camps memberwise — every left member of the first plus every left member of the second assembles the left camp of the sum — and the desk verifies the assembly is again a lawful cut. Addition, subtraction, and order pass inspection with modest labour. Multiplication — I am contractually honest, sir — multiplication is a swamp: signs multiply case upon case, and the full verification is the sort of virtuous drudgery that builds character in exactly the way character is not worth building twice. The desk defines the positive case, has you taste the swamp in a starred problem, and cites chapter and verse where the whole slog is performed by professionals so that humanity need never do it again. It has been done; it holds; we move.

Third — the inspection that justifies the enterprise — completeness. The commissioned specification demanded no unmanned frontiers: does the new system honour it, or have we merely relocated the holes one storey up? Suppose someone hands you a bounded, climbing crowd — not of fractions now, but of cuts, of the new numbers themselves — and demands its supremum. Dedekind's construction answers with a serenity that still startles me: pool the left camps. Take every fraction belonging to the left camp of any member of the crowd — their grand union — and the desk verifies in five lines that this pool is itself a lawful cut: it is a ceiling for the crowd, since it swallows every member's camp; and it is the least ceiling, since any rival ceiling must swallow all the same fractions. The supremum of a bounded family of cuts is simply the union of their left camps — it exists always, mechanically, by construction. Do you see what that means, sir? Cutting the new line never finds a gap, because a cut of cuts assembles into a cut — the material is gapless by its very grain. The manoeuvre that manufactured the numbers cannot be turned against them; run Dedekind's own knife through Dedekind's own line and it lands, every time, on a resident. One round of hole-filling, and the process terminates forever — no holes remain, no second storey is needed, the hotel is full and the ledger balances. The specification of the previous section is not a wish, sir. As of eighteen seventy-two it is a warehouse: the complete ordered field, in stock, constructed to order out of nothing but fractions and set membership. The real numbers exist because Dedekind built them.

And so — with the ceremony the moment deserves, for it is the first — I direct your attention to the ledger. Account the first, opened in the opening minutes of episode one: what is a number, actually? Paid in full, this hour, by Richard Dedekind of Brunswick. A real number is a cut of the rationals; the square root of two is the cut whose left camp is the shortfall club and its hangers-on; the diagonal of the unit square has a length, that length is a number, and we can now say — without geometry, without pictures, without a single ghost — exactly what it is. One account settled, sir. It will not be the last, but I confess none will be more satisfying to stamp.

The Rival Firm

Honesty, and the shakedown of history, both require me to tell you that Dedekind's is not the only construction on the market — and the story of the rival firm ties tonight to last week with a bow I did not arrange and could not have improved.

Recall where we left Georg Cantor in episode one: a young analyst at a provincial German university, dragged by Fourier's uniqueness question into the study of infinite sets of points. In eighteen seventy-two — the very year Dedekind's drawer finally opened; the coincidence forced Dedekind's hand, in fact, for it was the appearance of rival essays that stirred him to publish — Cantor issued his own construction of the real numbers, with the Frenchman Charles Méray having printed the essential idea in eighteen sixty-nine, three years earlier, a priority France remembers rather better than anyone else does; and Eduard Heine, Cantor's senior colleague, systematizing the approach in print the same season. The rival firm's raw material is not the cut but the journey. Consider sequences of fractions that huddle — whose terms, as you walk down the list, crowd ever closer to one another, eventually all lying within any tolerance you care to name of each other; the precise grammar of that huddling is episode four's centrepiece and I shall not poach it tonight. Some huddling sequences have a rational destination they crowd around. Others — the decimal approximations to the square root of two, say: one, then one and four tenths, then one and forty-one hundredths, onward — huddle impeccably around nothing at all; the crowd converges on a hole. Cantor's move is Dedekind's audacity in different dress: where the old mathematics says this journey has no destination, Cantor replies — then the journey shall be the destination. A real number is defined to be a huddling sequence of rationals; two sequences that huddle toward the same place — whose interleaving still huddles — are declared the same number; and the desk of episode four will make every word of that precise. Same vacancy, sir; a different appointee: Dedekind hires the hole, Cantor hires the road to it.

The two founders, incidentally, met that very summer — by pure chance, on holiday at Interlaken, in Switzerland, in that same eighteen seventy-two — and struck up what I do not hesitate to call the most consequential correspondence in the history of mathematics. It was in letters to Dedekind, the following year, that Cantor first hammered out the theorem that headlines our next episode; the proof crossed Europe in an envelope before it saw a journal. Keep the friendship in view, sir. It does both men credit, and history is shortly to treat one of them shabbily enough that the credit wants remembering.

Two firms, one product — and I mean that exactly, not rhetorically: both constructions deliver a complete ordered field, and by the uniqueness certificate already on credit, there is only one such field to deliver. The line built from cuts and the line built from journeys are the same line under different bookkeeping, and no theorem of this course will ever care which warehouse its numbers shipped from. Why keep both firms in business, then? Taste and terrain. Dedekind's cuts are the connoisseur's choice for the line itself — order is their native element, and completeness falls out, as you saw, in five lines of pooling. Cantor's sequences carry no order in their luggage, only distances — nearness, huddling — and that makes them the construction that travels. When mathematics later needed to complete other realms — spaces whose points are functions, sir, realms with distance but no left-and-right, the very spaces where season two will do its banking — it was Cantor's method, not Dedekind's, that packed its bags and went. File that; the invoice arrives in the second season, stamped by a Hungarian and an Austrian you have already been introduced to.

And a third stance on the whole affair, for completeness of a different kind: at the turn of the twentieth century, Hilbert proposed skipping the warehouse tour altogether — write down the axioms, completeness among them, declare the real numbers to be whatever satisfies the list, and proceed; existence by construction, he held, could be lodged once in the archives and thereafter taken as read. That is, in fact, how this course now operates and how every working analyst operates: tonight was the archive visit. From next episode onward we stand on the axiom and never again ask what a real number is made of — which is precisely the luxury Dedekind purchased for us, sir, on a November Wednesday, with a drawer, a knife, and no haste whatsoever.

The End of the Ghosts

The axiom is on the books and the warehouse is stocked; now the dividends — three theorems that fall out of completeness the way interest falls out of principal. And I shall introduce a small piece of stagecraft, sir, in my capacity as keeper of the ledger: each time the axiom of completeness is actually deployed in a proof — not admired, deployed — I shall ring a small figurative bell, so that by season's end you will have heard, and counted, precisely how much of analysis is this one sentence working under various names.

The first dividend sounds like a joke until you try to prove it: for every real number, there is a whole number beyond it. The whole numbers are unbounded in the reals; the counting numbers outclimb everything. Equivalently, turned upside down: for every positive real number, however small, some fraction of the form one over n sits below it. The property is named for Archimedes — who, with the scrupulousness we saluted last week, credited it to Eudoxus; the filing errors of history run in both directions.

Now, why on earth does such a statement need proof? Because obvious was the epitaph on every tombstone in episode one, and because — here is the fact that reorders one's furniture — there exist perfectly lawful ordered fields in which it is false. Number systems, sir, obeying every rule of arithmetic and order in the reference letter, that contain elements larger than every whole number, and, therefore, their reciprocals: elements positive yet smaller than every fraction one over n. Infinitely large numbers; infinitesimally small ones. Bishop Berkeley's ghosts of departed quantities, alive, housed, and paying rates — in a legal jurisdiction other than the real numbers. One of tonight's starred desk problems hands you the keys to such a jurisdiction and has you verify the ghosts personally. So the Archimedean property is not a triviality of arithmetic; it is a genuine theorem about the reals specifically, and it must earn its passage. What pays the fare? What else, sir.

The proof fits in the ear entire; attend. Suppose, for contradiction, the whole numbers were bounded above in the reals — some ceiling standing over one, two, three, and all their successors. A non-empty collection, bounded above: the axiom of completeness activates — the bell rings, deployment the first — and hands us the least ceiling, the supremum of the whole numbers; call it s. Now recall clause two of the supremum's job description: dip beneath s by any margin and the collection rises past you. Dip by one. Then s minus one is no ceiling, so some whole number n exceeds s minus one. Add one to both sides: n plus one exceeds s. But n plus one is itself a whole number — and s was hired as a ceiling over all of them. A ceiling with a whole number above it: contradiction, and the supposition dies. The whole numbers are unbounded. And the upside-down form follows in one breath: given any positive tolerance, its reciprocal is exceeded by some whole number n, whence one over n falls below the tolerance. Notice the anatomy of the argument, sir, for it is the season's signature move performed for the first time: completeness manufactures a frontier object; the frontier's own defining clause — the dip-and-overtake clause — is then turned against the supposition. You will see that one-two perhaps thirty times before we finish.

And now the exorcism, formally conducted. An infinitesimal, if the real numbers harboured one, would be a positive number smaller than every fraction one over n. The theorem just proved says every positive real has some one over n beneath it. Therefore the real numbers contain no infinitesimals — none, not as a matter of taste or squeamishness but as a theorem with a bell on it. The evanescent increments that haunted the calculus for two centuries, the quantities neither something nor nothing that Berkeley invoiced in seventeen thirty-four: as of this theorem they have no forwarding address on the real line. When episode four defines limits and episode seven defines the derivative, they will do it in a number system certified ghost-free — that is the entire point of building the system first. Honesty compels one refinement of last week's obituary, and I give it in two sentences: in the nineteen sixties, Abraham Robinson resurrected the infinitesimals with complete rigour, constructing enlarged number systems in which Berkeley's ghosts do honest work — a beautiful story that belongs to another course. In this one, the classical line holds: the reals are Archimedean, the ghosts are evicted, and completeness, you now see, is precisely the instrument that signed the eviction.

The Picket Fence

The second dividend repairs an asymmetry. Tonight began with the rationals dense in themselves — between any two fractions, a third. But we have since moved to a larger country, and the question renews: are the fractions dense in the reals? Between any two real numbers — two cuts, two arbitrary residents of the completed line, possibly both irrational — must a fraction always lie? If the answer were no, the rationals would leave whole stretches of the new line unpatrolled, and every approximation in the world would be in doubt. The answer is yes, and the proof strategy is a picture you will keep for the rest of the course. I shall give you the picture; the desk performs the carpentry with full honesty, including one step people wave at and we shall not.

Two real numbers, then, with a gap between them — a gap of some definite positive width. First move: the Archimedean property, freshly proved, supplies a whole number n so large that one over n is smaller than the width of the gap. Second move: erect the picket fence. Consider every multiple of one over n — zero, one over n, two over n, three over n, and so on, in both directions: fence posts planted along the entire line at perfectly even spacing, each post a fraction, the mesh finer than the gap. Third move, the obvious one that needs the care: a fence whose posts stand closer together than the gap is wide cannot step across the gap — some post must land inside. Which post? The first one past the gap's left edge, sir: march along the fence from inside the gap's territory and take the earliest post that overshoots the left endpoint. It cleared the left edge by construction; it cannot also have cleared the right edge, for then the fence would have straddled the whole gap in a single stride — a stride of one over n across a gap wider than one over n. So that first post stands strictly inside: a fraction between the two reals. The delicate clause is the phrase the first post past the edge — earliest members of infinite collections are exactly the sort of thing episode one taught us to stop taking for free — and the desk secures it with the well-ordering principle from chapter one, doing honestly what most accounts do by gesture. There is your theorem: between any two real numbers lies a rational; and between any two, therefore, infinitely many — apply the theorem inside its own gap, forever. The picket fence, sir. Remember the name; the technique of choosing a mesh finer than your problem returns all season. And observe, sir, that you have trusted the fence your whole life: the decimals are picket fences with meshes of one tenth, one hundredth, one thousandth, and so on — the everyday fact that any real number can be approximated in decimals to any accuracy you please is nothing but the density of that particular family of fences, demonstrated tonight rather than assumed.

And the irrationals return the compliment at once, by a trick of genuine elegance. Two reals, a gap between them. Slide the entire gap downward by exactly root two — the endpoints minus root two frame a new gap of the same width. Plant a fence post: some fraction lies in the slid gap. Now slide back up: that fraction plus root two lies in the original gap. And what is a fraction plus root two? Irrational, sir — rational plus irrational is irrational, a fact you proved yourself, or shortly will, in the second tier of last chapter's problems; tonight it graduates from exercise to load-bearing beam, which is the fate of all good exercises. So: between any two reals, a rational; between any two reals, an irrational. The two societies are interleaved everywhere, each infiltrating every gap of the other, neither ever holding an unbroken stretch of line, however small. Perfect mutual permeation — which invites, does it not, a perfectly natural conclusion: two populations so thoroughly interwoven must surely be populations of the same size. Equal partners, half and half, rational and irrational. Hold that conclusion loosely, sir. Next episode weighs the two crowds, and I shall say only that the scale does not balance, and that the reading is one of the great shocks in the history of thought.

The Russian Dolls

The third dividend is the season's trap-making equipment, and it will be used to catch a number as early as next week — in anger, in a famous proof — so examine the mechanism closely tonight.

The setup: a sequence of closed intervals — closed, both endpoints included, recall the square bracket — each interval containing the next. The first contains the second, the second the third, onward forever: a nest of Russian dolls, possibly shrinking as they go. The theorem — the nested interval theorem — says: some point inhabits every doll. However far the nesting proceeds, however cunningly the intervals shrink, they cannot shrink around nothing; at least one resident survives every stage of the confinement. If the widths are also driven below every tolerance, exactly one resident survives — the dolls become a trap that catches precisely one number, which is how the device will usually be deployed: to corner a quarry.

Why should it be true? Ask what the dolls' left endpoints are doing: each doll begins no earlier than its parent, so the left endpoints form a climbing crowd. And the crowd is bounded above — indeed by every right endpoint of every doll, a small cross-check the desk performs: any left endpoint stands at or below any right endpoint, since of the two dolls involved, one contains the other. A non-empty crowd, climbing, ceilinged: the bell rings, sir, deployment the second. Completeness delivers the supremum of the left endpoints; call it s. Clause one of the job description: s stands at or above every left endpoint. And every right endpoint, being a ceiling for the climbing crowd, stands at or above s — the least ceiling undercuts all rival ceilings. So s is wedged: no earlier than every doll's beginning, no later than every doll's end. The point s lies inside every doll. The trap is certified.

Now the fine print, and tonight the fine print gets the full treatment, because this theorem's hypotheses are load-bearing in a way best learned by watching the theorem die twice. Death the first: drop the word closed. Take the open intervals from zero to one over n — from zero to one, then zero to a half, zero to a third, endpoints excluded every time. Nested: certainly. Shrinking: beautifully. Common resident: none whatsoever. Zero itself is barred at the door of every doll — endpoints excluded. And any positive candidate, however small, is evicted the moment the dolls shrink past it: the Archimedean property — tonight's own dividend, already at work — supplies a whole number n with one over n beneath the candidate, and from that doll onward the candidate stands outside. Every applicant rejected; the nest is empty. The survivor, you see, wanted to live at the boundary, and open intervals are precisely intervals that disown their boundary: the point leaks out through the excluded endpoint. Closed is not decoration, sir; closed is the clause that lets the trap hold its prisoner. Death the second: keep closed, drop bounded. Take the intervals from n to infinity — from one onward, from two onward, from three onward: closed at their left ends, nested, each containing the next. Common resident: again none — any candidate whatsoever is eventually left behind, out-marched by the whole numbers, Archimedes again. An unbounded trap cannot corner anything; the quarry escapes to the right, forever.

So the theorem stands exactly as stated and not one word looser: closed, bounded, nested — a survivor guaranteed; weaken any clause and the nest may be found empty. And I ask you to make a note, sir, in whatever serves you as a commonplace book, of the pair of words that has now surfaced together for the first time: closed and bounded. You will meet the pair again in episode four, twice; and in episode six, holding up two of the three great theorems about continuous functions; and episode five exists, in its entirety, to explain why this particular pair of hypotheses keeps being the price of admission — to discover what closed and bounded really is when it stops being a coincidence and acquires a name and a throne. Consider the phrase a recurring character introduced tonight in a walk-on role. As for the dolls themselves: next week they graduate from equipment to weapon. In eighteen seventy-four, in his first great paper, Georg Cantor used precisely this theorem — these dolls — to prove that the real numbers cannot be listed, and the proof is on next week's programme in full. The trap you inspected tonight, sir, is the murder weapon in advance.

Terms of Engagement

The desk's half of tonight, then — chapter two of the volume — toured briskly, for the architecture is now familiar and only the inventory is new.

The notation ledger opens as always: the supremum and infimum acquire their written abbreviations, the axiom its formal one-line statement, the cut its official costume — on paper a cut travels light, carrying only its left camp, the right camp being understood as everything else — and the intervals their brackets, square and round, whose difference tonight stopped being typographic and started being the difference between a trap that holds and a trap that leaks. One clerical confession: the phrase I have been pronouncing all evening as dip beneath it by any margin, however slight appears on paper wearing a small Greek letter — epsilon, doing quiet clerical work in the supremum's second clause. Its formal debut in the audio, with full ceremony and an adversary attached, remains episode four's affair; on paper it is already employed, and you may as well begin nodding to it in the corridor.

The proofs in full, this chapter: the Archimedean property, exactly as narrated, one page with the bell still warm. The density of the rationals, with the picket fence erected honestly — the first post past the edge secured by well-ordering rather than by gesture — and the irrational corollary with the sliding trick. The nested interval theorem, with the cross-check performed and both deaths — the leaking open nest, the escaping unbounded one — computed in the margin. The pooling proof, five lines, that the system of cuts is complete: the set piece's serene finale in formal dress. The verification that the sum of two cuts is a cut. And one proof I flag with particular ceremony: the existence of the square root of two. You will recall — the honesty clause obliges me to keep such wounds open until healed — that chapter one carried a caution after its sixth theorem: we had proved no rational squares to two, while the existence of any number squaring to two remained an unpaid claim. Chapter two discharges it. The set of positive reals whose square falls short of two is non-empty and bounded above; completeness — the bell rings a third time, on paper — delivers its supremum; and a two-sided squeeze, fully written out, shows that supremum's square can neither fall short of two nor exceed it. It equals two. The number exists, manufactured by the axiom, and the diagonal of the unit square has, at long last and beyond all litigation, a length. Chapter one's caution is stamped paid, and with it, sir, the count of completeness deployments stands at three before the first problem is assigned.

The problems. Tier one, Calisthenics: frontier drills — compute the supremum and infimum, where they exist, of a small zoo of collections, deciding in each case whether the frontier is occupied; prove the supremum unique; prove that enlarging a collection can only raise its supremum; and chase the marching collection one minus one over n past any dipped ceiling with an Archimedean choice of threshold, tonight's third specimen done with full paperwork. Tier two, The Real Thing, five items: first, the shortfall club's missing supremum, completed — the squeeze I sketched performed with a single, rather elegant fraction that handles both halves of the argument in one motion, and since the audio sketched it, the solution is supplied in full; second, the sum rule — the supremum of a sum of two collections is the sum of their suprema — the workhorse identity of episode four, solution likewise supplied; third, the theorem that every real number is the supremum of the fractions beneath it — Eudoxus's company, sir, notarized at last: each number recoverable from, because constituted by, its rational entourage — hint only; fourth, a nest of closed intervals with rational endpoints, shrinking dutifully, whose common resident is irrational — so that inside the rationals the nest catches nothing, and the nested interval theorem is revealed as completeness in disguise rather than a free gift of order — hint only; fifth, the interleaving drill: between any two irrationals a rational, between any two rationals an irrational, the two societies certified mutually permeating in both directions. Tier three, For the Ambitious, two stars. The first: the multiplication of cuts — you are given the definition for the positive case, asked to verify it produces a lawful cut, and then invited to draft the definition for arbitrary signs, at which point you will discover personally why I called it a swamp; references are supplied to where the full slog is performed by professionals, and having tasted it, you may cite it forever after with a clear conscience. The second star, and I confess it my favourite problem of the young season: the jurisdiction of the ghosts. You are handed an ordered field built from ratios of polynomials, ordered by eventual dominance — who wins far down the line — and asked to verify the order laws, to exhibit an element that exceeds every whole number, to exhibit its reciprocal, positive yet beneath every fraction one over n, and to conclude the field is not Archimedean and therefore, by tonight's first dividend read in reverse, not complete. Berkeley's ghosts, sir, alive and lawfully housed one jurisdiction over — and completeness thereby revealed as exactly what it is: not a nicety, but the eviction order. Hints only, naturally; one does not chaperone a gentleman through a haunted house.

Solutions policy as established: tier one complete and terse, tier two selected, tier three hints alone. Listen, then work; the ear was tonight's, the desk is yours.

Until Next Time

The ledger, sir, briefly, as is now our custom. Account the first — what a number actually is — PAID, this hour, in full, by R. Dedekind of Brunswick; the stamp has rarely given me such satisfaction. Newly opened tonight, account the seventh: the axiom of completeness to be caught red-handed in every major theorem of the season — the bell has rung three times already, twice aloud and once on paper, and the count is running. Standing accounts: Zeno, due episode four; Fourier, instalments across both seasons; the monsters, first delivery episode six; the exchange of limits, memorandum until episode four. All current, none forgotten.

Next time, sir: infinity is weighed. Two infinite crowds — the fractions, dense, everywhere, insinuated into every gap on the line; and the reals entire. Common sense reports both crowds are simply infinite, and that is the end of the matter. Common sense is wrong twice over. The rationals, for all their density, can be marched single file — counted, listed, every last one — and the reals cannot: there are at least two sizes of infinity, the fact is provable, and Georg Cantor proved it in eighteen seventy-four using, as the murder weapon, tonight's own Russian dolls. You will also witness the four most consequential lines in mathematics, meet a dust made by deleting middle thirds forever, and hear of a question about infinity that the twentieth century proved unanswerable from the standard axioms — not unanswered, sir; unanswerable. Bring your intuition. It will not be coming back intact.

I remain, in the meantime, bounded above by my duties and below by my standards — the frontier, as always, occupied.

Good day, sir.
