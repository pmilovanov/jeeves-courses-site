Chapter 7Fubini, or the Crime of Swapping Integrals

Companion to Episode Seven. The episode built the plane from two lines (rectangles, the product tribe, Carathéodory rerun; the two routes agreeing), sharpened both knives (sections, with the monotone-class beam flagged honestly as the season’s one labelled borrowing), proved Tonelli by the four-stage reel (rectangles, tribe, staircases, climb), derived Fubini by gains and losses, canonized the standard operating procedure (check with Tonelli, spend with Fubini), convicted the staircase-and-shadows function at the crime scene (both itineraries computed; diagnosis: Season One’s rearrangement crime in planar dress), witnessed the sigma-finite fine print at the counting-measure diagonal, and banked the layer cake (wonder beat: the two seasons’ pictures of an integral meeting in the subgraph). Episode Digest

Sections are measurable (the monotone-class beam, labelled). The crime scene: the diagonal staircase with rightward shadows — columns total one then nought (the ground floor casts no shadow into itself), rows total nought without exception; iterated integrals one and nought, both finite, both correct; absolute mass infinite; the rearrangement theorem in planar dress. Fine print: sigma-finiteness (the counting-measure diagonal: nonnegative, measurable, itineraries nought and one). The layer cake: integrate the subgraph’s indicator both ways — vertical slices give the integral, horizontal slices give the acreage-above-height — Tonelli’s somersault; the bridge to tail probabilities pre-signed for the finale.

7.2 The Product Construction

Definition 7.1 (Rectangles).

A measurable rectangle is $A \times B$ with $A, B \in \mathcal{M}$ (on the two factor lines), priced $\rho(A \times B) = m(A) \, m(B)$ (extended arithmetic; $0 \cdot \infty = 0$).

Theorem 7.2 (The product measure).

On $\mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{M} = \sigma(\{\text{rectangles}\})$ there is a measure $m \times m$ agreeing with $\rho$ on rectangles; on sigma-finite factors (our case) it is the unique such.

Proof.

Existence: run Carathéodory on countable rectangle covers — define the planar outer measure as the infimum of $\sum_k \rho(R_k)$ over rectangle covers; the 1914 staircase (Chapter 2, quoted, not re-proved: symmetry, composition, proportion, limit) yields a sigma-algebra of splitters carrying a countably additive restriction; rectangles split cleanly (the half-plane turnstiles $A \times \R$ and $\R \times B$ mirror Chapter 2’s half-lines — Problem II.1 performs the audit), so the splitters include the rectangles, hence the generated tribe. That $\rho$ is itself countably additive on rectangles — the covering bound needs it — is Problem II.1’s second half, a Tonelli-in- miniature for indicator slices (or: the desk’s direct route via the line’s MCT on sections of a disjoint rectangle union). Uniqueness: two agreeing measures on rectangles agree on finite frames $[-N,N]^2$ by the frame squeeze of Chapter 2’s uniqueness theorem transplanted verbatim (rectangles play intervals; the estate’s robustness plays itself), and sigma-finiteness patches the frames by the crankshaft.

□
Corollary 7.3 (The two routes agree).

The direct planar Lebesgue measure (Chapter 2’s dimension license) and $m \times m$ coincide on the product tribe.

Proof.

Both keep the rectangle clause (boxes are rectangles; the direct construction prices boxes by area — its own interval theorem run twice); uniqueness closes. (The product tribe sits inside the direct construction’s full commonwealth; on the common jurisdiction they agree, and the completion remark of the Notes handles the veil.)

□
Definition 7.4 (Sections).

For $E$ in the plane and $y$ fixed: $E^{y} = \{ x : (x,y) \in E \}$; dually $E_{x}$. For functions: $f^{y}(x) = f(x, y)$, $f_{x}(y) = f(x, y)$.

Lemma 7.5 (Sections are measurable).

If $E \in \mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{M}$, every section $E^{y}, E_{x}$ is a citizen of its line; if $f$ is product-measurable, every section function is measurable.

Remark 7.6 (The monotone-class beam — the labelled borrowing).

The collection of sets with all sections measurable contains the rectangles ($ (A \times B)^{y}$ is $A$ or $\emptyset$) and is a sigma-algebra (sections commute with complements and countable unions — the accessible half, proved here in four lines: $(\bigcup_k E_k)^{y} = \bigcup_k E_k^{y}$ and $(E^{c})^{y} = (E^{y})^{c}$); hence it contains the generated tribe, and Lemma 7.5 follows — for sets. For the measure-theoretic upgrades Tonelli’s stage two needs — that $y \mapsto m(E^{y})$ is measurable with $\int m(E^{y}) \, dm(y) = (m \times m)(E)$ — the standard induction over the tribe wants the monotone class theorem: stated here (a monotone class containing an algebra contains its generated sigma-algebra), its proof cited to the shelves (Folland §2.1; Axler 5A; Royden ch. 20) per the plan’s instruction to flag the machinery honestly. The sigma-finite bookkeeping of the induction is Problem III.1’s guided skeleton. One beam, borrowed, labelled; everything else in the chapter is house timber.

7.3 The Matched Pair

Theorem 7.7 (Tonelli, 1909).

Let $f \ge 0$ be product-measurable (sigma-finite factors). Then $y \mapsto \int f^{y} \, dm$ and $x \mapsto \int f_{x} \, dm$ are measurable, and

\[ \iint f \, d(m \times m) \ =\ \int \! \Bigl( \int f(x,y) \, dm(x) \Bigr) dm(y) \ =\ \int \! \Bigl( \int f(x,y) \, dm(y) \Bigr) dm(x), \]

all three together, $\infty$ included.

Proof.

The four-stage reel. Rectangles: for $f = \mathbf{1}_{A \times B}$ all three read $m(A) m(B)$ by inspection. Tribe: the class of sets whose indicators satisfy the display contains the rectangles and is a monotone class closed under the algebra’s operations — the beam (Remark 7.6) carries it to $\mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{M}$; sigma-finite frames guard the complementation step (the guided skeleton). Staircases: linearity (Chapter 5’s discharged additivity, on the plane and the line alike) extends the display to simple functions. Climb: the staircase theorem writes $f$ as an increasing simple limit; monotone convergence (Chapter 5) passes the limit through all three integrals at once — the inner integrals climb by MCT on each section, and their integrals climb by MCT again. Rectangles, tribe, staircases, climb.

□
Theorem 7.8 (Fubini, 1907).

Let $f$ be product-measurable with $\iint \lvert f \rvert \, d(m \times m) < \infty$. Then for almost every $y$ the section $f^{y}$ is integrable (and dually), the a.e.- defined iterated integrals exist, and both equal $\iint f \, d(m \times m)$.

Proof.

Tonelli on $\lvert f \rvert$ prices $\int \bigl( \int \lvert f^{y} \rvert \bigr) dm(y) < \infty$, so the inner integral is finite for a.e. $y$ (Chapter 4’s little lemma, I.6, in its finite-a.e. form): bad slices are null, amnestied. Split $f = f^{+} - f^{-}$; Tonelli prices each part’s display; subtract — lawful, both totals finite by the hypothesis — section by section on the good slices and integral by integral overall (linearity).

□
Remark 7.9 (The standard operating procedure — canonized).

To swap: if $f \ge 0$, swap (Tonelli). Else compute $\iint \lvert f \rvert$ by either itinerary (Tonelli again — the check itself is swappable); if finite, swap (Fubini); if infinite, do not — you stand at the crime scene. Chapter 5’s small promise, discharged; the constitution is Season One’s series doctrine (absolute licenses rearrangement) in planar dress.

7.4 The Crime Scene and the Fine Print

Proposition 7.10 (The staircase and its shadows).

On $[0,\infty)^2$ let

\[ f \ =\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \Bigl( \mathbf{1}_{[k, k+1) \times [k, k+1)} \; - \; \mathbf{1}_{[k+1, k+2) \times [k, k+1)} \Bigr). \]

Then both iterated integrals exist and

\[ \int \! \Bigl( \int f \, dm(y) \Bigr) dm(x) \ =\ 1, \qquad \int \! \Bigl( \int f \, dm(x) \Bigr) dm(y) \ =\ 0, \]

while $\iint \lvert f \rvert = \infty$: the integrability check fails, and no license was issued.

Proof.

Columns (fix $x \in [j, j+1)$, integrate $dm(y)$): for $j = 0$ the column meets only the $k{=}0$ positive block: inner integral $1$. For $j \ge 1$: the positive block $k = j$ contributes $+1$ on $y \in [j, j+1)$ and the shadow with $k+1 = j$, i.e. $k = j - 1$, contributes $-1$ on $y \in [j-1, j)$: inner integral $0$. Outer: $\int = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. Rows (fix $y \in [k, k+1)$, integrate $dm(x)$): the storey’s positive block on $x \in [k, k+1)$ and its own shadow on $x \in [k+1, k+2)$: inner integral $0$, every $k \ge 0$ — shadows are cast sideways, so no storey lacks one. Outer: $0$. Absolute mass: each of infinitely many blocks carries $1$: $\iint \lvert f \rvert = \infty$ (Tonelli’s own count, either order). Diagnosis: the two itineraries are two orderings of the conditionally convergent block-series $1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots$-flavoured mass; Volume One’s rearrangement theorem, staged on the quarter-plane. Solved in full as Problem II.2, block by block.

□
Remark 7.11 (Sigma-finiteness’s witness).

Pair $([0,1], m)$ with $([0,1], \#)$ — counting measure, not sigma-finite — and take $f = \mathbf{1}_{\Delta}$, the diagonal’s indicator. Then $\int \bigl( \int f \, d\#(y) \bigr) dm(x) = \int 1 \, dm = 1$ (each $x$-section is one point, counted once), while $\int \bigl( \int f \, dm(x) \bigr) d\#(y) = \int 0 \, d\# = 0$ (each $y$-section is one point, length nought). Nonnegative, measurable in the appropriate joint sense, disagreeing: the sigma-finite clause in Theorems 7.7–7.8 is real. (Three lines, as promised; the measurability fine print of the diagonal in the product tribe is the Notes’ small-print reference.)

7.5 The Layer Cake

Theorem 7.12 (The layer-cake representation).

For measurable $f \ge 0$ on sigma-finite ground $E$:

\[ \int_{E} f \, dm \ =\ \int_{0}^{\infty} m\{ x \in E : f(x) > t \} \, dm(t). \]
Proof.

Tonelli’s somersault on the subgraph indicator $\mathbf{1}_{S}$, $S = \{ (x, t) : 0 \le t < f(x) \}$ (product-measurable: $S = \bigcup_{q \in \Q^{+}} \{f > q\} \times [0, q)$-flavoured assembly, Problem II.4). Heights first: $\int_0^\infty \mathbf{1}_S(x, t) \, dm(t) = f(x)$; then ground: the left side. Ground first: $\int_E \mathbf{1}_S(x, t) \, dm(x) = m\{f > t\}$; then heights: the right side. Tonelli licenses the equality unconditionally. — The finale’s uniform: expectation equals the integral of tail probabilities.

□

7.7 Hints

II.3.  One bad slice suffices and one is null: take any integrable $f_0(x, y)$ and redefine it on the single line $y = y_0$ to be a non-integrable section there (the spike family’s supremum, say, $1/x$ on $(0,1]$). The line is planar-null, so the redefinition changes neither product-measurability (amnesty) nor the double integral; yet the $y_0$-section is honestly non-integrable. The theorem’s “almost every slice” is exactly calibrated to tolerate it.

II.6.  Measurability: $(x,y) \mapsto x - y$ is continuous; $f$ measurable on the line; the safe direction (Chapter 4) needs $f$ Borel — upgrade by amnesty: replace $f$ by a Borel a.e.-equal version (Chapter 2’s structure theorem), the null discrepancy sweeping into planar null by Tonelli on the indicator.

III.2.  Rectangles with three sides generate both parenthesizations; uniqueness on sigma-finite frames closes.

7.8 Solutions

Tier I (terse).  I.3’s middle: $\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 (x + y) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12$ by staircase symmetry, either order. I.6: $\int_0^1 (1 - t) \, dt$ computed by the identity’s own layer cake — the season eating its own cooking. I.8: the tent on $[0, 2]$, peak one at $x=1$: $\min(x, 2-x)^{+}$.

II.2 (solution in full).  As Proposition 7.10, expanded: the column table for $j = 0, 1, 2$ and the general row; the absolute count $\sum_k (1 + 1) = \infty$; and the diagnosis displayed as a rearrangement: enumerate the blocks column-first and the partial sums visit $1$ eternally; row-first and they visit $0$ eternally — two orderings of $\pm 1$ masses, Volume One, Chapter 4’s theorem quoted with its chapter number.

II.6 (solution in full).  Measurability. Take $\tilde f$ Borel with $\tilde f = f$ a.e. (Chapter 2, Theorem 2.17 — an $F_\sigma$-built version); then $(x,y) \mapsto \tilde f(x-y)$ is Borel$\circ$continuous: product-Borel-measurable (Chapter 4’s safe direction on the plane); $\tilde f(x-y) g(y)$ product-measurable (algebra). The exceptional set $\{ \tilde f(x-y) \ne f(x-y) \}$ is planar-null: its $y$-sections are translates of a line-null set, and Tonelli on its indicator prices it at $\int 0 = 0$. Pricing. Tonelli (nonnegative case), inner over $x$ first: $\int f(x-y) \, dm(x) = \lVert f \rVert_1$ for every $y$ (translation invariance), so $\iint = \lVert f \rVert_1 \lVert g \rVert_1 < \infty$. Existence a.e. The $x$-sections’ finiteness for a.e. $x$ is the little lemma on the finite double integral (via the reversed itinerary — Tonelli again). Signed case: run the above on $\lvert f \rvert, \lvert g \rvert$ for the lid, then Fubini’s bookkeeping; $\lVert f * g \rVert_1 \le \lVert f \rVert_1 \lVert g \rVert_1$ by the triangle inequality under the integral. The algebra of convolution — commutativity, the smoothing folklore — is shelved (Notes); tonight only its existence papers were promised.

7.9 Notes

History. Fubini (1907), Tonelli (1909); the modern matched- pair presentation is the textbooks’ distillation. The staircase-and-shadows crime scene is the standard discrete-flavoured witness (Folland’s exercise circle); the classical smooth witness (the trade knows it with arctangents) is avoided here deliberately — its evaluation spends trigonometry, and Volume One’s loan L1 remains unspent by this course’s proofs.

The diagonal’s small print. In Remark 7.11, joint measurability of $\Delta$ holds in the product of Borel tribes; the pathology is additivity’s, not measurability’s — Folland §2.3 for the careful version.

Reading. Axler 5A–5B; Folland §2.1–2.3 (whose monotone-class staging this chapter’s labelled borrowing follows); Stein–Shakarchi ch. 2; Royden ch. 20. Convolution’s onward algebra: Folland §8.1.
