Lemma 8.1 (Pushforward).

For a random variable $X$ on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, $P_X(B) = P(X^{-1}(B))$ defines a measure on the Borel line with total mass one, and for $g \ge 0$ Borel, $\mathbb{E}\, g(X) = \int g \, dP_X$ (the change-of-variables identity).

Proof.

Preimages respect complements and countable disjoint unions (Chapter 4’s preimage bookkeeping), so countable additivity transfers; mass one is $X^{-1}(\R) = \Omega$. The identity: true for indicators by definition, lifts by staircases and the climb (Chapter 5’s reel, one dimension).

□
Proposition 8.2 (Independence; factored expectations).

Random variables $X, Y$ are independent iff the joint pushforward on the plane is the product of the marginals: $P_{(X,Y)} = P_X \times P_Y$. In that case, for integrable $X, Y$: $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}X \cdot \mathbb{E}Y$.

Proof.

The iff is definitional bookkeeping on rectangles plus uniqueness (Chapter 7). Factoring: for $X, Y \ge 0$, Tonelli on the product (via Lemma 8.1 to the plane): $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \iint xy \, d(P_X \times P_Y) = \int x \, dP_X \int y \, dP_Y$; signed case by the SOP — the absolute check factors first, licensing Fubini.

□

8.2 Borel–Cantelli

Theorem 8.3 (First lemma: finite risk).

If $\sum_n P(A_n) < \infty$, then $P(A_n \text{ infinitely often}) = 0$: almost surely only finitely many $A_n$ occur.

Proof.

This is Chapter 6’s tail-trap under its proper name, cross-reference stamped (Theorem 6.5’s engine): with $B_K = \bigcup_{n \ge K} A_n$, $P(B_K) \le \sum_{n \ge K} P(A_n) \to 0$ (subadditivity; the convergent tail), and $\{A_n \text{ i.o.}\} = \bigcap_K B_K$ has mass at most every tail: zero. The gambit’s skeleton, redeemed as promised.

□
Theorem 8.4 (Second lemma: independent infinite risk).

If the $A_n$ are independent and $\sum_n P(A_n) = \infty$, then $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 1$.

Proof.

Complements: for $K \le M$, $P\bigl( \bigcap_{n=K}^{M} A_n^{c} \bigr) = \prod_{n=K}^{M} (1 - P(A_n))$ (independence passes to complements: Problem I.5) $\le \prod \exp(-P(A_n)) = \exp\bigl( - \sum_{K}^{M} P(A_n) \bigr) \to 0$ as $M \to \infty$ (divergence; the inequality $1 - t \le e^{-t}$ is the desk’s one-line convexity-free calculus: the difference vanishes at $t=0$ with nonpositive derivative — Volume One’s harvest; the exponential rides Volume One’s $e$-manufacture). So $P(\bigcup_{n \ge K} A_n) = 1$ for every $K$; shrinking intersection over $K$ (mass one throughout, the finite floor trivial) keeps mass one.

□

8.3 The Laws

Proposition 8.5 (Chebyshev, christened).

For $X$ with $\mathbb{E} X = \mu$ and $\operatorname{Var} X = \mathbb{E}(X - \mu)^2 = \sigma^2 < \infty$, and any $\varepsilon > 0$: $P( \lvert X - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon ) \le \sigma^2 / \varepsilon^2$.

Proof.

Chapter 6’s embryo (mass bounds acreage, Lemma 6.2) applied to $g = (X - \mu)^2$ at level $\varepsilon^2$.

□
Theorem 8.6 (The weak law of large numbers).

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent with a common distribution, $\mathbb{E} X_i = \mu$, $\operatorname{Var} X_i = \sigma^2 < \infty$; let $\bar{X}_n = \tfrac1n \sum_{i \le n} X_i$. Then $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ in probability.

Proof.

$\mathbb{E} \bar{X}_n = \mu$ (linearity, Chapter 5’s discharged credit). Variance: $\operatorname{Var} \bar{X}_n = \tfrac{1}{n^2} \sum_i \operatorname{Var} X_i = \sigma^2 / n$, the cross-terms $\mathbb{E}(X_i - \mu)(X_j - \mu)$ vanishing for $i \ne j$ by factored expectations (Proposition 8.2) — the two-line computation displayed at Problem II.2. Chebyshev: $P(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \le \sigma^2 / (n \varepsilon^2) \to 0$. Four sentences, as the episode promised.

□
Theorem 8.7 (The strong law — stated with respect).

With only $\mathbb{E} \lvert X_i \rvert < \infty$ (independent, common distribution): $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ almost surely.

Sketch and references. Etemadi’s elementary route (1981): reduce to nonnegative variables; truncate $Y_i = X_i \mathbf{1}_{X_i \le i}$ (Borel–Cantelli reconciling $X_i$ with $Y_i$ via $\sum P(X_i > i) \le \mathbb{E} X_1 < \infty$ — the layer cake pricing the sum); control variances of truncations along geometric subsequences $n_k = \lfloor \alpha^k \rfloor$ by Chebyshev and a summable bound; sandwich general $n$ between geometric marks by monotonicity. Every instrument is the course’s; the bookkeeping is a further evening’s, and the honesty clause files it: Durrett §2.4, Williams ch. 12, Etemadi’s original two pages.

8.4 Radon–Nikodym and Conditional Expectation

Theorem 8.8 (Radon–Nikodym — stated; necessity proved).

Let $\mu, \nu$ be sigma-finite on a common tribe. There exists measurable $w \ge 0$ (the density, $w = \tfrac{d\nu}{d\mu}$, unique up to $\mu$-amnesty) with

\[ \nu(A) \ =\ \int_A w \, d\mu \quad \text{for all citizens } A \]

if and only if $\nu \ll \mu$ (absolute continuity: $\mu(A) = 0 \Rightarrow \nu(A) = 0$). Necessity is one line: an integral over a $\mu$-null set is zero (Chapter 4, manners (iv)). Sufficiency is stated on references (von Neumann’s Hilbert- space route rides the seventeenth ring’s geometry; the classical route rides a supremum-of-candidates exhaustion): Axler 9A, Folland §3.2, Royden ch. 18. Worked densities: Problems I.6–I.7 (uniform, reweighted, two-model likelihood ratio); the derivative calculus (chain rule, change of measure under integrals): Problem II.4.

Proposition 8.9 (The staircase’s distribution: the singular witness).

Let $c$ be the Cantor function (Chapter 4) read as a cumulative distribution: the measure $\mu_c$ with $\mu_c\bigl( [0, x] \bigr) = c(x)$. Then $\mu_c$ is a mass-one measure carried entirely by the dust ($\mu_c(C) = 1$, though $m(C) = 0$), with no atoms ($\mu_c\{x\} = 0$ for every $x$), and consequently no density with respect to length exists: $\mu_c \not\ll m$. Singular continuous — the standing witness that Theorem 8.8’s clause is real.

Proof.

Existence and countable additivity of $\mu_c$: $c$ is continuous and nondecreasing; the Stieltjes-flavoured construction runs Carathéodory on intervals priced by increments $c(b) - c(a)$ — Chapter 2’s staircase verbatim (Problem II.5 audits the rectangle clauses). Mass on the dust: each removed middle carries increment zero ($c$ constant there), so the complement’s countably many beads carry $\mu_c$-mass zero; additivity leaves everything on $C$. No atoms: $\mu_c\{x\} = c(x) - c(x^{-}) = 0$ by continuity. No density: $m(C) = 0$ with $\mu_c(C) = 1$ violates absolute continuity; necessity (proved above) forbids the density. The course’s fondest monster retires as the fine print of its final theorem.

□
Definition 8.10 (Conditional expectation).

Let $X$ be integrable on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ and $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ a sub-tribe. The conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the ($P$-a.s. unique) $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, integrable random variable $Z$ with

\[ \int_G Z \, dP \ =\ \int_G X \, dP \quad \text{for every } G \in \mathcal{G}. \]

Existence and uniqueness: apply Theorem 8.8 on $(\Omega, \mathcal{G})$ to $\nu(G) = \int_G X \, dP$ against $P\restriction_{\mathcal{G}}$ ($X \ge 0$ first; parts for signs) — $\nu \ll P$ by manners (iv), and the density is $Z$. (The finale states R–N; conditional expectation inherits the same stated-with-references standing for existence, with everything else below proved outright.)

Proposition 8.11 (The finite case; first properties).

(i) If $\mathcal{G}$ is generated by a finite partition $\{G_1, \dots, G_m\}$ with $P(G_j) > 0$, then on each cell $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \tfrac{1}{P(G_j)} \int_{G_j} X \, dP$: the cell average, the obvious best guess. (ii) Linearity; monotonicity. (iii) Tower rule: for $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$, $\mathbb{E}\bigl[ \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \bigm| \mathcal{H} \bigr] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$; in particular $\mathbb{E}\bigl[ \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \bigr] = \mathbb{E}X$. (iv) Taking out what is known: for bounded $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $Y$: $\mathbb{E}[XY \mid \mathcal{G}] = Y \, \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$.

Proof.

(i): the displayed $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable (constant on cells) and matches integrals on each generator, hence on the generated tribe (finite unions); uniqueness by the defining property. (ii): integrate the defining identity. (iii): both sides are $\mathcal{H}$-measurable and match integrals over every $H \in \mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$ — two applications of the definition. (iv): for indicators $Y = \mathbf{1}_{G'}$ the identity is the definition restricted to $G \cap G'$; staircases and bounded convergence (Chapter 5’s BCT, mass-one ground) lift to bounded $Y$. Worked two-block portfolio: Problem II.3. Projection characterization (square-integrable $X$: the a.s.-unique $\mathcal{G}$-measurable minimizer of $\mathbb{E}(X - Z)^2$, orthogonal projection in the seventeenth ring’s geometry): stated, with the $L^2$ fine print, references Axler 9B, Williams ch. 9 — Problem III.2 stages it.

□

8.5 The Last Settlement: the Bounded Estate Recovered

One account in the audit deserves its payment executed rather than merely announced. Debt Twelve, issued at Season One’s summit: Volterra’s function is differentiable at every point with a bounded derivative, and the Riemann integral refuses to read that derivative — the fundamental theorem’s crack. The terms fixed at issue promised that Volume Two would recover the bounded estate: every bounded derivative integrates, in the new theory, to the net change of its primitive. The season’s machine is now complete, and the payment takes four steps. (The name does double duty, and not by accident: a bounded derivative on finite land lives precisely in Chapter 4’s bounded estate.)

Theorem 8.12 (The bounded estate recovered — D12, bounded case).

Let $f : [a,b] \to \R$ be differentiable at every point of $[a,b]$ (one-sidedly at the endpoints), with $\lvert f'(x) \rvert \le M$ for all $x$. Then $f'$ is Lebesgue integrable on $[a,b]$ and

\[ \int_{[a,b]} f' \, dm \;=\; f(b) - f(a). \]
Proof.

Step 1: extend. Prolong $f$ to $[a,\, b+1]$ along the tangent line at $b$: set $f(x) := f(b) + f'(b)(x - b)$ for $x \in (b,\, b+1]$. The extension is differentiable at every point of $[a,\, b+1]$ (at $b$ the one-sided derivatives agree at $f'(b)$), its derivative is bounded by $M$ throughout, and $f$ is continuous.

Step 2: quotients under a constant lid. For $n \ge 1$ define on $[a,b]$

\[ q_n(x) := n \Bigl( f\bigl(x + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr) - f(x) \Bigr). \]

Each $q_n$ is continuous, hence measurable; differentiability at each point gives $q_n \to f'$ everywhere on $[a,b]$ (at $b$, exactly: the linear extension makes $q_n(b) = f'(b)$ for every $n$). So $f'$ is a pointwise limit of measurable functions — measurable by limit stability (Theorem 4.6) — and bounded by $M$, hence integrable on finite land (Proposition 4.14). The mean value theorem — Volume One’s Chapter Seven workhorse, proved there in full — supplies the lid: $q_n(x) = f'(\xi)$ for some $\xi \in (x,\, x + \tfrac{1}{n})$, so $\lvert q_n \rvert \le M$: a constant, integrable on $[a,b]$.

Step 3: the licensed swap. Dominated convergence (Theorem 5.8) gives

\[ \int_{[a,b]} f' \, dm \;=\; \lim_{n} \int_{[a,b]} q_n \, dm . \]

Step 4: compute the right-hand side with Volume One’s own instruments. Each $q_n$ is continuous, so its Lebesgue integral is its Riemann integral (Theorem 4.19); we may compute in the old regime. Let $F(x) := \int_a^x f$ (Riemann) on $[a,\, b+1]$; the fundamental theorem’s first part (Volume One, Chapter Eight; $f$ continuous) gives $F' = f$, the chain rule (Volume One, Chapter Seven) gives $\frac{d}{dx} F\bigl(x + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr) = f\bigl(x + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr)$, and the evaluation part then yields

\[ \int_a^b f\bigl(x + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr)\, dx = F\bigl(b + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr) - F\bigl(a + \tfrac{1}{n}\bigr), \]

whence, subtracting $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$ and scaling,

\[ \int_{[a,b]} q_n \, dm \;=\; n \int_b^{b + 1/n} f \;-\; n \int_a^{a + 1/n} f . \]

For any $c$, continuity of $f$ at $c$ prices the average:

\[ \Bigl\lvert \, n \int_c^{c + 1/n} f \; - \; f(c) \Bigr\rvert = \Bigl\lvert \, n \int_c^{c + 1/n} \bigl( f - f(c) \bigr) \Bigr\rvert \le \sup_{[c,\, c + 1/n]} \lvert f - f(c) \rvert \longrightarrow 0 . \]

The two averages converge to $f(b)$ and $f(a)$, and the theorem follows.

□
Remark 8.13 (The terms, honoured; the crack’s witness reads).

Volterra’s function — differentiable everywhere, derivative bounded, Riemann helpless before it (Volume One, Chapter Eight) — is a citizen of Theorem 8.12: the Lebesgue integral reads its derivative and returns the net change, and the fundamental theorem’s bounded estate is recovered exactly as Debt Twelve’s terms promised. Every instrument in the payment is on the premises and paid for: limit stability and the two ledgers from Chapter 4, the constant lid from Volume One’s mean value theorem, the swap from Chapter 5’s dominated convergence, the computation by the absorption theorem and Volume One’s fundamental theorem — no new completeness deployment anywhere (the bell’s business tonight is retirement, not new rings). The unbounded estate — derivatives existing everywhere but unbounded — remains honestly beyond the course, in the gauge integral’s jurisdiction (Denjoy, Perron, Henstock, Kurzweil; named at Volume One’s close), precisely as declared at issue.

II.1 (solution in full).  As Theorem 8.4, expanded: complement independence for finite families by I.5 and induction; the product bound $\prod_{K}^{M}(1 - p_n) \le \exp(-\sum_K^M p_n)$ from $1 - t \le e^{-t}$ on $[0, 1]$ — proved by Volume One’s harvest: $h(t) = e^{-t} - 1 + t$ has $h(0) = 0$ and $h'(t) = 1 - e^{-t} \ge 0$ (the exponential’s monotonicity, manufactured in Volume One, Chapter 4’s desk); divergence sends the exponent to $-\infty$, the product to $0$; hence $P(\bigcap_{n \ge K} A_n^c) = 0$ by shrinking chains from the finite stages (mass-one ground: the finite floor is free), so $P(\bigcup_{n \ge K} A_n) = 1$ for every $K$; the i.o. set is the shrinking intersection of these mass-one tails: mass one.

II.3 (solution in full).  Cells $C_1, \dots, C_4$, each mass $\tfrac14$, with $X = 1, 3, 5, 7$. Fine coarsening $\mathcal{G}$: pairs $\{C_1 C_2\}, \{C_3 C_4\}$ — $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = 2$ on the first pair, $6$ on the second (cell averages). Coarse $\mathcal{H}$: everything — $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}] = 4$. Tower, numerically: the $\mathcal{H}$-average of the $\mathcal{G}$-guess is $\tfrac12 \cdot 2 + \tfrac12 \cdot 6 = 4$: matches. Each defining integral displayed cell by cell; uniqueness by the finite case of Proposition 8.11(i).

8.9 Notes, and the Consolidated Further Reading

History. Kolmogorov’s Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung (1933) — the sixty pages; Borel (1909) and Cantelli (1917); Chebyshev (1867), in Markov’s telling; Radon (1913), Nikodym (1930); Etemadi (1981). The dictionary’s philosophical reception — probability as measure, full stop — took a generation; Doob’s advocacy settled it.

The further-reading essay, per the plan’s final mandate. The course ends at the doorstep, and three doors stand labelled. The martingale door: Williams, Probability with Martingales — conditional expectation, tonight’s crowning dividend, becomes the subject itself; the strong law, the convergence theorems’ probabilistic descendants, optional stopping; a Season Three’s natural spine, and the repayment venue for loan L1 (power series arrive with characteristic functions) and the logarithm’s credit alike. The functional-analytic door: Axler chs. 6–10 and Folland chs. 5–6 — the seventeenth ring’s geometry taken seriously; Hilbert space, duality (Hölder’s exile repaid with interest), the spectral theorem; Fourier’s account, this course’s oldest, settles in these rooms — the mean-distance arch was built for exactly that traffic, as Episode Six promised. The stochastic door: Brownian motion and the It\^o calculus (Durrett; Øksendal) — measure theory on path space, where the typewriter’s lessons about modes of convergence become daily bread. Each door is described, none opened: a possible Season Three, as the plan has said from Session Zero, and not scope creep for this one.

Reading for this chapter. Williams chs. 1–9 (the finale’s spiritual companion); Durrett §1–2; Axler 9A–9B; Folland §3.2.

The course closes. Sixteen chapters, two volumes, seventeen rings, one loan gracefully carried. The register balances; the desk is yours, sir.
