Chapter 1How Long Is a Set?

Companion to Episode One of the second season. The episode wrote the contract any honest length must keep, watched the hinge question — additivity across how many pieces? — select the countable clause as the season’s engine, saw Jordan’s finite machinery declare the rationals unmeasurable absurdly, built Lebesgue’s outer measure and rang the bell in the new theatre, measured the rationals at zero and made “measure zero” lawful, stated the catch, executed the honest retreat to sigma-algebras and the Borel estate, forged the oscillation gauge and the continuity-set anatomy, heard the rationals refuse to be a G-delta at Baire’s second and final discharge, and paid debt nine with ceremony: no function on the line is continuous exactly at the rationals. This chapter is the paperwork. Register notes: the bell rings once this chapter — the fifteenth ring, the first of the volume, marked $(\circledast)$ at Definition 1.7; the count carries over from Volume One’s fourteen. Debt nine is paid at Theorem 1.29; debt eleven pays its first instalment at Definition 1.13; the catch is stated on credit at Theorem 1.15, trial scheduled for Chapter 3 (Vitali presiding); the epsilon-over-two-to-the-k gambit is christened at Theorem 1.11.

Episode Digest

The argument of the hour. Length, made honest, selects its own jurisdiction. The contract: nonnegativity, nought for the empty set, intervals at $b-a$, translation invariance, and additivity across countably many pieces — finite additivity goes mute before limits, uncountable additivity contradicts the schoolroom, and the countable clause is exactly the engine analysis needs. Jordan’s finite covers fail the rationals absurdly (inner content zero, outer one). Lebesgue’s countable covers succeed: the outer measure — infimum over countable interval covers, completeness delivering the number (ring fifteen) — assigns every set a value, honours the contract’s first four clauses, and measures every countable set at zero: “measure zero” becomes lawful, the prophecy’s language redeemed. But countable additivity cannot hold on all sets (the catch, on credit until Chapter 3); the honest retreat defines sigma-algebras, and the intersection-from-above construction yields the Borel estate with its first ladder rungs, F-sigma and G-delta. The oscillation gauge proves every continuity set is a G-delta; Baire’s theorem proves the rationals are not; and the two instruments cross in a two-line ceremony: no function is continuous exactly on the rationals. The census closes exactly — continuity sets are precisely the G-delta sets (converse starred) — and the season’s method stands demonstrated: functions ask, sets answer.

The episode, beat by beat. 

A New Sign Above the Door. The establishment reopens; terms renewed (ear/desk, honesty clause, tiers); Season One’s debts carried at face value; the bell’s count stands at fourteen as the season opens. Credit line read into the record: Borel (1898) proposed countable operations and measured his estate; Lebesgue’s thesis (1902) finishes the house; master and student, credit divided exactly.

The Length of the Obvious. The contract’s four clauses (Definition 1.1): nought for the empty set; intervals at $b-a$; translation invariance; additivity of disjoint pieces — with the hinge question attached: how many pieces?

How Many Pieces? Uncountable additivity dies in a sentence (the unit interval is uncountably many weightless points; Remark 1.2). Finite additivity is safe and useless: limits are countable processes, and length must pass through them. The engine turned over once: countable additivity yields continuity along swelling chains (Proposition 1.3, by disjointification and the telescope), with the shrinking-chain caveat exhibited (half-lines $[n,\infty)$: infinite measures, empty intersection). The converse — continuity recovers countable additivity — is Problem II.2, solved in full.

Jordan’s Honest Attempt. Inner and outer content by finite packings and covers (Definition 1.4); the kinship made exact: contents are the Darboux ledgers of the indicator (Proposition 1.5) — the rationals’ Jordan failure and the static’s Riemann defeat are one failure. The verdict: inner content of $\Q \cap [0,1]$ is zero (no interval fits), outer is one (finite covers close up to endpoints; Proposition 1.6); unmeasurable, absurdly. Diagnosis in one word: finiteness.

The Civil Servant’s Idea. Why 1902 and not 1802: the permission to complete an infinite act — Cantor’s jurisdiction, Episode Three of the first season — had to exist before the schoolroom tools could be used countably at once. The definition (Definition 1.7, $(\circledast)$ ring fifteen): infimum over countable interval covers of the total length. Properties with their strategies: intervals measure $b-a$ (Theorem 1.10 — compactness converts the countable conspiracy to a finite committee; Volume One’s coronation cashed); monotonicity (Proposition 1.9); countable subadditivity by the epsilon-over-two-to-the-k gambit (Theorem 1.11, the season’s technique-character christened); translation invariance (Problem II.3, solved in full); the extended nonnegative arithmetic with its one prohibition (Remark 1.8). Scope: every set receives a number.

The Rationals Measured. Every countable set is null (Theorem 1.12: enumerate, house the $k$-th point under $\varepsilon/2^k$, geometric series pays). “Measure zero” defined (Definition 1.13) — debt eleven’s first instalment; the prophecy’s exhibits re-read. The null family is a sigma-ideal: hereditary, closed under countable unions (Proposition 1.14). The dust’s number is withheld — next chapter’s set piece.

The Scorecard. Four clauses banked (nonnegativity, nought, intervals, translation); the engine clause half-paid: subadditivity holds, additivity awaits a jurisdiction — next chapter’s criterion.

The Catch. Stated on credit (Theorem 1.15): no assignment on all subsets keeps the full contract; trial at Chapter 3, Vitali presiding, Choice in the dock.

The Honest Retreat. Sigma-algebras (Definition 1.16): complement and countable union — the traffic limits require; countable intersections free by De Morgan. Specimens: the tribe of two, the all-subsets tribe (barred by the catch), the countable-or-co-countable tribe (Problem II.1). The strategy: restrict the universal instrument to a tribe on which the contract can be proved.

The Borel Estate. The intersection-from-above construction (Theorem 1.18): the smallest sigma-algebra containing a given collection exists — unanimity across all containing tribes. The Borel sets (Definition 1.19); robustness: intervals, open sets, rational-endpoint intervals all generate the same estate (Theorem 1.20, via Volume One’s structure theorem, Proposition 5.3 — every open set a countable string of beads). The ladder’s first rungs: F-sigma and G-delta (Definition 1.21); the rationals are F-sigma, the irrationals G-delta; open sets are F-sigma too (Proposition 1.22, the fattened-endpoints trick).

Where a Function Is Continuous. The oscillation gauge (Definition 1.23): infimum over windows of the swing; continuity at a point is oscillation zero (Lemma 1.24); calibrations — the static at one everywhere, Thomae reading each fraction’s loudness, the step reading its jump, the cornered ramp reading zero (Remark 1.25). The $n$-quiet sets are open (Lemma 1.26, quietness inherited within the witness window); the continuity set is the countable intersection of the $n$-quiet sets: a G-delta, for every function whatsoever (Theorem 1.27).

The Rationals Refuse. $\Q$ is not a G-delta (Theorem 1.28): were $\Q = \bigcap G_n$, the $G_n$ and the punctured lines $\R \setminus \{q\}$ would form a countable family of open dense sets with empty intersection, contradicting Baire (Volume One, Theorem 5.28) — his second and final engagement, discharged.

The Ceremony. Debt nine paid (Theorem 1.29, boxed): continuity sets are G-delta; the rationals are not; no function is continuous exactly on the rationals — two lines after nine episodes. The census closes exactly: every G-delta is a continuity set (Problem III.1, starred) — the taxonomy was the theorem. The season’s method demonstrated: functions ask, sets answer.

The Season Before Us. The itinerary in eight sittings: the criterion and the dust’s number; Vitali and Banach–Tarski; integration rebuilt and the static tamed; the convergence theorems (the crown jewels); Littlewood’s principles; Fubini; Kolmogorov at the listener’s own door.

The toolkit acquired. The contract and its hinge; the epsilon-over-two-to-the-k gambit; the fattening trick (open covers at negligible cost); the intersection-from-above construction; the ladder’s first rungs; the oscillation gauge and quietness-inheritance; the punctured-lines assembly for Baire; and the two-instrument ceremony pattern — anatomy plus refusal.

Dates worth keeping. Jordan’s content in its mature form, 1893; Borel’s countably additive measure on his estate, 1898; Lebesgue’s thesis — Intégrale, longueur, aire — 1902; Vitali’s catch, 1905 (trial: Chapter 3); Baire’s theorem, 1899 (Volume One’s eleventh ring, spent here for the second and last time).

1.2 The Contract

Definition 1.1 (The contract for a length).

A candidate notion of length is an assignment $\mu$ of a value in $[0, \infty]$ to sets of reals (all sets, or a declared tribe of them), demanded to satisfy:

  1. (i)

    $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$;

  2. (ii)

    $\mu(I) = b - a$ for every interval $I$ with endpoints $a \le b$, open, closed, or half-open;

  3. (iii)

    (translation invariance) $\mu(A + t) = \mu(A)$ for every $t \in \R$, where $A + t = \{ a + t : a \in A \}$;

  4. (iv)

    (countable additivity) for every sequence $A_1, A_2, \dots$ of pairwise disjoint sets in the domain whose union is in the domain, $\mu\bigl( \bigcup_k A_k \bigr) = \sum_k \mu(A_k)$.

Finite additivity is the weakening of (iv) to finitely many pieces; it follows from (iv) by padding with empty sets, clause (i) absorbing the padding.

Remark 1.2 (Additivity across arbitrary families is not on the table).

$[0,1]$ is the disjoint union of its singletons. Each singleton has length zero under any candidate honouring (ii) and monotone common sense: $\{x\} \subseteq (x - \varepsilon, x + \varepsilon)$ for every $\varepsilon$. An additivity clause surviving uncountable disassembly would price $[0,1]$ as a “sum” of uncountably many zeros; no convention for such sums yields one (any reasonable definition — say, the supremum of finite subsums — yields zero). Clause (iv) stops at countable by necessity, not modesty.

Proposition 1.3 (Continuity of measure — the engine’s crankshaft).

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a sigma-algebra and $\mu$ a countably additive assignment on $\mathcal{A}$ with values in $[0,\infty]$ and $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$. (This proposition is stated for the tribes to come; from Chapter 2 onward it is used constantly.)

  1. (i)

    (Swelling chains) If $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots$, all in $\mathcal{A}$, then $\mu\bigl( \bigcup_n A_n \bigr) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$.

  2. (ii)

    (Shrinking chains) If $A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots$, all in $\mathcal{A}$, and $\mu(A_1) < \infty$, then $\mu\bigl( \bigcap_n A_n \bigr) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$. The finiteness hypothesis is not removable: $A_n = [n, \infty)$ shrinks to the empty set with every $\mu(A_n)$ infinite (once Chapter 2 delivers the measure, this is a lawful exhibit; the Archimedean property of Volume One, Theorem 2.12, empties the intersection).

Proof.

(i) Disjointify into rings of growth: $B_1 = A_1$ and $B_k = A_k \setminus A_{k-1}$ for $k \ge 2$. Each $B_k$ lies in $\mathcal{A}$ (complement and intersection, via De Morgan); the $B_k$ are pairwise disjoint; $\bigcup_{k \le n} B_k = A_n$; and $\bigcup_k B_k = \bigcup_n A_n$. Countable additivity prices the union as $\sum_k \mu(B_k)$, whose partial sums are, by finite additivity, $\mu(A_n)$ — the telescope, Volume One’s oldest cancellation. A series of nonnegative terms is the limit of its partial sums: $\mu(\bigcup A_n) = \lim \mu(A_n)$.

(ii) Pass to complements within $A_1$: the sets $A_1 \setminus A_n$ swell to $A_1 \setminus \bigcap_n A_n$. Apply (i), then subtract from $\mu(A_1)$ — lawful precisely because $\mu(A_1) < \infty$ keeps every quantity finite and the prohibited $\infty - \infty$ off the page (finite additivity gives $\mu(A_1) = \mu(A_n) + \mu(A_1 \setminus A_n)$, so $\mu(A_1 \setminus A_n) = \mu(A_1) - \mu(A_n)$, and likewise for the intersection). The identity rearranges to the claim. The converse direction — (i) plus finite additivity recovers countable additivity — is Problem II.2, solved in full: the engine and the clause are one mechanism.

□

1.3 Jordan’s Content

Definition 1.4 (Inner and outer content).

For bounded $A \subseteq \R$: the inner content $\underline{c}(A)$ is the supremum of $\sum_{j=1}^{N} \lvert I_j \rvert$ over all finite families of pairwise disjoint intervals with $\bigcup_j I_j \subseteq A$; the outer content $\overline{c}(A)$ is the infimum of $\sum_{j=1}^{N} \lvert I_j \rvert$ over all finite families with $A \subseteq \bigcup_j I_j$. Always $\underline{c}(A) \le \overline{c}(A)$; when equal, $A$ is Jordan measurable with content the common value.

Proposition 1.5 (The kinship: contents are Darboux ledgers).

Let $A \subseteq [a,b]$ and let $\mathbf{1}_A$ be its indicator. Then

\[ \underline{c}(A) = \underline{\int_a^b} \mathbf{1}_A \qquad\text{and}\qquad \overline{c}(A) = \overline{\int_a^b} \mathbf{1}_A . \]

In particular $A$ is Jordan measurable exactly when $\mathbf{1}_A$ is Riemann integrable, with content equal to integral. The rationals’ failure below and the static’s defeat (Volume One, Proposition 6.25 and Chapter 8) are one failure.

Proof.

Lower ledgers. A partition $P$ of $[a,b]$ has $L(\mathbf{1}_A, P) = \sum' \Delta x_i$, the primed sum over slices lying entirely inside $A$ (the floor is one exactly there) — a finite disjoint family of intervals inside $A$, so $L(\mathbf{1}_A,P) \le \underline{c}(A)$. Conversely a finite disjoint packing of $A$ by intervals of total length $\ell$ yields, after listing all endpoints as a partition and shaving $\varepsilon$ at the joins (closed slices must sit inside $A$; open packing intervals lose two points’ worth, which cost nothing after shaving), a lower sum exceeding $\ell - \varepsilon$. Suprema agree. The upper ledgers mirror: slices meeting $A$ versus finite covers, with the same endpoint bookkeeping. The desk leaves the mirrored half as drill within Problem I.4’s ambit.

□
Proposition 1.6 (Jordan’s verdict on the rationals).

For $A = \Q \cap [0,1]$: $\underline{c}(A) = 0$ and $\overline{c}(A) = 1$. The set is not Jordan measurable.

Proof.

Inner: an interval of positive length inside $A$ would consist of rationals only, impossible — the irrationals are dense (Volume One, Corollary 2.16). Only degenerate packings exist; the supremum is zero.

Outer: let $I_1, \dots, I_N$ be a finite cover of $A$ and let $F = \overline{I_1} \cup \cdots \cup \overline{I_N}$, the union of closures — a closed set of total length $\sum_j \lvert I_j \rvert$ at most (closing an interval adds endpoints, which carry no length). $F$ contains $A$, and $F$ is closed, so $F$ contains $\overline{A} = [0,1]$: the rationals are dense in the unit interval (Volume One, Theorem 2.15), and a closed superset of a set contains its closure. A finite union of intervals containing $[0,1]$ has total length at least $1$ (order the pieces meeting $[0,1]$ and walk from $0$ to $1$: each step is paid for by disjoint stretches of the pieces; the desk notes this is Jordan’s own counting, or Volume One’s Heine–Borel walk in miniature). Hence every finite cover totals at least $1$; the interval $[0,1]$ itself achieves $1$; the infimum is $1$.

□

1.4 The Outer Measure

Definition 1.7 ($(\circledast)$ Lebesgue outer measure — ring fifteen).

For any $A \subseteq \R$,

\[ m^{*}(A) \ =\ \inf \Bigl\{ \, \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \lvert I_k \rvert \ : \ A \subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} I_k, \ I_k \text{ intervals} \Bigr\}, \]

the infimum over all countable interval covers of the total length (a series of nonnegative terms: convergent or honestly infinite). Covers exist for every $A$ ($\R$ itself is a countable union of intervals), so the set of totals is a nonempty subset of $[0, \infty]$; if any total is finite, the set of finite totals is nonempty and bounded below by $0$, and the bell rings — the fifteenth, the first of the volume: the completeness axiom (Volume One, Axiom 2.6), and nothing else, delivers the greatest lower bound. (If every total is infinite, $m^{*}(A) = \infty$, an honest verdict.) The remaining suprema and infima of this chapter are clerical work upon this one and carry the standing annotation: completeness — small change, uncounted.

Remark 1.8 (Extended arithmetic; open covers at negligible cost).

Values live in $[0,\infty]$ with $a + \infty = \infty$; $\infty - \infty$ is never written. Two service facts, used silently hereafter. Fattening: any cover $\{I_k\}$ may be upgraded to open intervals $J_k \supseteq I_k$ with $\lvert J_k \rvert \le \lvert I_k \rvert + \varepsilon / 2^{k}$ — total cost at most $\varepsilon$, the geometric series footing the bill — so the infimum may be computed over open covers alone. Degenerate intervals (points) are permitted in covers and change nothing.

Proposition 1.9 (Monotonicity).

$A \subseteq B$ implies $m^{*}(A) \le m^{*}(B)$.

Proof.

Every cover of $B$ covers $A$, so the infimum for $A$ runs over a larger family of candidate totals and cannot exceed the infimum for $B$. (With clause (i) of the contract: $m^{*}(\emptyset) = 0$, the empty set hiding under covers of arbitrarily small total.)

□
Theorem 1.10 (The interval clause is kept).

For every interval $I$ with endpoints $a \le b$: $m^{*}(I) = b - a$.

Proof.

($\le$) $I \subseteq (a - \varepsilon, b + \varepsilon)$, a one-interval cover of total $b - a + 2\varepsilon$, for every $\varepsilon > 0$; the infimum dips below each, hence to $b - a$ at most.

($\ge$), the deep direction; it suffices to treat $I = [a,b]$ closed (any interval contains closed subintervals of length as near its own as desired, and monotonicity transfers the bound). Let $\{I_k\}$ be any countable cover of $[a,b]$; fatten to open $J_k$ at cost $\varepsilon$ (Remark 1.8). The open $J_k$ cover the compact $[a,b]$; Heine–Borel (Volume One, Theorem 5.18) extracts a finite committee $J_{k_1}, \dots, J_{k_N}$ still covering. Audit the committee by the walk: some member $(\alpha_1, \beta_1)$ contains $a$; if $\beta_1 > b$ stop; otherwise $\beta_1 \in [a,b]$ is covered by some member $(\alpha_2, \beta_2)$ with $\alpha_2 < \beta_1 < \beta_2$; continue. The walk visits distinct members with strictly increasing right endpoints, so it stops within $N$ steps, at some $\beta_M > b$. Then

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{M} \lvert J_{(i)} \rvert \ \ge\ (\beta_1 - \alpha_1) + (\beta_2 - \beta_1) + \cdots + (\beta_M - \beta_{M-1}) \ =\ \beta_M - \alpha_1 \ >\ b - a, \]

each step’s length at least the fresh ground gained (since $\alpha_{i+1} < \beta_i$). Hence $\sum_k \lvert I_k \rvert \ge \sum_k \lvert J_k \rvert - \varepsilon \ge b - a - \varepsilon$, for every cover and every $\varepsilon$; the infimum is at least $b - a$. — The clause cost compactness, exactly as narrated: the countable conspiracy collapses to an auditable committee, and Jordan’s own counting convicts the committee.

□
Theorem 1.11 (Countable subadditivity — the gambit’s debut).

For any sets $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots$

\[ m^{*} \Bigl( \bigcup_{k} A_k \Bigr) \ \le\ \sum_{k} m^{*}(A_k). \]
Proof.

If any $m^{*}(A_k) = \infty$ the claim is vacuous. Otherwise let $\varepsilon > 0$. For each $k$, the infimum’s approximation property (Volume One’s oldest reflex) supplies a cover $\{I_{k,j}\}_j$ of $A_k$ with $\sum_j \lvert I_{k,j} \rvert \le m^{*}(A_k) + \varepsilon/2^{k}$. Pool the covers: $\{I_{k,j}\}_{k,j}$ is a countable family (a countable union of countable families — Volume One, Chapter 3’s census) covering $\bigcup_k A_k$, of total

\[ \sum_{k} \sum_{j} \lvert I_{k,j} \rvert \ \le\ \sum_k \Bigl( m^{*}(A_k) + \frac{\varepsilon}{2^k} \Bigr) \ =\ \sum_k m^{*}(A_k) + \varepsilon, \]

the geometric series picking up the bill. One cover within $\varepsilon$ of the target, for every $\varepsilon$: the infimum obeys. The epsilon-over-two-to-the-k gambit, christened — a fixed tolerance spent across countably many claimants; it recurs until the finale.

□
Theorem 1.12 (Every countable set is null).

If $A \subseteq \R$ is countable, then $m^{*}(A) = 0$.

Proof.

Enumerate $A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots\}$ (finite sets: pad the argument or stop early). Given $\varepsilon > 0$, house $a_k$ in an interval of length $\varepsilon / 2^{k}$ centred there. The family covers $A$ with total $\varepsilon$ exactly. So $m^{*}(A) \le \varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$, and $m^{*}(A) \ge 0$ always: $m^{*}(A) = 0$. In particular $m^{*}(\Q \cap [0,1]) = 0$ and $m^{*}(\Q) = 0$: the set that defeated Jordan is dispatched, and the entire difference is the word countable.

□
Definition 1.13 (Measure zero).

$A \subseteq \R$ has measure zero (is null) when $m^{*}(A) = 0$: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there are countably many intervals covering $A$ of total length below $\varepsilon$. This is the phrase Volume One’s finale quoted as prophecy (Volume One, Chapter 8, the Lebesgue criterion, still on credit — proof at Chapter 4 of this volume); debt eleven pays its first instalment here.

Proposition 1.14 (The null family is a sigma-ideal).

(i) If $B \subseteq A$ and $A$ is null, $B$ is null. (ii) If each of $A_1, A_2, \dots$ is null, so is $\bigcup_k A_k$.

Proof.

(i) is monotonicity (Proposition 1.9). (ii) is countable subadditivity (Theorem 1.11): $m^{*}(\bigcup A_k) \le \sum_k 0 = 0$. Hereditary downward, closed under countable assembly: negligibility with working papers. When “almost everywhere” arrives in force, these two laws discharge exceptional sets by the countable armful.

□
Theorem 1.15 (The catch — stated on credit).

There is no assignment $\mu$ defined on all subsets of $\R$ satisfying the full contract of Definition 1.1 — nonnegativity with $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$, intervals to length, translation invariance, and countable additivity. In particular $m^{*}$, which keeps the first three clauses on all sets, is not countably additive on all sets.

On credit. The proof is the Vitali construction, Chapter 3’s centrepiece, with the Axiom of Choice named in the indictment; nothing in this volume uses the catch before its trial. Until then it stands as the season’s motivation: some sets must be turned away, and the honest question is which stay.

1.5 Sigma-Algebras and the Borel Estate

Definition 1.16 (Algebra; sigma-algebra).

A collection $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $\R$ (or of any ambient set) is a sigma-algebra when: (i) $\emptyset \in \mathcal{A}$; (ii) $A \in \mathcal{A}$ implies $A^{c} \in \mathcal{A}$; (iii) $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$ implies $\bigcup_k A_k \in \mathcal{A}$. Weakening (iii) to finite unions defines an algebra. Consequences, by De Morgan (Volume One, Chapter 1’s toolkit): the ambient set belongs ($\emptyset^{c}$); countable intersections belong ($\bigcap A_k = (\bigcup A_k^{c})^{c}$); differences belong. The walls are drawn exactly where the traffic runs: complements and countable assemblies are what limiting arguments perform.

Remark 1.17 (Three specimens).

The tribe of two, $\{\emptyset, \R\}$: closed under everything, useless for everything. The all-subsets tribe $\mathcal{P}(\R)$: maximal hospitality, and the catch (Theorem 1.15) bars it from carrying the contract. The countable-or-co-countable tribe $\{A : A \text{ countable or } A^{c} \text{ countable}\}$: a genuine sigma-algebra containing every singleton yet not $[0, \tfrac12]$ — dissected in Problem II.1. Membership is a distinction.

Theorem 1.18 (Generated sigma-algebras exist — from above).

Let $\mathcal{G}$ be any collection of subsets. Among all sigma-algebras containing $\mathcal{G}$ there is a smallest, written $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$: it is contained in every sigma-algebra containing $\mathcal{G}$.

Proof.

The family of sigma-algebras containing $\mathcal{G}$ is nonempty: $\mathcal{P}(\R)$ qualifies. Let $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$ be their intersection — the sets belonging to every sigma-algebra containing $\mathcal{G}$, the unanimous sets. The three closure checks, each one line: $\emptyset$ belongs to every chamber, hence unanimously. If $A$ is unanimous then, chamber by chamber, $A^{c}$ belongs (each chamber is a sigma-algebra), hence $A^{c}$ is unanimous. If $A_1, A_2, \dots$ are unanimous then each chamber contains them all and therefore their union: the union is unanimous. So $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$ is a sigma-algebra; it contains $\mathcal{G}$ (every chamber does); and it is contained in every containing sigma-algebra, being their intersection — unanimity cannot outvote a chamber. Floor-by-floor construction from below is not available at any countable stage (the Notes gesture at the transfinite abyss); the intersection from above is the whole trick.

□
Definition 1.19 (The Borel estate).

$\mathcal{B} = \sigma(\{\text{open subsets of } \R\})$: the Borel sets. Every set this course has ever named in anger — intervals, opens, closeds, $\Q$, the irrationals, the dust, every continuity set — is Borel; producing a non-Borel set requires the ghost-machinery of Chapter 3 or deliberate transfinite bureaucracy (Notes).

Theorem 1.20 (The estate does not depend on the founder’s taste).

$\sigma(\text{open sets}) = \sigma(\text{all intervals}) = \sigma(\text{open intervals}) = \sigma(\text{open intervals with rational endpoints})$.

Proof.

Write $\mathcal{B}$, $\mathcal{I}$, $\mathcal{I}_{o}$, $\mathcal{I}_{\Q}$ for the four generated tribes; we chain inclusions both ways, using twice the master principle: if every member of $\mathcal{G}_1$ lies in $\sigma(\mathcal{G}_2)$, then $\sigma(\mathcal{G}_1) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{G}_2)$ — $\sigma(\mathcal{G}_2)$ is a sigma-algebra containing $\mathcal{G}_1$, and the smallest such is contained in it.

$\mathcal{I}_{\Q} \subseteq \mathcal{I}_{o} \subseteq \mathcal{I}$: generators nest. $\mathcal{I} \subseteq \mathcal{B}$: every interval is Borel — open intervals are open; $[a,b] = \bigcap_n (a - \tfrac1n,\, b + \tfrac1n)$, a countable intersection of opens; half-open likewise, one side at a time. $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{I}_{o}$: every open set is a countable disjoint union of open beads — Volume One’s structure theorem (Proposition 5.3) — hence belongs to $\sigma(\text{open intervals})$, care of closure clause (iii); unbounded beads are countable unions of bounded ones. $\mathcal{I}_{o} \subseteq \mathcal{I}_{\Q}$: every open interval is a countable union of rational-endpoint ones — pick rational endpoints marching out to the true ones, density (Volume One, Theorem 2.15) supplying the recruits. The chain closes: all four tribes are one estate. The rational-endpoint generating set is countable — a fact of consequence for the season’s bookkeeping and for Chapter 8’s dictionary.

□
Definition 1.21 (The ladder’s first rungs).

A set is $F_{\sigma}$ when it is a countable union of closed sets; $G_{\delta}$ when a countable intersection of open sets. (F for fermé, sigma for sum; G for Gebiet, delta for Durchschnitt: the subject’s bilingual fossils.) Complementation exchanges the two rungs, by De Morgan.

Proposition 1.22 (Rung calibrations).

(i) $\Q$ is $F_{\sigma}$; the irrationals are $G_{\delta}$. (ii) Every closed set is $F_{\sigma}$ and every open set $G_{\delta}$, trivially; moreover every open set is $F_{\sigma}$ and every closed set $G_{\delta}$.

Proof.

(i) Singletons are closed; $\Q = \bigcup_{q \in \Q} \{q\}$ is a countable union of them. Complement and De Morgan deliver the irrationals as $G_{\delta}$. (ii) For open $U$: write $U$ as a countable union of open beads (Volume One, Proposition 5.3) and each bounded bead $(a,b)$ as $\bigcup_n [a + \tfrac1n,\, b - \tfrac1n]$ (fattened endpoints, run inward; unbounded beads as $\bigcup_n [a + \tfrac1n,\, n]$ and kin) — a countable union of closed sets. A countable union of countable unions is countable (Volume One, Chapter 3). Closed sets as $G_{\delta}$: complements.

□

1.6 The Continuity-Set Anatomy

Definition 1.23 (Oscillation).

For $f : \R \to \R$ (or on an interval), the swing on a set $W$ where $f$ is bounded is $\omega_f(W) = \sup_W f - \inf_W f$ (unbounded: $\omega_f(W) = \infty$). The oscillation at a point is

\[ \omega_f(x) \ =\ \inf_{\delta > 0} \, \omega_f\bigl( (x - \delta,\, x + \delta) \bigr), \]

the swings settling downward as windows shrink (monotone in $\delta$; the infimum is small change, uncounted).

Lemma 1.24 (Continuity is oscillation zero).

$f$ is continuous at $x$ if and only if $\omega_f(x) = 0$.

Proof.

($\Rightarrow$) Given $\varepsilon > 0$, continuity yields $\delta$ with $\lvert f(y) - f(x) \rvert < \varepsilon/2$ on the window, so the swing there is at most $\varepsilon$; the infimum dips below every $\varepsilon$. ($\Leftarrow$) If $\omega_f(x) = 0$: given $\varepsilon$, some window swings below $\varepsilon$; on it, $\lvert f(y) - f(x) \rvert \le \omega_f(W) < \varepsilon$ — both values live in the window’s range. That is continuity’s definition (Volume One, Chapter 6).

□
Remark 1.25 (Calibrations).

The static (Volume One, Definition 6.24): every window contains rationals and irrationals (Volume One, 2.15–2.16), so every swing is $1$: $\omega_D \equiv 1$. Thomae (Volume One, Definition 6.26): at irrational $x$, oscillation zero (continuity, Volume One, Theorem 6.27); at rational $p/q$ in lowest terms, oscillation exactly $1/q$ — the value there is $1/q$, while nearby values are small (the counting lemma inside Volume One’s Theorem 6.27 forces large denominators nearby) and irrationals nearby read zero, so the swing settles to the fraction’s own loudness. The step function: oscillation equal to the jump’s height at the jump, zero elsewhere. The cornered ramp (Volume One, Chapter 8): oscillation zero even at the corner — the gauge detects broken continuity, not broken smoothness.

Lemma 1.26 (The quiet sets are open).

For every $c > 0$, the set $V_c = \{ x : \omega_f(x) < c \}$ is open.

Proof.

Let $x \in V_c$. The infimum’s approximation property produces a witness window $W = (x - \delta, x + \delta)$ with $\omega_f(W) < c$. Every resident $y \in W$ owns a sub-window $W' \subseteq W$ centred at $y$ (openness of the interval), and swings only shrink on subsets: $\omega_f(W') \le \omega_f(W) < c$, so $\omega_f(y) < c$. Thus $W \subseteq V_c$: quietness certified by one window is inherited by everyone living in it, and $V_c$ contains a neighbourhood of each of its points — open, by the very definition (Volume One, Chapter 5).

□
Theorem 1.27 (Every continuity set is a $G_{\delta}$).

For every $f$, the set $C_f$ of points of continuity satisfies

\[ C_f \ =\ \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \, \Bigl\{ x : \omega_f(x) < \tfrac{1}{n} \Bigr\}, \]

a countable intersection of open sets.

Proof.

By Lemma 1.24, $x \in C_f$ iff $\omega_f(x) = 0$ iff $\omega_f(x) < 1/n$ for every $n$ — a nonnegative quantity below every threshold is zero, the Archimedean gate (Volume One, Theorem 2.12). Each threshold set is open by Lemma 1.26. No hypothesis on $f$ was used: the theorem binds every function whatsoever.

□

1.7 The Refusal and the Ceremony

Theorem 1.28 (The rationals are not a $G_{\delta}$).

There do not exist open sets $G_1, G_2, \dots$ with $\Q = \bigcap_n G_n$.

Proof.

Suppose otherwise. Each $G_n \supseteq \Q$ is open, and dense — it houses the dense tenant $\Q$ (Volume One, Theorem 2.15). For each rational $q$, the punctured line $\R \setminus \{q\}$ is open (a point is closed) and dense (no interval fits in a point). The family $\{G_n\}_n \cup \{\R \setminus \{q\}\}_{q \in \Q}$ is a countable family of open dense sets (countable plus countable, Volume One, Chapter 3). Its intersection is empty: a surviving point would be rational (unanimity of the $G_n$) yet avoid every rational (the punctures). But Baire’s theorem (Volume One, Theorem 5.28, with its dense-intersection reading) makes any countable intersection of open dense subsets of $\R$ dense, hence nonempty. Contradiction; the supposition dies. Monsieur Baire’s second and final engagement, discharged.

□
Theorem 1.29 (The ceremony: debt nine paid).

There is no function $f : \R \to \R$ whose set of continuity points is exactly $\Q$.

Proof.

$C_f$ is a $G_{\delta}$ (Theorem 1.27); $\Q$ is not (Theorem 1.28). Two lines, nine episodes in the arranging.

□
Remark 1.30 (The census closes exactly).

Thomae’s function realizes the irrationals as a continuity set (lawfully: they are $G_{\delta}$); the rationals are forbidden; and the characterization is complete in both directions: every $G_{\delta}$ subset of $\R$ is the continuity set of some function — Problem III.1, starred, the two-sign construction staged in hints. The continuity sets are precisely the $G_{\delta}$ sets; nothing else about a candidate set — cardinality, density, order — is relevant. Volume One’s Episode Six inventory (everything; nothing; exactly the irrationals; never exactly the rationals) stands fully explained.

1.9 Hints

II.1.  For the union clause: if every $A_k$ is countable, the union is countable (Volume One, Chapter 3); if some $A_{k_0}$ is co-countable, the union’s complement sits inside $A_{k_0}^{c}$. For $\sigma(\{\text{singletons}\})$: show the tribe is a sigma-algebra containing the singletons, and that any such sigma-algebra contains every countable set and every co-countable set.

II.4.  Suppose $D = \{d_1, d_2, \dots\}$ countable, dense, and $= \bigcap_n G_n$ with $G_n$ open (dense, since $D$ is). The punctured lines $\R \setminus \{d_k\}$ are open; dense because a puncture cannot contain an interval. Intersect everything and apply Baire. Density of $D$ entered twice: making the $G_n$ dense, and making the contradiction bite ($\Q$ qualifies; a countable closed set need not).

II.5.  The complement of $\{ \omega_f \ge c \}$ is $\bigcup_{c' < c} V_{c'}$ — or directly: $V_c$ open for every $c$ (Lemma 1.26) and $\{\omega_f \ge c\} = \R \setminus V_c$. For the $F_{\sigma}$ conclusion: the discontinuity set is $\bigcup_n \{ \omega_f \ge 1/n \}$.

II.6.  Direct exhibition: the irrationals are $\bigcap_{q \in \Q} (\R \setminus \{q\})$, a countable intersection of opens. The anatomy’s exhibition uses the quiet sets $V_{1/n}$ of Thomae’s oscillation — compute which points each $V_{1/n}$ admits (rationals with denominator above $n$, and all irrationals), and observe the two families differ set by set though their intersections agree.

III.1.  Continuity on $G$: for $x \in G$ and $N$ given, $W = \bigcap_{n \le N} U_n$ is an open neighbourhood of $x$; any $y \in W$ has either $f(y) = 0$ ($y \in G$) or $m(y) > N$, whence $\lvert f(y) \rvert < 1/N$. Discontinuity at $x \notin G$ with $m(x) = k$: every window at $x$ contains points of the opposite rationality class (Volume One, 2.15–2.16); such a point $y$ gives either $f(y) = 0$ (if $y \in G$: gap $1/k$) or $f(y)$ of opposite sign (gap exceeding $1/k$). Either way $\omega_f(x) \ge 1/k > 0$. Mind that $m$ is well-defined only off $G$, and that the decreasing arrangement of the $U_n$ is what makes $m(y) > N$ mean $y \in U_1, \dots, U_N$.

III.2.  If the irrationals were $F_{\sigma}$, the rationals would be $G_{\delta}$ (De Morgan): Theorem 1.28 forbids. For $A$: were $A$ $G_{\delta}$, then $A \cap (-\infty, 0) = \Q \cap (-\infty, 0)$ would be $G_{\delta}$ in the open half-line, and Baire localizes to any complete-metric-flavoured stage — run the punctured-lines argument inside $(-\infty, 0)$; the mirrored half kills $F_{\sigma}$. References: Oxtoby for the honest treatment of relative Baire arguments.

III.3.  Key identity: for bounded $A$, $\overline{c}(A) = \overline{c}(\overline{A})$ (closing a finite cover costs endpoints only) and $\underline{c}(A) = \underline{c}(A^{\circ})$ (an interval inside $A$ avoids the boundary after shaving). Then $\overline{c}(A) - \underline{c}(A) = \overline{c}(\partial A)$ via $\overline{A} = A^{\circ} \sqcup \partial A$ and finite additivity of content on such splittings.

1.10 Solutions

Tier I (terse).  I.1 House the $j$-th of the $N$ points in an interval of length $\varepsilon / N$: total $\varepsilon$, for every $\varepsilon$; the infimum is $0$. I.2 $[a,b] + t = [a+t, b+t]$, an interval of the same length; the cover $(a + t - \varepsilon, b + t + \varepsilon)$ witnesses $\le b - a + 2\varepsilon$; Theorem 1.10 forces $= b - a$ (or II.3 plus the theorem, as the drill anticipates). I.3 Monotonicity twice (Proposition 1.9); middle term $= \tfrac12$ by Theorem 1.10. I.4 Covers within $\varepsilon/2$ of each outer measure pool to a countable cover of the union (two countables); total below the sum plus $\varepsilon$. Mirrored ledgers: slices of a partition meeting $A$ form a finite cover of $A$; conversely a finite cover’s endpoints, listed as a partition with $\varepsilon$-shaving at the joins, give an upper sum within $\varepsilon$ of the cover’s total. I.5 Ambient set $= \emptyset^{c}$; intersections by $\bigcap A_k = \bigl( \bigcup A_k^{c} \bigr)^{c}$; difference $A \setminus B = A \cap B^{c}$. I.6 Evens: countable, in. Rationals: countable, in. $[0,1]$: uncountable with uncountable complement, out. Irrationals: complement countable, in. The transversal of the intervals $[n, n+1)$: countably infinite (one point per integer), in — the tribe cares only for cardinality, not location. I.7 $[a,b) = \bigcup_n [a,\, b - \tfrac{b-a}{n}]$ (closed) $= \bigcap_n (a - \tfrac1n,\, b)$ (open). I.8 Step: at the jump every window swings the jump’s height $h$, and constancy elsewhere swings nought — $\omega = h$ at the jump, $0$ off it. Parabola: continuous everywhere (Volume One, Chapter 6), so $\omega \equiv 0$ (Lemma 1.24). Static: every window contains both values $0$ and $1$ (density both ways): $\omega \equiv 1$.

II.2 (solution in full).  ($\Rightarrow$) is Proposition 1.3(i). ($\Leftarrow$) Let $E_1, E_2, \dots$ be pairwise disjoint members with union $E$. The partial unions $U_n = \bigcup_{k \le n} E_k$ form a swelling chain in the sigma-algebra with union $E$, so continuity gives $\mu(E) = \lim_n \mu(U_n)$. Finite additivity prices each stage: $\mu(U_n) = \sum_{k \le n} \mu(E_k)$, a partial sum. The limit of the partial sums is the series (nonnegative terms; Volume One, Chapter 4’s monotone license makes the limit exist in $[0,\infty]$): $\mu(E) = \sum_{k} \mu(E_k)$. Countable additivity holds; the engine and the clause are one mechanism seen from two sides, as the episode claimed.

II.3 (solution in full).  Fix $A$ and $t$. If $\{I_k\}$ covers $A$, then $\{I_k + t\}$ covers $A + t$ — $a \in A$ gives $a \in I_k$ for some $k$, so $a + t \in I_k + t$ — and translated intervals keep their lengths: $\lvert I_k + t \rvert = \lvert I_k \rvert$ (endpoints shift together). So every candidate total for $A$ is a candidate total for $A + t$: $m^{*}(A + t) \le m^{*}(A)$, the infimum running over a family at least as rich. Apply the same inequality to the set $A + t$ and the shift $-t$: $m^{*}(A) = m^{*}((A + t) + (-t)) \le m^{*}(A + t)$. The two inequalities close: $m^{*}(A + t) = m^{*}(A)$. Clause three of the contract is kept by the new instrument — the clause that will sign Chapter 3’s confession is, for now, in impeccable standing.
