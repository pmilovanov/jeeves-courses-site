4.2 Measurable Functions

Definition 4.1 (Measurable function).

Let $E \in \mathcal{M}$. A function $f : E \to [-\infty, \infty]$ is measurable when $\{ f > a \} = \{ x \in E : f(x) > a \}$ is a citizen for every $a \in \R$.

Proposition 4.2 (Equivalent thresholds; Borel preimages).

For $f : E \to [-\infty, \infty]$ the following are equivalent: $\{f > a\} \in \mathcal{M}$ for all $a$; $\{f \ge a\} \in \mathcal{M}$ for all $a$; $\{f < a\} \in \mathcal{M}$ for all $a$; $\{f \le a\} \in \mathcal{M}$ for all $a$. Any of them implies $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{M}$ for every Borel $B \subseteq \R$.

Proof.

$\{f \ge a\} = \bigcap_n \{f > a - \tfrac1n\}$ and $\{f > a\} = \bigcup_n \{f \ge a + \tfrac1n\}$: countable tribe operations convert the two strict/weak forms; complements within $E$ convert to the two below-forms. For Borel preimages: the collection $\{ B : f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{M} \}$ is a sigma-algebra (preimages respect complements and unions), and it contains the half-lines, hence the intervals, hence — the estate’s robustness, Chapter 1, Theorem 1.20 — all of $\mathcal{B}$.

□
Proposition 4.3 (The parade).

Continuous functions on citizens are measurable ($\{f > a\}$ is the trace of an open set); monotone functions (the pile above a waterline is an interval or half-line trace); step and simple functions; indicators of citizens; the static and Thomae’s function; and any function on a null ground. Ghost indicators ($\mathbf{1}_{V}$, $V$ Vitali’s delegation) are the canonical non-measurable specimens.

Proof.

Each is a one-line pile audit; the desk records the static’s: $\{D > a\}$ is $\emptyset$ ($a \ge 1$), $\Q \cap E$ ($0 \le a < 1$), or $E$ ($a < 0$) — citizens all. Problem I.1 drills the rest.

□
Proposition 4.4 (The algebra).

If $f, g$ are measurable (finite-valued where combined) and $c \in \R$: $cf$, $f + g$, $fg$, $\lvert f \rvert$, $\max(f,g)$, $\min(f,g)$ are measurable.

Proof.

The sum, the one with an idea: $f + g > a$ iff some rational $q$ has $f > q$ and $g > a - q$ (density splits the surplus), so $\{f + g > a\} = \bigcup_{q \in \Q} \bigl( \{f > q\} \cap \{g > a - q\} \bigr)$ — a countable union of citizens; the picket fence’s contract, executed at the function level. Products via $fg = \tfrac14 \bigl( (f+g)^2 - (f-g)^2 \bigr)$ once squares are settled ($\{f^2 > a\}$ unwinds to two piles); moduli and max/min are unions and intersections of piles. Problem I.1’s second half.

□
Proposition 4.5 (Composition: the safe direction).

If $f$ is measurable and $g : \R \to \R$ is Borel measurable (in particular continuous), then $g \circ f$ is measurable. The reverse composition — a measurable function of a continuous function — can fail: Theorem 4.22.

Proof.

$(g \circ f)^{-1}(a, \infty) = f^{-1}\bigl( g^{-1}(a,\infty) \bigr)$: $g^{-1}(a, \infty)$ is Borel, and Proposition 4.2 finishes.

□
Theorem 4.6 (Limit stability — the crown of the class).

Let $f_1, f_2, \dots$ be measurable on $E$. Then $\sup_n f_n$, $\inf_n f_n$, $\limsup_n f_n$, $\liminf_n f_n$ are measurable; and if $f_n \to f$ pointwise on $E$, then $f$ is measurable.

Proof.

$\{\sup_n f_n > a\} = \bigcup_n \{f_n > a\}$: some function clears the waterline — a countable union of citizens. Infima by $\inf f_n = -\sup(-f_n)$ (or the mirrored intersection). $\limsup_n f_n = \inf_{n} \sup_{k \ge n} f_k$: countable operations stacked twice, each stage measurable by the preceding clauses. Pointwise limits equal their limsup. No hypothesis beyond measurability was used; the sigma-algebra’s countable clause is what every line spent.

□

4.3 Atoms, Staircases, and the Integral

Definition 4.7 (Simple functions; the merchant’s multiplication).

$\varphi$ is simple when it takes finitely many values, each on a citizen: $\varphi = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ with $A_i \in \mathcal{M}$ disjoint. For simple $\varphi \ge 0$,

\[ \int \varphi \, dm \ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \, m(A_i) \qquad (0 \cdot \infty = 0). \]
Proposition 4.8 (Well-priced; simple linearity and monotonicity).

The price is independent of the representation; for simple $\varphi, \psi \ge 0$ and $c \ge 0$: $\int (\varphi + \psi) = \int \varphi + \int \psi$, $\int c\varphi = c \int \varphi$, and $\varphi \le \psi \Rightarrow \int \varphi \le \int \psi$.

Proof.

Two representations refine to a common one (intersect the storeys — Chapter 1’s finite tribe operations), on which both prices telescope to the same sum; additivity of $m$ on the disjoint refinement is the only fact used. Linearity and monotonicity read off the common refinement likewise. (Problem I.2 drills a two-representation specimen.)

□
Theorem 4.9 (The staircase of the range).

Let $f : E \to [0, \infty]$ be measurable. Define

\[ \varphi_n \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n 2^{n}} \frac{k-1}{2^{n}} \, \mathbf{1}_{A_{n,k}} \; + \; n \, \mathbf{1}_{\{f \ge n\}}, \qquad A_{n,k} = \Bigl\{ \tfrac{k-1}{2^n} \le f < \tfrac{k}{2^n} \Bigr\}. \]

Each $\varphi_n$ is simple; $\varphi_n \le \varphi_{n+1} \le f$; and $\varphi_n \to f$ pointwise on $E$ — uniformly on any set where $f$ is bounded.

Proof.

The bins $A_{n,k}$ are differences of piles: citizens (Proposition 4.2); finitely many storeys: simple. Monotonicity: passing $n \to n+1$ halves each denomination — a point binned at $\tfrac{k-1}{2^n}$ is re-binned at $\tfrac{2k-2}{2^{n+1}}$ or $\tfrac{2k-1}{2^{n+1}}$, never lower — and raises the ceiling. Convergence: if $f(x) < \infty$, then for $n > f(x)$ the assignment sits within $2^{-n}$ of $f(x)$; if $f(x) = \infty$, the assignments are $n \uparrow \infty$. Where $f \le K$: for $n > K$ the error is uniformly under $2^{-n}$.

□
Definition 4.10 ($(\circledast)$ The integral — ring sixteen).

For measurable $f : E \to [0, \infty]$,

\[ \int_{E} f \, dm \ =\ \sup \Bigl\{ \int \varphi \, dm \ : \ 0 \le \varphi \le f, \ \varphi \text{ simple} \Bigr\} . \]

The bell rings — the sixteenth: the supremum over the inventory of under-staircases exists in $[0, \infty]$ by the completeness axiom (finite case) and honest convention (infinite), the same authority that manufactured the Darboux ledgers at rings thirteen and fourteen. One ledger only, by design: measurability has done the vetting Riemann’s upper book performed by hand (the bounded estate’s Proposition 4.14 exhibits the second book collapsing onto the first where both exist), and Theorem 4.9 guarantees the supremum is climbed, not merely bounded. Nothing is refused; the value $\infty$ is an honest verdict.

Remark 4.11 (The handshake: the identity, re-integrated).

For $f(x) = x$ on $[0,1]$, the staircase $\varphi_n$ of Theorem 4.9 has bins $A_{n,k} = [\tfrac{k-1}{2^n}, \tfrac{k}{2^n})$ of measure $2^{-n}$, so

\[ \int \varphi_n \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}} \frac{k-1}{2^{n}} \cdot \frac{1}{2^{n}} \ =\ \frac{1}{4^{n}} \cdot \frac{2^{n}(2^{n}-1)}{2} \ =\ \frac12 - \frac{1}{2^{n+1}} \ \uparrow\ \frac12 , \]

and no under-staircase exceeds $\tfrac12$ (each is dominated by the Riemann upper ledger, or directly: Problem I.3). The new integral returns the schoolroom’s one half; the general agreement is Theorem 4.19.

Definition 4.12 (Integrable; the general integral).

For measurable $f$: $f^{\pm} = \max(\pm f, 0)$ are measurable (Proposition 4.4), $f = f^{+} - f^{-}$, $\lvert f \rvert = f^{+} + f^{-}$. $f$ is integrable when $\int \lvert f \rvert \, dm < \infty$ (equivalently both $\int f^{\pm} < \infty$), and then $\int f \, dm = \int f^{+} - \int f^{-}$ — lawful subtraction. If exactly one part is infinite the integral is $\pm\infty$; if both, undefined.

Proposition 4.13 (Manners within tonight’s reach).

(i) Monotonicity: $0 \le f \le g$ measurable $\Rightarrow \int f \le \int g$ (every under-staircase of $f$ under-staircases $g$). (ii) $\int c f = c \int f$ for $c \ge 0$ (staircases scale). (iii) Ground conventions: $\int_E f = \int f \mathbf{1}_E$, the two definitions agreeing storey by storey. (iv) $f = 0$ a.e. $\Rightarrow \int f = 0$ (every under-staircase’s storeys of positive value stand on null ground). (v) Bounded additivity: for bounded measurable $f, g$ on finite-measure ground, $\int (f + g) = \int f + \int g$ — via Proposition 4.14’s sandwiches (Problem II.2’s ambit).

Proposition 4.14 (The bounded estate: two ledgers meet).

Let $f$ be measurable and bounded on $E$ with $m(E) < \infty$. Then

\[ \sup_{\substack{\varphi \le f \\ \varphi \text{ simple}}} \int \varphi \ =\ \inf_{\substack{\psi \ge f \\ \psi \text{ simple}}} \int \psi . \]
Proof.

The staircase construction at resolution $n$ (ceiling above the bound) yields $\varphi_n \le f \le \varphi_n + 2^{-n}$ pointwise, so $\psi_n = \varphi_n + 2^{-n}$ over-staircases $f$ with $\int \psi_n - \int \varphi_n = 2^{-n} m(E) \to 0$; the sup and inf are pinched together. (Solved in full as Problem II.2, with the bounded-additivity dividend; the converse — ledger agreement forces measurability — is the desk’s note for the ambitious, references supplied.)

□
Remark 4.15 (The additivity credit — one week’s maturity).

For arbitrary measurable $f, g \ge 0$, $\int (f+g) = \int f + \int g$: the $\ge$ direction is free (under-staircases add), and the $\le$ direction is stated on credit tonight, discharged in Chapter 5 as the monotone convergence theorem’s first corollary (staircases $\varphi_n \uparrow f$, $\psi_n \uparrow g$ give $\varphi_n + \psi_n \uparrow f + g$; MCT passes the limit). No argument of this chapter uses the credit: the taming, the absorption, and the criterion live in the bounded estate, where Proposition 4.14 pays cash.

Proposition 4.16 (Almost everywhere, at work).

(i) If $f = g$ a.e. on $E$ and both are measurable, then $\int_E f = \int_E g$ (whenever either side is defined). (ii) If $f$ is measurable and $g = f$ a.e., then $g$ is measurable — completeness of $\mathcal{M}$ earning its keep.

Proof.

(ii) $\{g > a\}$ differs from the citizen $\{f > a\}$ within the null disagreement set; the amnesty (Chapter 2, completeness) makes the difference a citizen, and the tribe’s algebra reassembles. (i) For $f, g \ge 0$: any under-staircase of $f$ converts to one of $g$ by zeroing its storeys on the null disagreement — prices unchanged, $0 \cdot \infty = 0$ and Proposition 4.13(iv) covering the leftovers; the suprema agree. General case: positive and negative parts disagree only on the null set.

□
Theorem 4.17 (The taming of the static — debt four, act three).

$\displaystyle \int_{[0,1]} D \, dm = 0$.

Proof.

$D = 1 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\Q \cap [0,1]} + 0 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[0,1] \setminus \Q}$ is simple; $\int D = 1 \cdot m(\Q \cap [0,1]) = 1 \cdot 0 = 0$. One line, as promised in Volume One, Chapter 8. The three-act contract — born at Volume One’s Definition 6.24, victorious over Riemann in Volume One, Chapter 8, tamed here — is complete.

4.4 The Old Regime, Absorbed

Remark 4.18 (The boundary post: improper integrals).

The absorption below covers the proper Riemann integral. A conditionally convergent improper integral — finite by cancellation between infinite gains and infinite losses — has $\int \lvert f \rvert = \infty$ and is refused by Definition 4.12, on the same grounds Volume One’s rearrangement theorem convicted conditional series: totals that depend on the order of summation have no order-free value, and the sorted count is order-free by construction. Standard specimen: the sign-alternating bump train with masses $1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \dots$ — improperly convergent to the alternating harmonic total, Lebesgue-undefined. Absolutely convergent improper integrals are absorbed (Problem III.2’s ambit, via next week’s tools). Lebesgue honours everything Riemann proved; some things Riemann merely got away with.

Theorem 4.19 (Riemann absorbed).

Let $f$ be (properly) Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$. Then $f$ is measurable, integrable, and $\int_{[a,b]} f \, dm = \text{(R)}\!\int_a^b f$.

Proof.

$f$ is bounded (Riemann’s ticket, Volume One). Take partitions $P_n$, each refining the last, mesh $\to 0$, with Darboux ledgers $L(f, P_n) \uparrow$ and $U(f, P_n) \downarrow$ toward the common Riemann value $R$ (integrability). Let $\varphi_n \le f \le \psi_n$ be the step functions taking the slice-infima and slice-suprema on $P_n$'s slices: simple functions with $\int \varphi_n = L(f, P_n)$ and $\int \psi_n = U(f, P_n)$ — the merchant’s multiplication reading Darboux’s books aloud. Refinement makes $\varphi_n \uparrow \varphi$ and $\psi_n \downarrow \psi$ pointwise (monotone bounded sequences; Volume One’s license), with $\varphi \le f \le \psi$; both limits are measurable (Theorem 4.6). On the finite-measure frame, the bounded estate prices

\[ \int (\psi - \varphi) \ \le\ \int (\psi_n - \varphi_n) \ =\ U(f, P_n) - L(f, P_n) \ \longrightarrow\ 0 \]

(monotonicity; bounded additivity for the differences, Proposition 4.13(v)); so $\int (\psi - \varphi) = 0$ with $\psi - \varphi \ge 0$, forcing $\psi = \varphi$ a.e. (Problem I.6’s little lemma: a nonnegative function of zero integral vanishes a.e.). Then $f$, trapped between a.e.-equal measurable functions, equals $\varphi$ a.e.: measurable by amnesty (Proposition 4.16(ii)), and

\[ \int f \ =\ \int \varphi \ =\ \lim_n \int \varphi_n \ =\ \lim_n L(f, P_n) \ =\ R \]

(the middle equality by the bounded two-ledger pinch against $\psi_n$; partition endpoints, countably many, are null and filed).

□

4.5 The Prophecy, Proved

Theorem 4.20 (Lebesgue’s criterion — debt ten retired).

Let $f$ be bounded on $[a,b]$, $\Delta_f$ its set of discontinuity points. Then $f$ is Riemann integrable if and only if $m(\Delta_f) = 0$.

Proof.

Write $D_\sigma = \{ x : \omega_f(x) \ge \sigma \}$ (the loud sets; oscillation as in Chapter 1), so $\Delta_f = \bigcup_n D_{1/n}$ (Chapter 1’s Lemma 1.24). Let $K$ bound $\lvert f \rvert$.

($\Rightarrow$) Fix $\sigma > 0$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose a partition with $U - L < \sigma \varepsilon$. On any slice whose interior meets $D_\sigma$, the slice’s swing is at least $\sigma$ (the gauge reads a swing of at least $\sigma$ in every window of an interior loud point); letting $w$ be those slices’ total width,

\[ \sigma \varepsilon \ >\ U - L \ =\ \sum_i (\text{swing}_i)\, \Delta x_i \ \ge\ \sigma \, w, \]

so $w < \varepsilon$: the interior-loud slices cover $D_\sigma$ save the finitely many partition points, null. Hence $m^{*}(D_\sigma) < \varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon$: $D_\sigma$ is null, and $\Delta_f$ is a countable union of nulls — null (Chapter 1’s ideal).

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $m(\Delta_f) = 0$; fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and set $\sigma = \varepsilon / \bigl( 2 (b - a) \bigr)$. The loud set $D_\sigma$ is closed (Chapter 1, Problem II.5’s grain — proved there), bounded: compact; null (inside $\Delta_f$); so finitely many open intervals cover it with total length below $\varepsilon / (8K)$ (null + compact: a countable $\varepsilon$-cover admits a finite subcover). Off those intervals, the leftover $F$ is compact with every point quiet below $\sigma$: each $x \in F$ owns $\delta_x > 0$ with the window $(x - \delta_x, x + \delta_x)$ swinging under $\sigma$ (the gauge’s approximation property); the half-radius windows cover $F$; extract a finite subcover (Heine–Borel, Volume One, Theorem 5.18 — the same manoeuvre as Heine–Cantor’s proof, Volume One 6.22, and the kinship is the point); let $\delta$ be the least half-radius. Now partition $[a, b]$ by: the cover intervals’ endpoints, and a mesh finer than $\delta$ elsewhere. Slices inside the loud cover: total width below $\varepsilon / (8K)$, swing at most $2K$ — contribution below $\varepsilon / 4$. Slices meeting $F$: each lies inside some full window (mesh under the half-radius), swing at most $\sigma$ — contribution at most $\sigma (b - a) = \varepsilon / 2$. Hence $U - L < \varepsilon$; the auditor’s criterion (Volume One, Chapter 8) stamps. The Volume One finale’s prophecy stands proved; the vocabulary crisis is dissolved; the exhibits table reads as theorems, line by line.

4.6 The Devil’s Staircase

Theorem 4.21 (The Cantor function — debt eight’s account closes).

There is a function $c : [0,1] \to [0,1]$, continuous and nondecreasing, with $c(0) = 0$, $c(1) = 1$, constant on each interval removed in the dust’s construction — hence differentiable with $c' = 0$ at almost every point — and $\int_{[0,1]} c \, dm = \tfrac12$. Its entire unit of ascent is accomplished on the null dust.

Proof.

Construction. Assign to the stage-$n$ removed middles, read left to right, the values $\tfrac{1}{2^n}, \tfrac{3}{2^n}, \dots, \tfrac{2^n - 1}{2^n}$ (each middle the dyadic mean of its neighbours’ values); the assignments are consistent under staging and monotone across the complement. For $x \in C$ set $c(x) = \sup \{ c(t) : t \text{ in removed middles}, t \le x \}$ (sup over a nonempty bounded set — small change), with $c(0) = 0$; monotonicity extends.

Continuity. $c$ is monotone with range dense in $[0,1]$ — every dyadic value is attained on a middle. A monotone function’s only discontinuities are jumps, and a jump omits an interval of values, contradicting dense range. So $c$ is continuous (and $c(1) = 1$ by the sup over values climbing to one).

Flatness a.e. On each removed middle $c$ is constant, so $c' = 0$ there; the middles carry measure one (Chapter 2); the dust where ascent happens is null.

The integral. The construction is symmetric under $x \mapsto 1 - x$ with values reflecting through $\tfrac12$: $c(x) + c(1 - x) = 1$ for all $x$ (verified on middles, extended by continuity). Reflection invariance of $m$ (Chapter 3, Problem I.7) gives $\int c(1 - x) \, dm = \int c \, dm$, so $2 \int c = \int \bigl( c(x) + c(1-x) \bigr) \, dm = m([0,1]) = 1$ (bounded additivity), and $\int c = \tfrac12$.

□
Theorem 4.22 (The two gifts).

Let $h(x) = x + c(x)$ on $[0,1]$. Then $h$ is a homeomorphism onto $[0, 2]$ with $m\bigl( h(C) \bigr) = 1$; and there exist

  1. (i)

    a measurable set $A \subseteq C$ (null, hence a citizen) that is not Borel;

  2. (ii)

    a measurable function $g$ and a continuous function $F$ with $g \circ F$ non-measurable — the composition caution, detonated.

(One conjuring, declared: the ghost inside $h(C)$ is Chapter 3’s.)

Proof.

$h$ is continuous and strictly increasing (identity strictly, $c$ nondecreasing): a continuous bijection $[0,1] \to [0,2]$, hence a homeomorphism (compact domain; Volume One’s machinery). On each removed middle, $c$ is constant, so $h$ is a translation there: lengths preserved; the complement’s beads carry total measure one into $[0,2]$, and additivity leaves $m(h(C)) = 2 - 1 = 1$. The ghost factory (Chapter 3, Theorem 3.6) supplies non-measurable $N \subseteq h(C)$. Set $A = h^{-1}(N) \subseteq C$: null by heredity, hence a citizen by amnesty. Not Borel: were $A$ Borel, then $N = (h^{-1})^{-1}(A)$ would be a Borel set’s preimage under the continuous $h^{-1}$ — Borel (Proposition 4.2) — but ghosts are not even measurable. The detonation: $g = \mathbf{1}_{A}$ is measurable (citizen indicator); $F = h^{-1} : [0,2] \to [0,1]$ is continuous; and $g \circ F = \mathbf{1}_{N}$, whose unit pile is the ghost: non-measurable. The veil made specimen: Chapter 2’s count argued that non-Borel citizens exist; here is one, a single conjuring back.

□

(defects on $\Q$), so $\int t = 0$ by Proposition 4.16(i) against the zero function. I.8 Ramp’s step: Darboux $\tfrac12$ (Volume One); merchant: $0 \cdot \tfrac12 + 1 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$.

II.2 (solution in full).  The pinch. With $K$ a bound and resolution $n > K$: Theorem 4.9’s $\varphi_n$ has $f - 2^{-n} < \varphi_n \le f$ pointwise (the bin width), so $\psi_n = \varphi_n + 2^{-n} \mathbf{1}_E$ is simple with $f \le \psi_n$ and $\int \psi_n - \int \varphi_n = 2^{-n} m(E)$. Hence $\inf_{\psi \ge f} \int \psi - \sup_{\varphi \le f} \int \varphi \le 2^{-n} m(E) \to 0$; the reverse inequality is monotonicity (any $\varphi \le f \le \psi$ has $\int \varphi \le \int \psi$ via Proposition 4.8). Bounded additivity. For bounded $f, g \ge 0$ on finite ground: take under-staircases $\varphi \le f$, $\chi \le g$; $\varphi + \chi \le f + g$ is simple, so $\int f + \int g \le \int (f+g)$ (sup over sums). For $\le$: over-staircases $\psi \ge f$, $\omega \ge g$ give the simple $\psi + \omega \ge f + g$, whence every under-staircase of $f + g$ prices at most $\int \psi + \int \omega$ (Proposition 4.8); infimize using the pinch on each: $\int (f + g) \le \int f + \int g$. Signed bounded cases split into parts, all finite on finite ground. Dividend banked: every use in Theorems 4.19 and 4.20 cites this problem.

II.3 (solution in full).  Monotonicity of the step sandwiches. $P_{n+1}$ refines $P_n$: each $P_{n+1}$-slice sits inside a $P_n$-slice, so slice-infima rise ($\varphi_{n+1} \ge \varphi_n$) and slice-suprema fall, pointwise off the (countable, null) set of all partition points — where values may be redefined freely under the a.e. dialect (Proposition 4.16). Limits. Monotone bounded sequences converge pointwise (Volume One, MCT for sequences); $\varphi, \psi$ measurable by Theorem 4.6. The pinch. $\int(\psi - \varphi) \le \int (\psi_n - \varphi_n) = U - L \to 0$ uses monotonicity of the integral on the nonnegative differences (Proposition 4.13(i)) and bounded additivity (II.2) to split $\int \psi_n - \int \varphi_n$; I.6 then forces $\psi = \varphi$ a.e.; amnesty passes measurability to $f$; and $\int f = \int \varphi = \lim L(f, P_n) = R$, the middle step because $\varphi_n \le \varphi \le \psi_n$ pinches $\int \varphi$ between $L(f, P_n)$ and $U(f, P_n)$.
