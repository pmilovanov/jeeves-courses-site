Chapter 6The Zoo of Convergence, or Littlewood’s Three Principles

Companion to Episode Six. The episode read Littlewood’s three principles as field guide, paraded the five modes with their working currencies, walked the implication map compass in hand, staged the typewriter (wonder beat: "you can hear the carriage return"), extracted the subsequence consolation with its Borel–Cantelli skeleton flagged for the finale, proved Egorov aloud in strategy (shrinking crankshaft + the gambit), assembled Lusin from three nearlies, built the mean distance (amnesty as geometry), rang ring seventeen at Riesz–Fischer on ring twelve’s precedent, and exiled Hölder and Minkowski by name — "the honest division of labour is not a compromise; it is the design." This chapter is the paperwork; its core is the diagram with a proof or a counterexample on every arrow, per the plan’s mandate. Register notes: the bell rings once — $(\circledast)$ at Theorem 6.10, flagged for veto, precedent Volume One’s ring twelve.

Episode Digest

The argument of the hour. Five modes, one map, three principles. Uniform, pointwise, almost-everywhere, in-the-mean, in-measure — each the currency of a working question (guarantees, trajectories, expected loss, consistency). The map: uniform at the headwaters; mean $\to$ measure by Chebyshev’s embryo (mass bounds acreage); a.e. $\to$ measure on finite ground (the bump patrols the frontier); mean and a.e. incomparable (the spike one way, the typewriter the other). The typewriter — blocks sweeping the interval in halving generations — converges in measure and mean, pointwise nowhere: smallness of offence and persistence of offence are different failures. In-measure extracts a.e. along a fast subsequence (the gambit + tail-trap; Borel–Cantelli in embryo). Egorov: on finite ground, a.e. convergence is uniform off epsilon acreage (shrinking crankshaft, finite floor cited; the bump witnesses the clause). Lusin: measurable functions are continuous off epsilon acreage (restriction, not pointwise — the static on the irrationals pins the distinction; the property characterizes measurability); assembled from staircases + the first principle + Egorov + the three-legged stool. The mean distance makes the integrable functions a space (points = a.e.-classes); Riesz–Fischer: it is complete — fast subsequence, stacked series, climbing license, dominated closure — ring seventeen on ring twelve’s precedent. Hölder and Minkowski: named, motivated, exiled; proved at the desk via Young.

6.2 The Map

Definition 6.1 (The two integral modes).

On measurable ground $E$: $f_n \to f$ in the mean when $\int_E \lvert f_n - f \rvert \, dm \to 0$; in measure when for every $\varepsilon > 0$, $m\{ x \in E : \lvert f_n(x) - f(x) \rvert \ge \varepsilon \} \to 0$. (Uniform, pointwise, and a.e. are of long standing.)

Lemma 6.2 (Chebyshev’s embryo: mass bounds acreage).

For measurable $g \ge 0$ and $\varepsilon > 0$: $m\{ g \ge \varepsilon \} \le \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon} \int g \, dm$.

Proof.

$\varepsilon \, \mathbf{1}_{\{g \ge \varepsilon\}} \le g$ pointwise; integrate (manners, Chapter 4). The finale christens the general form.

□
Theorem 6.3 (The diagram, every arrow warranted).

On ground $E$:

  1. (i)

    uniform $\Rightarrow$ pointwise $\Rightarrow$ a.e.; uniform $\Rightarrow$ mean when $m(E) < \infty$ ($\lVert f_n - f \rVert_1 \le d(f_n, f) \, m(E)$); the spread kills the finiteness clause.

  2. (ii)

    mean $\Rightarrow$ measure, always (Lemma 6.2 on $\lvert f_n - f \rvert$).

  3. (iii)

    a.e. $\Rightarrow$ measure when $m(E) < \infty$ (via Theorem 6.7: off acreage $\varepsilon$ the discrepancy is eventually uniform, hence eventually below $\varepsilon$ everywhere there; the offending ground is eventually inside the sacrificed acreage). The bump kills the clause on infinite ground.

  4. (iv)

    mean $\not\Rightarrow$ a.e. (the typewriter, Proposition 6.4); a.e. $\not\Rightarrow$ mean (the spike); measure $\not\Rightarrow$ a.e. (typewriter); measure $\not\Rightarrow$ mean (spike, which converges in measure); pointwise $\not\Rightarrow$ uniform (Volume One’s ramps); a.e. $\not\Rightarrow$ pointwise (redefine on $\Q$).

The five-by-five table with one-line warrants is Problem I.1’s completed grid.

Proof.

All displayed; (iii)’s derivation from Egorov is Problem II.1, solved in full, with the bump’s frontier patrol.

□
Proposition 6.4 (The typewriter).

For $n = 2^k + j$, $0 \le j < 2^k$, let $T_n = \mathbf{1}_{[j 2^{-k}, (j+1) 2^{-k}]}$ on $[0, 1]$. Then $T_n \to 0$ in measure and in the mean ($m(\mathrm{supp}\, T_n) = \int T_n = 2^{-k} \to 0$), and $(T_n(x))$ converges for no $x \in [0,1]$: each generation’s carriage passes every point once, so $T_n(x) = 1$ infinitely often and $T_n(x) = 0$ infinitely often.

Proof.

The two computations are displayed; the pointwise verdict is Problem II.2, solved in full (for each $k$ there is exactly one $j$ with $x \in [j 2^{-k}, (j+1) 2^{-k}]$ — two at a dyadic seam, which only helps — so ones recur eternally; zeros recur since $2^k > 1$ blocks per generation miss $x$).

□
Theorem 6.5 (The consolation: a.e. along a subsequence).

If $f_n \to f$ in measure on $E$, there are indices $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots$ with $f_{n_k} \to f$ almost everywhere.

Proof.

Harvest: choose $n_k$ with $m(A_k) \le 2^{-k}$ for $A_k = \{ \lvert f_{n_k} - f \rvert \ge 2^{-k} \}$ (in-measure convergence supplies each). Tail-trap: let $B_K = \bigcup_{k \ge K} A_k$, so $m(B_K) \le 2^{1-K}$ (the gambit’s series), and $N = \bigcap_K B_K$ is null. Off $N$: $x$ eventually avoids every $A_k$ beyond some $K$, so $\lvert f_{n_k}(x) - f(x) \rvert < 2^{-k}$ for all $k \ge K$: convergence.

□
Remark 6.6 (The skeleton, flagged).

The step “summable acreages $\Rightarrow$ the visited-infinitely-often set is null” is the first Borel–Cantelli lemma, proved here in embryo; the finale states it under its name and spends it on almost-sure convergence. A converse service: if every subsequence of $(f_n)$ has a further subsequence converging a.e. to $f$, then $f_n \to f$ in measure (Problem II.4 — the trade’s standard trick for upgrading subsequential verdicts).

Theorem 6.7 (Egorov, 1911).

Let $m(E) < \infty$ and $f_n \to f$ a.e. on $E$, all measurable and finite a.e. For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a citizen $A \subseteq E$ with $m(E \setminus A) < \varepsilon$ and $f_n \rightrightarrows f$ on $A$.

Proof.

For $k, n \in \N$ let $E_{n,k} = \bigcup_{m \ge n} \{ \lvert f_m - f \rvert > \tfrac1k \}$: citizens, shrinking in $n$. Their intersection over $n$ is contained in the null non-convergence set (a point in every $E_{n,k}$ suffers $\tfrac1k$-discrepancies beyond every stage), so, the finite floor being available ($m(E) < \infty$ — the crankshaft’s shrinking clause, Proposition 1.3(ii), spent here and nowhere else), $m(E_{n,k}) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. Choose $n_k$ with $m(E_{n_k, k}) < \varepsilon 2^{-k}$ (the gambit); set $A = E \setminus \bigcup_k E_{n_k, k}$, so $m(E \setminus A) < \varepsilon$. On $A$: for every $k$ and every $m \ge n_k$, $\lvert f_m - f \rvert \le \tfrac1k$ — one threshold per tolerance, valid across all of $A$: uniform convergence. The bump on $[0, \infty)$ converges everywhere yet is uniform on no co-finite-acreage set (its unit block always escapes any fixed bounded remainder): the finiteness clause is real.

□
Theorem 6.8 (Lusin, 1912 — sketched, with references).

Let $f$ be measurable and finite a.e. on $[a,b]$. For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a closed $F \subseteq [a,b]$ with $m([a,b] \setminus F) < \varepsilon$ such that the restriction $f\restriction_F$ is continuous. Moreover the property characterizes measurability.

Assembly (the three nearlies, epsilons split by the gambit). Staircases $\varphi_j \to f$ a.e. (Theorem 4.9); each $\varphi_j$ is continuous off open acreage $\varepsilon 2^{-j-1}$ — its finitely many storeys are nearly finite unions of intervals (Littlewood’s first principle, Chapter 2’s manners, Problem II.4 there), and off the boundary slivers the storey structure is locally constant; Egorov (Theorem 6.7) makes $\varphi_j \to f$ uniform off acreage $\varepsilon / 4$; a uniform limit of continuous restrictions is continuous (Volume One’s three-legged stool, on the restricted set); the closed certificate comes from inner regularity (Chapter 2). Full details: Axler 2D, Royden ch. 3, Stein–Shakarchi ch. 1; the characterization converse: Problem III.1’s references. Footnote pinned in the episode: continuity of the restriction, not continuity at points — the static restricted to the irrationals is identically $0$.

6.3 The Mean Distance and Riesz–Fischer

Definition 6.9 ($L^1$; the mean distance).

$L^1(E)$: the integrable functions on $E$, identified when equal a.e. (the amnesty as geometry: distance-zero pairs are one point); $d_1(f, g) = \int_E \lvert f - g \rvert \, dm$. Metric axioms: symmetry and nonnegativity are manifest; $d_1(f,g) = 0$ iff $f = g$ a.e. (Chapter 4’s little lemma, I.6); the triangle inequality is additivity on the pointwise triangle $\lvert f - h \rvert \le \lvert f - g \rvert + \lvert g - h \rvert$.

Theorem 6.10 ($(\circledast)$ Riesz–Fischer, 1907 — ring seventeen).

$L^1(E)$ is complete: every Cauchy sequence in the mean distance converges in the mean to a member of $L^1(E)$.

Proof.

(The guided centrepiece, solved in full; each jewel cited at its expenditure.) Let $(f_n)$ be $d_1$-Cauchy. Harvest $n_k$ with $d_1(f_{n_{k+1}}, f_{n_k}) \le 2^{-k}$ (Cauchy supplies; the gambit’s rates). Stack: let $g = \lvert f_{n_1} \rvert + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \lvert f_{n_{k+1}} - f_{n_k} \rvert$, a nonnegative measurable function; the climbing license through the series corollary (Corollary 5.4) prices $\int g \le \int \lvert f_{n_1} \rvert + \sum_k 2^{-k} < \infty$: $g$ is integrable, hence finite a.e. Limit: where $g$ is finite, the telescoping series $f_{n_1} + \sum_k (f_{n_{k+1}} - f_{n_k})$ converges absolutely (Volume One’s comparison against the stack); call the a.e. limit $f$ — measurable (Theorem 4.6), and $\lvert f \rvert \le g$: integrable. Closure: $\lvert f - f_{n_k} \rvert \le \sum_{j \ge k} \lvert f_{n_{j+1}} - f_{n_j} \rvert \le g$ with a.e. convergence to $0$; the dominated jewel’s rider (Corollary 5.9, via Theorem 5.8 with lid $g$) gives $d_1(f, f_{n_k}) \to 0$; and a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges (Volume One’s huddle lemma, verbatim in any metric): $d_1(f, f_n) \to 0$. The bell rings — the seventeenth: the completeness of the space in which twentieth-century analysis lives, on the precedent of ring twelve (Volume One’s $C$ under the uniform distance); flagged for veto, per standing practice.

6.4 The Exiles

Theorem 6.11 (Young’s inequality).

Let $p, q \in (1, \infty)$ with $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q = 1$. For $a, b \ge 0$: $ab \le \tfrac{a^p}{p} + \tfrac{b^q}{q}$, with equality iff $a^p = b^q$.

Proof.

Fix $b \ge 0$ and maximize $h(a) = ab - a^p / p$ over $a \ge 0$: $h'(a) = b - a^{p-1}$ vanishes at $a_* = b^{1/(p-1)} = b^{q-1}$ — a maximum, $h''< 0$ (Volume One’s harvest). Then $h(a_*) = b^{q-1} b - b^{(q-1)p}/p = b^q (1 - \tfrac1p) = b^q / q$, using $(q-1)p = q$. So $ab - a^p/p \le b^q / q$ for all $a$, which is the claim; equality exactly at $a = a_*$, i.e. $a^p = b^q$. (No logarithms; the credit stands unspent.)

□
Theorem 6.12 (Hölder’s inequality).

With $p, q$ conjugate and $f, g$ measurable:

\[ \int \lvert f g \rvert \, dm \ \le\ \Bigl( \int \lvert f \rvert^p \Bigr)^{1/p} \Bigl( \int \lvert g \rvert^q \Bigr)^{1/q} \ =\ \lVert f \rVert_p \, \lVert g \rVert_q . \]
Proof.

If either norm is $0$ or $\infty$, the desk’s two-line cases settle it (a null factor annihilates; an infinite bound is vacuous). Otherwise normalize: $u = \lvert f \rvert / \lVert f \rVert_p$, $v = \lvert g \rvert / \lVert g \rVert_q$; Young pointwise: $uv \le u^p / p + v^q / q$; integrate (additivity): $\int uv \le \tfrac1p + \tfrac1q = 1$; unnormalize.

□
Theorem 6.13 (Minkowski’s inequality).

$\lVert f + g \rVert_p \le \lVert f \rVert_p + \lVert g \rVert_p$ for $1 \le p < \infty$.

Proof.

$p = 1$ is additivity on the pointwise triangle. For $p > 1$:

\[ \int \lvert f + g \rvert^p \le \int \lvert f \rvert \, \lvert f + g \rvert^{p-1} + \int \lvert g \rvert \, \lvert f + g \rvert^{p-1} \le \bigl( \lVert f \rVert_p + \lVert g \rVert_p \bigr) \, \bigl\lVert \, \lvert f+g \rvert^{p-1} \bigr\rVert_q \]

by the pointwise triangle then Hölder twice; and $\lVert \lvert f+g \rvert^{p-1} \rVert_q = ( \int \lvert f+g \rvert^p )^{1/q}$ since $(p-1)q = p$. Divide (finiteness first: $\lvert f + g \rvert^p \le 2^p (\lvert f \rvert^p + \lvert g \rvert^p)$ keeps the divisor finite; zero divisor: nothing to prove).

□
Remark 6.14 (The $L^p$ glimpse).

On finite ground, $L^p \subseteq L^r$ for $p > r$ (Hölder with exponent $p/r$ against the constant one: Problem I.8); the family’s completeness generalizes Theorem 6.10 (Problem III.1). The course’s boundary: duality, Hilbert space, and Fourier’s settlement belong to a further season; the Notes point onward.

6.6 Hints

II.3.  The crankshaft’s shrinking clause demands a finite first floor; $E_{n,1}$ for the bump has infinite measure for every $n$.

II.4.  If $f_n \not\to f$ in measure: some $\varepsilon_0, \delta_0$ and indices $n_j$ with $m\{\lvert f_{n_j} - f \rvert \ge \varepsilon_0\} \ge \delta_0$; no further subsequence can converge a.e. (it would converge in measure on the finite frame trapping $\delta_0$ of the acreage — II.1).

II.5.  Closed: inner regularity (Chapter 2) inside the Egorov set; the three sacrifices: boundary slivers ($\varepsilon/2$ summed), Egorov ($\varepsilon/4$), regularity ($\varepsilon/4$).

III.2.  In a metric space, $x_n \to x$ iff every subsequence has a sub-subsequence $\to x$; the typewriter violates the conclusion while satisfying the premise for a.e.-mode.

6.7 Solutions

Tier I (terse).  I.1 Grid as in Theorem 6.3; the spread’s line: uniform yes, pointwise yes, a.e. yes, mean no, measure yes (acreage $\{ \tfrac1n \ge \varepsilon \}$ is empty for large $n$ — note it converges in measure though not in mean). I.5 $A = [\tfrac1{10}, 1]$: beyond $n = 10$ the spike’s support misses $A$; threshold $N = 10$ for every tolerance. I.7 $d_1(D, 0) = 0$ — the static IS the zero of $L^1$: one point of the space, the amnesty as geometry made vivid; $d_1(t, 0) = 0$ likewise; two generation-$k$ blocks: $2 \cdot 2^{-k}$ when disjoint. I.8 $\lVert f \rVert_1 = \int \lvert f \rvert \cdot 1 \le \lVert f \rVert_2 \lVert 1 \rVert_2 = \lVert f \rVert_2$; on $[0,\infty)$: $f = \tfrac1x \mathbf{1}_{[1,\infty)}$ has $\lVert f \rVert_2 < \infty = \lVert f \rVert_1$.

II.1 (solution in full).  Let $m(E) < \infty$, $f_n \to f$ a.e., $\varepsilon > 0$, $\delta > 0$. Egorov supplies $A$ with $m(E \setminus A) < \delta$ and uniform convergence on $A$: beyond some $N$, $\lvert f_n - f \rvert < \varepsilon$ on all of $A$, so $\{ \lvert f_n - f \rvert \ge \varepsilon \} \subseteq E \setminus A$ for $n \ge N$: acreage below $\delta$. As $\delta$ was arbitrary, the acreage tends to $0$: in-measure convergence. The bump: $\{ \lvert \mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]} \rvert \ge \tfrac12 \}$ has measure $1$ for every $n$ — the arrow dies without the finite floor, and Egorov’s own hypothesis was the casualty (Problem II.3).

II.2 (solution in full).  Fix $x \in [0,1]$ and a generation $k$. The blocks $[j 2^{-k}, (j+1) 2^{-k}]$, $j = 0, \dots, 2^k - 1$, cover $[0,1]$, so some $j$ has $x$ in its block: $T_{2^k + j}(x) = 1$. Ones occur in every generation: infinitely often. For zeros: $2^k \ge 2$ blocks exist from generation one onward and at most two contain $x$ (a seam), so some block misses $x$: $T_n(x) = 0$ infinitely often. A sequence with both values infinitely often has no limit; no $x$ is spared, seams included. (The subsequence of first blocks $T_{2^k}$ converges to $0$ at every $x > 0$: the consolation prize’s illustration.)
