Chapter 3Vitali and Banach–Tarski, or the Price of Choice

Companion to Episode Three. The episode called the catch’s trial: the measure problem framed and closed; Vitali’s construction narrated in full (the sorting, the delegation — Choice’s first flagged expenditure — the tiling, the two-sided absurdity); the ghost’s own ledgers read (inner zero, outer positive) and the ghost-factory corollary (every set of positive measure harbours non-measurable subsets); the confession (Zermelo 1904; the Five Letters with Hadamard carrying the day against the course’s own heroes; Zermelo’s 1908 defence; the plea bargain via Gödel and Cohen); the domestic audit (this course’s own quiet countable choices); Solovay’s verdict, honestly fine-printed; the alphabet trick (the free group doubles); the transport to the sphere and ball; Banach–Tarski stated with full effect and defused (the pieces are ghosts; no volume was ever present to conserve; dimension’s role — commutative alphabets cannot spell paradox; Banach’s finitely additive extensions in dimensions one and two); the paradox-as-instrument service note; and debt six paid with the family gathered. This chapter is the paperwork. Register notes: the bell is silent — count holds at fifteen; the evening spent Choice, not completeness, and the two axioms run different departments. Debt thirteen (the catch) is proved at Theorem 3.4; debt six is paid and closed at Remark 3.17. Choice’s expenditures are flagged at Definition 3.2 and Theorem 3.14 — the register’s new column, “conjurings, declared.”

Episode Digest

The argument of the hour. The measure problem — extend Lebesgue’s measure to every set — closes in the negative, and the culprit is not measure but Choice. Vitali: sort the reals into families differing by rationals (canonical, constructive); choose one delegate per family (the axiom’s flagged expenditure); the delegation’s rational shifts tile disjointly, blanket the unit interval, and fit inside a length-three frame — whence no measure value, zero or positive, is consistent: the contract cannot hold on all sets (debt thirteen, proved). The ghost has readable ledgers (inner zero, outer positive: Jordan’s gap, incurable) and breeds (every positive-measure set contains a ghost). The confession: the Five Letters — Baire, Borel, Lebesgue objecting, each in his own key; Hadamard’s diagnosis (psychology, not mathematics); Zermelo’s defence; the plea bargain (consistent by Gödel, independent by Cohen, retained for its indispensability — the course’s own census breakfasted on it). Solovay: without full Choice (dependent choice retained, a higher-infinite hypothesis honestly named, Shelah’s necessity cited), “every set measurable” is consistent — ghosts are purchasable, never discoverable. Then the theatre: the free group’s four bins double the dictionary (the alphabet trick, pure combinatorics); two free rotations embed the alphabet in the sphere’s symmetries (powers-of-three fingerprint); orbits, delegates (Choice again, flagged), the sphere doubles; exceptions absorbed by hotel manoeuvres; the ball follows; five pieces optimal. Banach–Tarski stated with effect, then defused: the pieces are non-measurable mists — the theorem plus measure theory proves their ghostliness; no conservation law touched; matter measurable, physics safe; dimensions one and two immune (commutative alphabets; Banach’s finitely additive extensions); amenability named at the door. The word “measurable” in every hypothesis of your working library is Vitali’s monument — and everything constructible is measurable: the monster’s practical content is reassurance. Debt six paid: the two-population policy adopted, conjurings declared.

3.2 The Vitali Construction

Proposition 3.1 (The sorting).

The relation $x \sim y \iff x - y \in \Q$ is an equivalence on $\R$. Each class $[x] = x + \Q$ is countable and dense in $\R$; the classes partition $\R$; and there are uncountably many classes.

Proof.

Reflexive: $x - x = 0 \in \Q$. Symmetric: negatives of rationals are rational. Transitive: $(x - y) + (y - z) = x - z$, and $\Q$ is closed under addition (Volume One, Chapter 2). Each class is the bijective image $q \mapsto x + q$ of $\Q$: countable, and dense since $\Q$ is (translation preserves density). Partition: equivalence classes always partition. Uncountably many: a countable family of countable classes would render $\R$ countable (the census, Volume One, Chapter 3, read in reverse) — contradicting the diagonal.

□
Definition 3.2 (The delegation — a conjuring, declared).

Each class meets $[0,1]$ (density). By the Axiom of Choice, select exactly one point of $[0,1]$ from each class; let $V$ be the set of delegates. No rule performs the selection: the classes are structureless, uncountably many, and almost all their members are unnameable (Volume One, Chapter 3’s census); the axiom asserts the selection’s existence and provides nothing further.

Lemma 3.3 (The tiling).

Let $q_1, q_2, \dots$ enumerate $\Q \cap [-1, 1]$. Then:

  1. (i)

    the sets $V + q_k$ are pairwise disjoint;

  2. (ii)

    $[0,1] \ \subseteq\ \bigcup_k (V + q_k) \ \subseteq\ [-1, 2]$.

Proof.

(i) If $v + q_k = w + q_j$ with $v, w \in V$, then $v - w = q_j - q_k \in \Q$: $v \sim w$, so $v = w$ (one delegate per class), whence $q_k = q_j$.

(ii) Right inclusion: $V \subseteq [0,1]$ and $q_k \in [-1,1]$. Left: let $x \in [0,1]$; its class has a delegate $d \in V \cap [0,1]$; then $q = x - d \in \Q$ and $q \in [-1, 1]$ (both points in $[0,1]$), so $q = q_k$ for some $k$ and $x \in V + q_k$.

□
Theorem 3.4 (Vitali, 1905: the catch, proved).

$V$ is not Lebesgue measurable. Consequently there is no assignment $\mu$ on all subsets of $\R$ satisfying the contract of Chapter 1 (nonnegativity, intervals to length, translation invariance, countable additivity).

Proof.

Suppose $V \in \mathcal{M}$ with $m(V) = v$. Citizenship translates (Chapter 2), so each $V + q_k \in \mathcal{M}$ with measure $v$; the union $U = \bigsqcup_k (V + q_k)$ is a countable disjoint union of citizens, and countable additivity prices it:

\[ m(U) \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} m(V + q_k) \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} v \ =\ \begin{cases} 0, & v = 0,\\ \infty, & v > 0 \end{cases} \]

(a series of identical nonnegative terms admits no third verdict; Volume One, Chapter 4). But the sandwich (Lemma 3.3) and monotonicity demand

\[ 1 = m([0,1]) \ \le\ m(U) \ \le\ m([-1,2]) = 3 . \]

Door one ($v = 0$): $m(U) = 0 < 1$. Door two ($v > 0$): $m(U) = \infty > 3$. Both contradict. So $V \notin \mathcal{M}$. For the second statement: the argument above used only the contract’s clauses — additivity, translation invariance, interval pricing, and monotonicity (additivity’s child) — so any full-contract $\mu$ on all sets meets the same two doors with $\mu(V)$ in place of $v$. No such $\mu$ exists. Debt thirteen: proved as charged.

□
Proposition 3.5 (The ghost’s ledgers).

$m^{*}(V) > 0$, while every closed (indeed every measurable) $F \subseteq V$ is null — the inner measure of $V$ is zero.

Proof.

If $m^{*}(V) = 0$, $V$ would be null, hence a measurable citizen (Chapter 2), contradicting Theorem 3.4. For the inner ledger, let $F \subseteq V$ be measurable. The shifts $F + q_k$ are pairwise disjoint (inherited from Lemma 3.3(i)) and lie in $[-1,2]$; countable additivity and invariance give $\sum_k m(F) \le 3$, forcing $m(F) = 0$.

□
Theorem 3.6 (The ghost factory).

Every $E \subseteq \R$ with $m^{*}(E) > 0$ contains a non-measurable subset.

Proof.

It suffices to treat $E \subseteq [0,1]$ ($E$ meets some unit frame in positive outer measure — countable subadditivity across frames — and subsets transfer). Write $E = \bigcup_k \bigl( E \cap (V + q_k) \bigr)$, the tiling restricted to $E$ (Lemma 3.3(ii), with $\Q \cap [-1,1]$ re-enumerated as needed). If every piece $E \cap (V + q_k)$ were measurable, each would be null — it sits inside a translate of $V$, and Proposition 3.5’s argument applies verbatim to measurable subsets of translates — whence $E$, a countable union of null sets, would be null: contradicting $m^{*}(E) > 0$. Some piece is non-measurable.

□
Remark 3.7 (The confession, the audit, the verdict).

Choice and aliases. The axiom (every family of nonempty sets admits a choice function) is equivalent to the well-ordering principle and to Zorn’s lemma; Jech and the notes chart the folklore. The Five Letters (Hadamard–Borel–Baire–Lebesgue, 1905) are reprinted and translated in the references; the positions as narrated. Domestic audit. This course’s quiet expenditures are countable/dependent choice: the census theorem (choosing enumerations), sequence extractions toward suprema and accumulation points, and kin. Dependent choice suffices for all of them; they are retained in Solovay’s model and are hereby declared once for the whole course.

Remark 3.8 (Solovay, with the fine print).

Solovay (1970): relative to the consistency of an inaccessible cardinal, ZF + dependent choice + “every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable” is consistent. Shelah (1984): the inaccessible is necessary. Consequently no Choice-free construction of a non-measurable set exists; ghosts are purchased, never found. The plea bargain’s other halves: Gödel (1938) — Choice is consistent; Cohen (1963) — so is its negation. References: Solovay’s Annals paper; Shelah; Jech; Wagon.

3.3 The Alphabet Trick

Definition 3.9 (Equidecomposability; paradoxical sets).

Let a group $G$ act on a set $X$. Subsets $A, B \subseteq X$ are $G$-equidecomposable, $A \sim_G B$, when $A$ and $B$ admit partitions $A = \bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i$, $B = \bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n} B_i$ with $B_i = g_i A_i$ for some $g_i \in G$. (Transitivity requires refining pieces against pieces: Problem II.4.)

Definition 3.10 (Paradoxical).

$X$ is $G$-paradoxical when $X$ contains disjoint subsets $A, B$ with $A \sim_G X$ and $B \sim_G X$: two full copies, carved from one, with room to spare.

Theorem 3.11 (The free group doubles).

Let $F$ be the free group on letters $a, b$, acting on itself by left multiplication, and for each symbol $x \in \{a, a^{-1}, b, b^{-1}\}$ let $W(x)$ be the set of reduced words with leading symbol $x$. Then

\[ F \ =\ \{e\} \sqcup W(a) \sqcup W(a^{-1}) \sqcup W(b) \sqcup W(b^{-1}), \]
\[ F \ =\ W(a) \ \sqcup\ a \, W(a^{-1}), \qquad F \ =\ W(b) \ \sqcup\ b \, W(b^{-1}), \]

whence $F$ is $F$-paradoxical: the four bins assemble two copies of the dictionary, the empty word to spare.

Proof.

The five-fold partition is the definition of “leading symbol.” For the first reassembly: $a \, W(a^{-1})$ consists of the reductions of $a w$ with $w$ leading with $a^{-1}$ — write $w = a^{-1} u$ where $u$ is reduced and does not lead with $a$ (else $w$ was not reduced); then $a w = u$. As $w$ ranges over $W(a^{-1})$, $u$ ranges over exactly the reduced words not leading with $a$ (including $e$, from $w = a^{-1}$): $a \, W(a^{-1}) = F \setminus W(a)$. Disjointness is the same computation read backward: a word cannot both lead with $a$ and be the reduction of $a \cdot (a^{-1}u)$ with $u$ not leading with $a$. The $b$-reassembly mirrors. Paradoxicality: take $A = W(a) \cup W(a^{-1})$ and $B = W(b) \cup W(b^{-1})$ — disjoint; each reassembles $F$ with one one-letter motion. (Guided as the Tier II summit, Problem II.5, where every cancellation is tracked.)

□
Remark 3.12 (Commutative alphabets collapse).

If $ab = ba$, the word $a b a^{-1} b^{-1}$ reduces to $e$: bins leak into one another, the leading-symbol sorting is not even well-defined on normal forms, and no four-bin doubling exists — correctly so, since Banach’s extensions (Remark 3.16) forbid paradox in the commutative motion groups of the line and plane. Freedom of the letters is the paradox’s fuel; the desk’s amenability note (Problem 3.5) is this observation grown up.

3.4 The Sphere and the Ball

Remark 3.13 (Free rotations: the fingerprint).

Two rotations by $\arccos \tfrac13$ about perpendicular axes generate a free subgroup of the rotation group: in coordinates, each letter multiplies entries by thirds, and a reduced word of length $n$ carries an uncancellable factor $3^{-n}$ in designated entries — a nontrivial word cannot act as the identity, whose entries are whole. Classical references (\'Swierczkowski; Wagon, ch. 2); the fingerprint staged for the ambitious at Problem III.1.

Theorem 3.14 (The sphere and ball double — on stated references).

The sphere’s surface, minus the countable set of poles of the free rotations’ words, is paradoxical under rotations: sort the remaining points into orbits of the embedded free group; by Choice (conjuring, declared) select one delegate per orbit; the four bins of Theorem 3.11, applied to the delegates’ words, carve the punctured sphere into four pieces that reassemble two punctured spheres. The countable exceptional set is absorbed (a rotation of infinite order moves it off itself; the hotel manoeuvre — circle rehearsal at Problem II.6); radial cones carry the sphere’s carving to the punctured ball; the centre is absorbed by a rotated interior circle; and with Robinson’s optimization, five pieces suffice (and four do not). Full assembly: Wagon, chs. 2–4; Stromberg’s Monthly exposition; Tomkowicz–Wagon.

Theorem 3.15 (Banach–Tarski, 1924 — stated).

A closed ball in space is equidecomposable, under rigid motions, with the disjoint union of two copies of itself — in five pieces. Strong form: any two bounded subsets of space with nonempty interior are equidecomposable (the pea and the sun). No proof is attempted here beyond Theorem 3.14’s staging; the references carry the construction entire.

Remark 3.16 (The defusal; dimensions one and two).

If the five pieces were measurable, additivity and motion-invariance of volume (Chapter 2’s dimension license) would yield $1 = 2$; the pieces are therefore non-measurable — the theorem plus measure theory is a proof of ghostliness, and no measurable object ever changes size: physics is untouched. In dimensions one and two no doubling exists at all: Banach (1923) extended length and area to finitely additive, motion-invariant assignments on all subsets — the ghosts can be finitely priced there — because the line’s and plane’s motion groups are too commutative to spell free words (Remark 3.12); the modern dividing line is amenability (von Neumann 1929; Tarski’s alternative: paradoxical $\iff$ non-amenable), left ajar at Problem 3.5.

Remark 3.17 (Debt six: the family, the policy).

The unnameable numbers (Volume One, Chapter 3); the non-Borel veil (Chapter 2); the delegation; the five pieces: provably existent, never exhibitable — Solovay certifying the never. Policy adopted for the course: two populations, constructible and conjured; conjurings declared at the point of expenditure; every computable theorem lives in the first population. Debt six: paid and closed.

3.6 Hints

3.7 Solutions

Tier I (terse).  I.1 As in the proposition; the arithmetic is closure of $\Q$ under negation and addition. I.2 The census enumerates a countable-by-countable array; its choice footnote is Remark 3.7; reverse: uncountable $\R$ refuses such an array. I.3 A common point gives $v - w = \tfrac12 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac16 \in \Q$, so $v = w$, so $\tfrac13 = \tfrac12$ — Lemma 3.3(i)’s clause. I.4 The delegate $d$ exists in the class of $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ meeting $[0,1]$; $q = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} - d$ is rational in $[-1,1]$ by the two memberships; the argument names $q$'s existence only — the dodge is the wholesale quantifier. I.5–I.6 Mechanical; the traps cancel to shorter words, and the bin of the reduction is the leading symbol after cancellation. I.7 $abab^{-1}a^{-1}b^{-1}$-style words all reduce under commutativity; $e \in a W(a^{-1})$ and $e \notin W(a)$, but with $ab = ba$, $W(a) \cap b W(b^{-1}) \ni ab \cdot b^{-1}$-reductions: the parcels overlap. I.8 Additivity (Carathéodory’s theorem, Chapter 2), invariance (Chapter 1, II.3 + Chapter 2 citizenship), intervals (Chapter 1), monotonicity (Chapter 1) — four clauses, no more.

II.2 (solution in full).  Let $F \subseteq V + q$ be measurable, and translate: $F - q \subseteq V$ is measurable (citizenship translates) with $m(F - q) = m(F)$ (invariance). The shifts $(F - q) + q_k$, $q_k \in \Q \cap [-1,1]$, are pairwise disjoint — contained in the disjoint $V + q_k$ of Lemma 3.3(i) — and lie in $[-1, 2]$.
