# Episode Ten — Every Frame Perfect, or the Moving of a Cathedral

*The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux*

## The Name on the Road Sign

Good day, sir.

In two thousand and eight, a Danish engineer at Red Hat named Kristian Høgsberg was spending his days inside the machinery we spent last week deploring. He was no outsider to it — quite the reverse: he had built some of the extension era's own plumbing, the conduits by which three-dimensional rendering and direct hardware access had been grafted onto the elderly protocol. He knew the building, in other words, the way a heating engineer knows a stately home: from inside the walls, where the pipework tells a franker story than the plasterwork. And somewhere on his driving route through the towns west of Boston — through, as it happens, a small Massachusetts town named Wayland — the pieces of a different answer crystallised, and the project that resulted took the town's name with it. There is a pleasing municipal thoroughness to the affair, sir: the reference implementation that followed was named for Weston, the next town along. New England has no idea how much of the modern Linux desktop is named after its commuter belt.

What Høgsberg wrote down, when he announced the spare-time project later that year, was less a design than a vow, and I shall give it to you in his own words, because the season has few sentences in it more consequential: the goal was a system in which "every frame is perfect, by which I mean that applications will be able to control the rendering enough that we'll never see tearing, lag, redrawing or flicker." Sir will recognise, in each of those four nouns, a specific complaint from last week's audit — the shear across a panning scene, the beat of latency the bolted-on compositor levied, the naked repaints, the flash of stale contents. The vow was, item by item, a promise to retire the drawing room's whole catalogue of visible sins.

The difficulty was never the design. The difficulty was that the thing to be replaced was not a component but a *cathedral* — four decades of accumulated structure with the entire congregation still at prayer inside: every toolkit, every application, every screen reader and screenshot tool and test harness and window-management habit, all of them standing, knowingly or not, on the open doors and flat trust of nineteen eighty-four. This evening is the story of how the cathedral was moved — stone by stone, portal by portal, quarrel by quarrel, over the better part of two decades — and why, as I record this in early twenty twenty-six, the move is at last substantially complete. It is a story about architecture, of course. But it is more instructively a story about what replacement *costs* in a commons: our house has no proprietor to decree a new drawing room by fleet order, as Redmond and Cupertino each did; it has only persuasion, bridges, and time.

## Part One — The Verdict of the Stewards

Begin with the question every sensible householder asks before a demolition: could it not be mended? The people best placed to answer were the small crew who actually maintained X — and through the two-thousands and early twenty-tens, in talks and essays of increasing bluntness, they delivered their verdict: no. Not because the code was old, though it was; because the *premises* were wrong, and premises cannot be patched. The flat trust domain was not a bug but the protocol's constitution: its every verb assumes any client may touch any window, and an isolation retrofit breaks each verb in turn — which is why the aftermarket locks of the nineties pinched so badly that nobody wore them. The four unconducted clocks of the compositing era were likewise structural: a server that neither knows nor controls when clients draw cannot promise the monitor an assembled frame. One of X's own senior developers took to delivering a conference talk that was, in effect, a guided tour of the building's condemned notices — genial, devastating, and delivered with the authority of a man who had personally shored up half the affected walls.

And the sharpest observation in the verdict was an audit of what the server still actually *did*. Follow the evening's work of a modern X server, circa twenty ten, and the ledger reads strangely. Drawing? The clients did that now — Render's confession, completed by the toolkits painting every pixel themselves above stairs. Assembling the screen? The compositing manager did that — an ordinary client, holding everyone's buffers. Talking to the graphics hardware? Increasingly the kernel's business, as we shall see below stairs presently. Deciding window policy? Never its job to begin with — the butler's, always. What remained to the server, at the centre of the whole edifice, was *brokerage*: shuttling buffers and events between the clients, the compositor, and the kernel, adding latency at each hand-off and isolation nowhere. The grand exchange of nineteen eighty-four had become a telephone exchange through which nobody's actual conversation passed any longer — retained, at considerable expense, because every building on the street still had its wiring routed through the premises.

Høgsberg's design begins by abolishing the middleman. If the compositor already holds every window's buffer and already assembles every visible frame — then let the compositor *be* the server. In Wayland there is no separate display server standing between: the compositor owns the display, reads the input devices, and speaks directly with its clients. One process where X had three-in-a-trench-coat; one hand-off where X had a bucket brigade.

And the clients' side of the bargain completes the simplification. A Wayland client does not send drawing commands — there are none to send; the protocol has abolished the drawing service along with the middleman, ratifying what the toolkits had long since made true in practice. A client renders its window itself, above stairs, by whatever means it pleases — the graphics card through Mesa's estate, a software rasteriser, it is the client's affair — into a buffer, whole and finished. What crosses the protocol is, in essence: *here is my new frame, entire; attach it when you next compose.* The compositor, knowing precisely when the monitor will refresh — for it owns the display and keeps its heartbeat — takes each client's most recent complete frame and composes the screen atomically. No client's window is ever shown half-painted, because half-painted states never cross the boundary. No frame tears, because composition happens once, in step with the glass. The vow, sir, is not an aspiration bolted to the design; it *is* the design. Every frame is perfect because the architecture has no place where an imperfect one could leak through.

## Part Two — The New Constitution

Let me give the new constitution's shape, because its economies are as instructive as the old one's excesses. Wayland is not a program; it is a *protocol* — a small one — plus a pair of slim libraries for speaking it. There is deliberately no single canonical server: GNOME's compositor, KDE's, and a family of community compositors built on a shared foundation library each implement the protocol themselves, policy and mechanism finally under one roof apiece. Where X centralised mechanism and exiled policy, Wayland does nearly the opposite: the protocol standardises the *conversation*, and each compositor is sovereign in its own house — its own policy, its own effects, its own opinions, with no committee obliged to bless them. The lesson of X's extension hatch was learned and made a founding organ: the protocol is a small core plus named, versioned interfaces, discovered and negotiated at connection, grown over the years by proposal and adoption rather than by breaking the core. The window-management conversation itself — how a client asks to be a toplevel window or a menu, to be maximised or full-screened — is one such interface family, agreed across desktops so that applications need not care whose compositor they face.

Since this is a working tour, let me give sir the actual grammar of the conversation, because its brevity is the whole argument. A client asks the compositor for a *surface* — the abstract canvas that will become its window. It renders a frame, above stairs, into a buffer of its own. Then three verbs: *attach* the buffer to the surface; declare, if it cares to, which regions have changed since last time — the *damage*, so the compositor need recompose only what moved; and *commit* — the atomic full stop, the instant at which, and only at which, everything attached and declared becomes the surface's new official state. Between commits the compositor shows the previous frame, entire; a client that crashes mid-render leaves its last good frame on screen, not a smear. And in the other direction flows the pacing: a client may ask to be told when its next frame is *worth* drawing, and the compositor answers in time with the display — with the elegant consequence that an occluded window, receiving no such callbacks, simply stops burning power on invisible work. Where the old protocol had hundreds of requests, the working heart of the new one is this handful of verbs around a single idea: the atomic commit. Sir has met that idea twice already in this house — in the journal of the filesystem episode and the transaction of the memory ledger — and its arrival in the drawing room completes a pattern: wherever the house guarantees consistency, it does so by making change all-or-nothing.

One practical mystery deserves its answer while we are at working depth: if clients render whole frames, does the desktop not drown in copying pixels from process to process? It does not, and the trick is one of the prettier uses of machinery sir already owns from earlier evenings. The socket between client and compositor is a Unix domain socket, and over such a socket a process may pass not merely bytes but *file descriptors* — the same trick by which, in the concurrency episode, one process lent another an open door. For software-rendered frames the descriptor names a stretch of shared memory; for hardware-rendered ones it names a buffer living on the graphics card itself — the kernel's buffer-sharing handles, minted below stairs precisely so that different devices and processes can hold the same video memory without a copy. So the frame never travels, sir. What travels is the *title deed* — and in the happy case the compositor hands that same deed onward to the display hardware, which scans the client's pixels out to the glass directly: zero copies, client to cathode, so to speak. Input, meanwhile, rides the same modernisation: both worlds now read keyboards and mice through one shared library below the compositor, which is why the old wars of exotic input configuration quietly ended in the same decade.

Now set the two constitutions side by side where last week's episode hurt most. Under X, to connect is to hold full citizenship: see every window, read every keystroke, forge any input, photograph the whole room, by documented right. Under Wayland, a client's world is precisely its own windows. It cannot enumerate the others — it has no way to *ask*. It receives input only when the compositor gives it focus; it has no global view of the pointer, no sight of the screen beyond its own surfaces — indeed a client is not, in the base protocol, even told *where on the screen* its own window sits, a detail that scandalises every port from the old world and follows directly from the founding stance: such knowledge is the compositor's, and policy is no longer the client's to see. The inversion is total, and worth saying as a sentence: **under X, everything was permitted and nothing could be revoked; under Wayland, nothing is permitted and anything must be granted.** After our evening among the locks and keys, sir will recognise the shape of the reform instantly — ambient authority withdrawn, capability granted on request, the drawing room finally furnished with doors that close. Every window on the modern desktop is, in the sandbox sense, contained by construction.

## Part Three — The Price, and the Decade of Rebuilding

But now the bill, sir, and it is a long one, because here is the tragedy the drawing room set up last week: forty years of legitimate tooling had been built *on the open doors*. The screenshot utility photographed the room because any client could. The screen recorder, the video call sharing your slides, the automation harness that types into dialogue boxes, the testing rig that drives an interface by force, the colour-management tool reading and correcting the whole display, the global hotkey daemon listening for its chord behind every window, the screen reader narrating another application's widgets to a blind user — every one of them, from the burglar's-eye view of the new constitution, is *indistinguishable from an attack*. Close the doors on the burglar and you have closed them on the blind user in the same motion. This — not nostalgia, not stubbornness — is the honest reason the migration took a decade and a half, and the portion of the community that shouted loudest in the interim had a point: for years, on the new desktop, perfectly ordinary workflows simply did not work.

What was built to replace the open doors is the pattern sir met in the security episode, now applied to pictures: the brokered request. The desktop grew a set of **portals** — a neutral front-desk service, reached over the message bus, at which an application states its business: I should like to capture the screen; to pick a file; to record this window; to inject input. The portal consults policy and, where the business is sensitive, *asks the user* — the little dialogue sir has seen: this application wishes to share your screen — and only then opens a channel. And the channel itself, for anything involving moving pictures or sound, flows through the house's new media plumbing: PipeWire, the same service that quietly unified the desktop's audio in these years, carrying screen contents as a negotiated stream rather than a licence to trespass. Screenshots, screen sharing, remote desktop: rebuilt this way, one by one. Input injection acquired its own brokered library, late, for the automation and accessibility cases. Colour management landed as a protocol interface only in the middle twenties, to the professional colourists' extended and justified grumbling. Accessibility remains the slowest debt on the books — the screen-reader world's plumbing is still being re-laid as I record, and I decline to pretend otherwise. The doors now close, sir; the cost was rebuilding, over roughly a decade, every legitimate errand that had ever walked through them open, and the accounting should be stated both ways: the new errands are *better* — consented, scoped, revocable — and there were years in which they simply did not exist.

One more piece of statecraft made the whole transition survivable at all: the bridge. XWayland is a genuine X server — the real article, maintained to this day — that runs *as a Wayland client*: rootless, windowless in its own right, presenting each old application's window to the compositor as though it were native. The old country's applications connect to it and are none the wiser; the compositor composes them alongside the new. It is the embassy of the old world, sir, granted extraterritorial rights inside the new — and, in strict keeping with the season's running theme, the X clients inside the embassy still enjoy their old open-door habits *with each other* while seeing nothing of the natives around them: the flat trust domain, shrunk to a diplomatic compound. Without this bridge there is no migration; with it, the congregation could keep praying while the stones moved one at a time beneath them.

## Part Four — The Plumbing Below Stairs, and the Two Quarrels

All of this — both worlds of it, old X and new compositors alike — came to stand on plumbing that was being re-laid below stairs while the upstairs argued, and here I pay a debt booked last week. The horror of the elderly arrangement, sir will recall, was the X server as root, personally programming the graphics card's registers from userspace because the kernel offered no such service. That ended in stages around the turn of the twenty-tens as the kernel finally took graphics into the household staff proper. The Direct Rendering Manager — the kernel's graphics arbiter — became the sole authority over the hardware; **kernel mode-setting** moved the programming of displays, resolutions and refresh below stairs where it always belonged, which is why a crashing display server stopped taking the console's very ability to show text down with it; a **generic buffer manager** gave userspace a common way to allocate the graphics memory those finished frames live in; and, in time, **atomic mode-setting** let a compositor present an entire display update — every plane, every property — as a single all-or-nothing commit, timed to the monitor's refresh. Note what that last one is, sir: it is the vow, rendered into kernel machinery. Every frame is perfect upstairs because below stairs the house finally offers a way to flip the whole tableau at once or not at all. And with the hardware's keys surrendered to the staff, the display server — old or new — was at last demoted from root to an ordinary member of the family, some thirty years after the pilot's bright line said it should have been.

Now the quarrels, of which the cathedral's move had two worth the record.

The first was the affair of the rival. In March of twenty thirteen, Canonical — the house of Ubuntu, then at the height of its convergence ambitions, one interface from telephone to desktop — announced that it would build neither on X nor on Wayland but on a display server of its own, named Mir. The announcement landed badly, and then worse: its stated technical case against Wayland contained claims about the protocol that were simply mistaken — misreadings publicly corrected by Wayland's developers, with an apology in due course from the authors — and the community read the venture, fairly or not, as fragmentation in service of one vendor's control. The temperature of the period is fixed by one detail sir may enjoy: Intel's graphics-driver team publicly declined to carry Canonical's compatibility patches in their upstream driver. Within the year Canonical had shelved the plan to ship Mir under the ordinary desktop; the telephone-and-tablet dream carried it alone for a while; and in April of twenty seventeen Mark Shuttleworth ended the whole campaign in a single announcement — convergence abandoned, the in-house desktop retired, Ubuntu returning to GNOME, which meant, with quiet finality, to Wayland's path. Mir itself survived by conversion: refitted as a library for *building Wayland compositors*, the rival ended as a subject — and the episode entered the commons' textbook as its great cautionary tale about going it alone against a settling standard.

The second quarrel was longer, quieter, and touched sir's own machines more nearly: the matter of NVIDIA. The new world's buffer plumbing — the generic buffer manager just described — was the agreed coin of the realm for every driver in the open stack. NVIDIA, whose proprietary driver we met in the tradesmen's episode as the great peninsula of binary, declined the coin: in twenty sixteen it proposed an alternative buffer mechanism of its own devising, and for five years the two camps stood their ground — most compositors refusing to maintain two parallel plumbing systems for one vendor's convenience, the vendor declining to adopt the commons', and the practical upshot being that the Wayland desktop on the world's most common discrete graphics cards ranged from shaky to unusable. The stalemate broke in the autumn of twenty twenty-one, when NVIDIA's four hundred and ninety-fifth driver release simply adopted the standard mechanism — the quarrel conceded rather than settled, in the fashion sir will remember from the driver wars: the commons does not negotiate, it waits. A residue of glitches remained even then, traceable to a genuinely interesting technical difference — the open drivers synchronise work on the graphics card *implicitly*, behind the scenes, while NVIDIA's expects the parties to exchange explicit completion signals — and the definitive cure ran the other way: in twenty twenty-four the protocol world adopted explicit synchronisation as a first-class interface, the drivers and compositors followed, and the last chronic source of flicker and stutter on those cards drained away. Peace, sir, on the graphics front, roughly sixteen years after the vow was sworn — and note its shape: the commons moved *toward* the technically superior semantics the holdout had wanted, once it could do so on its own constitutional terms.

## Part Five — The Slow Victory, and the Houses That Never Needed It

The adoption ledger, then, kept honestly, because the slowness is the story's substance. Fedora, ever the vanguard estate, made the new session its desktop default in the autumn of twenty sixteen — and for years thereafter "default" carried asterisks: not on NVIDIA, not for this workflow, log out and pick the old session from the little gear on the login screen. Ubuntu tried the switch in twenty seventeen, retreated for its long-term release when the rough edges showed, and returned for good in twenty twenty-two. KDE's sixth Plasma generation arrived with Wayland in front. The professional holdouts came over constituency by constituency as their particular blockers fell — the gamers when the synchronisation peace was signed and a games company built its handheld console's whole interface as a Wayland compositor; the tiling-window-manager faithful once sway, the meticulous Wayland re-creation of their beloved original, matured — and I can date the mood of the final act precisely, sir: this very January, the author of that original tiling manager published his own long migration notes under a title that is a small white flag in interrogative form — *can I finally start using Wayland?* — and his answer, with reservations duly itemised, was yes.

And the old country itself? Red Hat's tenth enterprise release shipped, in twenty twenty-five, with no X server in the box at all — the corporate actuaries, who keep the coldest books in our world, pricing the cathedral's remains at zero. GNOME's forty-ninth release switched its X session off by default with removal declared the destination; Fedora now ships its flagship desktop Wayland-only. The X.Org server as a project stands in avowed maintenance — patched for security, released seldom, its remaining development energy long since emigrated — with one telling exception: XWayland, the embassy, is maintained separately and actively, the bridge outliving the country it leads from. I shall not pronounce X dead, sir; institutions of that size do not die, they *linger* — on the elderly estates, in the laboratories with one irreplaceable instrument console, wherever the cost of moving exceeds the cost of staying. But the succession is no longer contested.

Two witnesses from beyond the hedge close the case, because each shows what the same problem looks like *without* the cathedral. Android, first: born in the same year the vow was sworn, and born composited — from its very first release its screen has been assembled by a compositor of its own, SurfaceFlinger, every application rendering into buffers and one service composing them to glass, which sir will recognise as precisely the Wayland shape, arrived at independently and shipped at planetary scale while the desktop was still arguing. Android never carried X for a day: no legacy, no congregation, no move required — the luxury of being born after the lesson, which sir will recall was Cupertino's luxury too. And ChromeOS, the most austere witness of all: it began its life running X like any other Linux, and in twenty fifteen simply *removed it* — no Wayland either, at first, but a minimal in-house stack by which the browser talks directly to the kernel's mode-setting and rendering interfaces. When one application owns the entire screen for a living, sir, the correct amount of display server is very nearly none: the two ends of the design space — the telephone's compositor-for-many and the appliance's no-server-at-all — bracketing, between them, the desktop's negotiated middle way.

What was truly lost, before the books close, deserves one unsentimental paragraph. Network transparency — the real, load-bearing, eighties article — is gone: the new protocol never pretended to it, the forwarding tools that exist for it are curiosities, and the honest modern answer to pictures-at-a-distance is the purpose-built streaming the portals and PipeWire now broker. Gone too is the universal tinkerer's toolbox that one flat trust domain made possible: the five-line script that watched a window, clicked a button, scraped a screen — any screen, no questions asked. Sir and I may permit ourselves one glass raised to that lawless convenience; the locksmith's verdict, from last week's evening, is nonetheless final on why it had to go. The trade was real: a commons of open doors for a commons of brokered ones, forty years of accumulated habit for four adjectives — no tearing, no lag, no redrawing, no flicker — finally made true.

## The Books of the Evening

The accounts, sir. A vow sworn on a Massachusetts road in two thousand and eight — every frame perfect — by a man who knew the old building from inside its walls; the stewards' verdict that X's faults were premises, not defects, its server latterly a telephone exchange nobody spoke through; the abolition of the middleman — the compositor *becomes* the server — and of the drawing service with it, clients rendering their own finished frames and the compositor composing them atomically on the display's own heartbeat. The constitutional inversion: mechanism and policy reunited under each compositor's roof, isolation by default, everything-permitted exchanged for nothing-permitted-until-granted. The price paid in the open: a decade rebuilding screenshot and screen-share, automation and accessibility, colour and hotkeys as brokered portals through the front desk with PipeWire carrying the stream — the burglar and the screen reader having been, architecturally, the same visitor; XWayland as the embassy that made the move survivable. Below stairs, the debt paid: mode-setting, buffer management and atomic commits taken into the kernel, the display server demoted from root, the vow given mechanical foundations. Two quarrels on the record — the rival that became a subject, and the vendor the commons outwaited until it adopted the coin and then met halfway on synchronisation. And the slow victory: the vanguard in twenty sixteen, the enterprise actuaries by twenty twenty-five, the last constituencies crossing as I record — while the telephone in sir's pocket and the appliance on the café table testify that the destination was never exotic: born-composited was always what the problem wanted; the desktop merely had a cathedral to move first, and moved it without once asking the congregation to leave.

Next week, sir, the season's set-piece of comparative architecture, and a debt that has stood since Episode One falls due at last. The hook is a letter: in twenty seventeen, the professor whose teaching system a young Finn once found too restrictive — and whose famous verdict on that student's monolithic kernel we have carried unpaid for nine episodes — wrote an open letter to Intel, thanking them, with exquisite dryness, for making his little teaching microkernel the most widely deployed operating system on Earth: running, in secret, inside the management engine of very nearly every machine in this room, including the ones running Linux. The microkernel question, paid in full: what Tanenbaum actually argued and what Mach's tragedy actually proved; the message-passing house that shipped in your motor car; the kernel that was mathematically *proved*; NT read fairly; the telephone empires — the C++ house on eight megabytes, the two acts of BlackBerry; Android as the deepest study of a Linux wearing foreign dress; and the houses within houses — virtualisation from trap to hardware, the cloud as a block of flats, and the old joke that a hypervisor is merely a microkernel that shipped. Architecture proposed, economics disposed, sir.

Until then.

*— End of Episode Ten.*
