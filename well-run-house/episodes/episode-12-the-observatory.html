Episode Twelve — The Observatory, and the State of the House

The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux

The Man Who Shouted at the Disks

Good day, sir.

In the last days of two thousand and eight, in a Sun Microsystems datacentre, an engineer named Brendan Gregg stood before a rack of disk arrays with a colleague filming him, leaned in close to the machines, and shouted at them. Not in frustration, sir — that demonstration occurs daily in datacentres worldwide and yields no data. This was an experiment. On the screen beside him ran an analytics instrument his team had built, drawing the latency of every disk operation as a living picture — and as he shouted, the picture shuddered: a spike of slow operations, blooming exactly in time with his voice, as the vibration of a human yell shook the read heads of spinning disks measurably off their tracks. He shouted; the disks flinched; the instrument saw it. The clip went round the world's engineers in a week, and it has never stopped being shown, because it teaches the deepest lesson in our final evening's subject in eleven seconds flat: the machine is a physical object, haunted by physical truths — vibration, heat, contention, queues — and between those truths and your understanding stands only the quality of your instruments. Nobody would have believed the shout mattered. The picture made it undeniable. You cannot manage what you cannot see.

Tonight, sir, the season's last ascent: up past the family floors, past the staff quarters, into the observatory — the instruments by which a running house is seen. It is the capstone evening by design, because every figure this course has quoted — the pilot's load numbers, the memory episode's page counts, the post office's retransmission tallies — was read off an instrument, and tonight we examine the instruments themselves: the counters the kernel was keeping all along, the tracing machinery that can put a stethoscope to any function in the house while it runs, the verified errand-runner from the pilot grown into the great telescope of the age, and the discipline of actually reading what they show. Then, before the tour ends, two closing duties: the state of the house as I record — told honestly, including what the arguments have cost — and the audit of this season's debts, paid and outstanding, down to the last entry.

Part One — The Ledgers Already Kept

Begin with what costs nothing, because it was already paid for. The kernel does not merely do the house's work; it counts it as it goes — every context switch, every page fault, every packet, every disk operation ticking counters in kernel memory — and the proc and sys filesystems — the house's ledger room, as the pilot styled them: filesystems that occupy no disk at all, every file conjured at the moment of reading as a window onto the kernel's live state — publish them as files. Every dashboard tool sir has ever run — the process monitors, the disk and memory reporters — is a modest program reading those files in a loop and doing arithmetic. There is no magic in the observatory's ground floor, and knowing that is itself useful: when a tool's figure looks wrong, sir can go and read the counter it came from.

But counters, sir, are honest witnesses that answer misleading questions, and the observatory's first discipline is knowing the three classic deceits. First: the load average — the most quoted and least understood figure in Unix. It counts, roughly, how many threads want the processor on average — except that Linux, by a patch of nineteen ninety-three whose consequences outlived all its context, also counts the threads in uninterruptible sleep — the state of a task gone below to wait on slow hardware, deaf to every signal until the device answers; the tradesman who never comes, as the season has styled him — so a Linux load average conflates the hungry with the stuck: a machine can post an alarming load while its processors idle, because forty threads are wedged waiting on a dead disk. Second: the average itself. The post office episode taught this once and the observatory repeats it as law — the mean conceals the tail, and the tail is where the users live. A disk whose average read takes one millisecond, with one read in a thousand taking two full seconds, will show a lovely average and a stream of complaints; never accept an average where a distribution can be had. Third: utilisation. Ninety per cent busy sounds like ten per cent headroom, but queueing does not oblige — service times curve viciously upward long before the wall — and "busy" can itself deceive on modern hardware, where a processor stalled waiting on memory still reports itself hard at work; the memory episode's invisible latencies hide inside the very figure that claims to measure activity.

I shall add a fourth deceit, because sir has surely stared at it: the figure the dashboards label iowait. It presents itself as a measurement of disk misery, and administrators have been paging each other over it for thirty years. It is nothing of the kind. It is merely idle time that happens to coincide with an outstanding disk request — the processor twiddling its thumbs while somebody's read is in flight. Add processors and iowait shrinks with the work unchanged, because the idleness spreads; add unrelated computation and it vanishes entirely, the disks as slow as ever, because the processor is no longer idle enough to confess. A figure whose value changes when you observe the machine differently, while the thing it claims to measure stands still, is not a measurement, sir. It is a mood.

Which is why the honest gauge the season has twice promised to revisit earns its place here: pressure-stall information — P-S-I, as the manuals and the metric names abbreviate it — contributed to the kernel at the end of the twenty-tens by an engineer at Facebook, out of the bitter experience of running fleets by deceitful averages. Pressure-stall asks the only question that matters, for each of the three great resources — processor, memory, I/O: what fraction of the time, lately, was somebody stalled for want of this? — with the crucial refinement of two figures each: the share of time when at least some work was stalled, and the share when all of it was — the difference between a house running warm and a house standing still. It is the closest thing the kernel offers to a truthful answer to "how is the machine feeling", it is what the modern desktop's userspace out-of-memory steward watches so it can intervene before the kernel's out-of-memory killer must — the memory episode's last resort, sir remembers, which when memory is truly exhausted selects a resident and shoots it to save the house — and if sir takes one practical instruction from tonight, it is: alert on pressure, not on utilisation.

One ledger more belongs on this floor, kept for testimony rather than tuning, and it pays a note the process episode issued. The family tree, that episode confessed, records parentage, not history: when a process execs — discards its inherited body and takes possession of the program it means to run — the kernel's books keep its number and its parent but replace its name and substance, so the tree tells you who spawned whom and nothing about what anything used to be. The instrument that remembers is the audit subsystem: a journal below stairs, fed by rules sir writes, that records the events he has declared notable — every exec, every change of credentials, every touch of a watched file — each entry stamped with who, what, and when, and handed upstairs to a logging daemon for the permanent record. It answers no performance question at all; it answers the magistrate's — able to testify, weeks later, that this shell became that compiler became that linker, which no reading of the living tree could ever reconstruct.

Part Two — The Stethoscope

Counters tell you that; they cannot tell you why. For why, you must catch the house in the act — and the machinery for that was assembled over two decades, piecewise, in a lineage worth reciting because sir owns all of it.

The audacious ancestor came in the middle two-thousands: kernel probes — kprobes, as the sources and every tracing manual since have written it, one word with the k sounded as its own letter, kay-probes — the ability to set, at runtime, a breakpoint inside any function of the running kernel, no rebuild, no reboot. The mechanism earns one slow beat, because everything later stands on it, and three things figure in it: the chosen function, the trap, and the observer's handler. The kernel lifts one instruction out of the function and sets the trap in its place. The running house, arriving at that spot in the ordinary course of its work, springs the trap. The handler runs and takes its note. Then the borrowed instruction is made good, and the function proceeds, none the wiser — the patient examined without ever being anaesthetised. A later twin, the uprobe — the u for userspace — performs the same trespass above stairs, inside any function of an ordinary running program. Above these grew tracepoints: named, maintained stations at the meaningful junctions — a context switch, a block request issued, a packet queued — stable enough to build tooling upon. Note the two castes, sir, because they echo a constitutional theme from the tradesmen's evening. A tracepoint is a maintained station: placed deliberately, named in the record, kept meaningful across releases — a promise, of the modest kind the kernel is willing to make. A kprobe is trespassing by permission: sir may attach to any function he likes, and the kernel makes no promise whatever that the function will exist next release, bear the same name, or mean the same thing — the no-stable-inner-interfaces bargain applied to observation. The competent observatory uses the stations where they exist and trespasses knowingly where they do not, and its tooling expects, as a fact of climate, to be lightly broken by every kernel upgrade. Then the function tracer, ftrace — one word in the sources, ef-trace to the ear — at the end of the two-thousands, born of the real-time engineers' need to find latency: the ability to record, into a ring buffer in memory, every function call the kernel makes, or any chosen subset, with timings — the house narrating its own actions at microsecond grain. And beside it, the performance tool — perf, arriving at the decade's turn as a door to the processor's own hardware counters and growing into the trade's omnibus profiler. Its master technique is sampling: interrupt the machine some thousands of times a second, note each time exactly where it stood — which function, called from which, called from which — and let statistics do the rest. A few tens of seconds of sampling yields a faithful census of where the processor's time actually went, at a cost too small to notice.

And here the record requires a courtesy toward a rival house, because the observatory's modern shape was not our invention. In the middle two-thousands, Sun's Solaris shipped DTrace — Bryan Cantrill and colleagues — and it was, sir, an epochal humiliation for everyone else: the first instrument that let an engineer ask the whole running system a question phrased as a sentence — every disk read, by size, by process, now — safely, in production, and have it answered in seconds. The shouting video that opened tonight was that lineage in action: the analytics screen that saw the disks flinch was built on DTrace. For a decade, Linux engineers watched Solaris colleagues answer in one line questions that cost us a week of patching and rebooting, and the envy was formative: the pieces just recited — probes, tracepoints, ftrace, perf — were assembled by a community that knew exactly what it was chasing. What it lacked, until the twenty-tens, was the unifying instrument. Then it built one, and overshot.

Part Three — The Great Telescope

Sir met the machinery in the pilot as a curiosity of the modern kernel: the errand notarised at the door — small programs, submitted by userspace, verified by the kernel to be safe and bounded, then run inside the house itself. Its name in every manual is eBPF — a small e, then capital B, P, F, spoken as the letters, e-B-P-F; the expansion, extended Berkeley Packet Filter, is a fossil of its first career filtering network traffic, and the trade long ago stopped expanding it. The season has since shown it working the post office and standing guard with the burglar alarms. But eBPF's third career, observability, is its greatest, and here is the working depth I promised.

The design's stroke is where the arithmetic happens, and the cast is small enough to set out before anything moves: a station — any tracepoint, any kprobe, any function entry; a little verified eBPF program, attached to that station; a map — the program's ledger in kernel memory, a table of counts or a histogram of buckets; and, upstairs, the engineer who will eventually ask to see it. Now follow one event through. A disk read completes; the station fires. The attached program runs on the spot, below stairs, and does one small sum — drops that read's latency into the right bucket of its histogram — and is finished. Nothing was reported. Nothing crossed to userspace. The event came and went, and all that remains of it is a single increment in a ledger. Let that happen millions of times a second, if that is what the house does: the cost is an addition per event, paid where the event happened. Only when the engineer upstairs asks does anything cross the boundary, and what crosses is the summary — the finished histogram, never the flood that built it. That single decision — count below stairs, read above — is what makes it affordable to watch everything, always, even in production at full service. The one-line tracing language built atop it — bpftrace, of the late twenty-tens: the letters, then the word, b-p-f-trace — turned the capability into questions a tired engineer can type at a prompt. Hear a few, sir, spoken as what they are: show me every file opened, by which process, as it happens. Draw me the full latency distribution of every disk read on this machine — the histogram, not the average — live, in the terminal. Count the TCP retransmissions by destination. Tell me where threads go when they block — sum the time this application spent waiting, by the exact path that led it to wait. That last one deserves its title: off-CPU analysis, the profiling of waiting — and since the concurrency episode taught sir that a modern program's life is mostly waiting, a profiler that sees only running threads sees only half the patient. The great telescope sees both halves.

And pause on why any of this is sane to do against a production machine, because the answer is the pilot's notary earning his keep. Every one of those little programs passes the verifier before it may attach: proven to terminate — no unbounded loops below stairs; proven to touch only its own maps and the context handed to it; forbidden the blunders that would let an instrument wound the patient. The house will refuse an unsafe question rather than risk answering it, which is precisely what makes the safe questions free to ask at any hour. And the reach extends above stairs, too: applications and runtimes carry their own deliberate probe stations — user-level statically defined tracing, the manuals call the arrangement, U-S-D-T for short: tracepoints above stairs in all but name — the database announcing query begins and query ends, the language runtime announcing its garbage collection — so one instrument can draw a single causal thread from the application's sentence, through the bell rope — the system call, that is: the one door by which any program asks its kernel for any service — to the block layer's queue and back. On this whole apparatus now stands the era's practice of continuous profiling — the fleet sampled always, at trivial cost, so that when the three-in-the-morning question arrives, the answer was already being recorded while everyone slept. On this machinery now stands a whole industry — the continuous profilers, the cloud's tracing agents, the security sensors of the burglars' episode — all of them, underneath, verified errands aggregating below stairs. The instrument DTrace made us envy, sir, we eventually built bigger than the original.

Part Four — Reading the Smoke

One picture organises everything the telescope collects, and since sir will meet it within a week of typing any of tonight's questions, let me teach it aloud. The flame graph — invented by the same Brendan Gregg who shouted at the disks, in twenty eleven, reportedly out of desperation before a wall of profiler text. Take those thousands of sampled call stacks; merge every identical stack; draw the result as a mountain range: width is time — the wider a block, the more samples landed there — and height is depth, each block sitting atop the function that called it. Read it so: the base is the program entire; ridges rising from it are the major activities; and any wide plateau at the top of a ridge is a function personally consuming time in bulk — the place to look. Two cautions make sir a competent reader at once. The horizontal axis is not chronology — stacks are sorted, not sequenced; nothing about left means earlier. And colour, in the classic rendering, is decoration — warmth, not meaning. The variants complete the kit: mixed-mode graphs stacking kernel frames above user frames, so sir can watch a database's innocent read call broaden below stairs into filesystem, block layer and driver; and the off-CPU flame graph, the same picture drawn for waiting, which with its partner covers the whole of a thread's existence.

Let me read one aloud, so sir has heard it done. A web service is slow; the graph is drawn. The base block, full width: the server process entire. Rising from it, three ridges. The leftmost, two-thirds of the width, climbs through the request handler into — of all things — the compression library, terminating in a vast plateau in one hashing routine: two-thirds of every processor-second is being spent compressing responses, and the plateau's name has already told us the likely story, an over-eager compression level set by some well-meaning hand. The middle ridge, a quarter wide, climbs through serialisation — the ordinary cost of speaking to the world. And at the right, a narrow spire drops below stairs into the kernel's send path — thin, sir, which is the quiet good news: the post office is blameless. Thirty seconds of sampling, one picture, and the investigation is over before the second cup of tea; the fix is one line in a configuration file, and the proof of the fix is the same graph drawn tomorrow, with the mountain gone.

And with that, the observatory's practical liturgy — what to actually do, on sir's own machines, in the order that respects the evening's lessons. Begin with pressure: three gauges, one question — is anyone stalled, and for what. If pressure points at the processor, sample and draw the flame graph; the plateau confesses. If it points at I/O or lock-shaped misery, profile the waiting instead — off-CPU, latency histograms, never averages. Check saturation before utilisation for each resource in turn — the queue, not the busyness, is the harbinger. And the discipline that outranks every instrument: measure before, measure after, same instrument, same load — for the observatory's final deceit is the engineer who, having changed something, sees the improvement he came to see. The counters keep no such faith. That is their charm, sir. They are the one member of the household with no interest whatever in sir's feelings.

Part Five — The State of the House

The instruments stand described; now point them at the house itself, as I record, in this year of twenty twenty-six, and report honestly.

The great argument of the decade is over language, and it has been settled at real human cost, so I shall tell it properly. The case: the burglars' episode showed sir the forty years' war of memory corruption, and the plain statistic that the majority of the kernel's serious vulnerabilities have always been memory-safety blunders — the very class of error that a language like Rust makes structurally inexpressible. The kernel took its first Rust toehold at the end of twenty twenty-two, and there followed not a technical failure but a social one: the argument over whether a second language belonged in the house at all ran hot for years, and in August of twenty twenty-four the engineer who had led the effort for nearly four years, Wedson Almeida Filho, resigned it with a letter whose weary candour belongs in the season's record — he found himself, he wrote, lacking the energy to keep answering "some of the nontechnical nonsense", and left behind a warning delivered without heat: he believed the future of kernels lay with memory-safe languages, and if Linux did not internalise this, some other kernel would do to it what it had done to Unix. Sir should hear that sentence twice. It is the genesis episode's lesson — the incumbent unseated by the thing it dismissed — aimed back at the victor by one of its own. And yet the settlement, when it came, came decisively: by the end of last year the "experiment" was formally concluded as permanent; the binder driver — the spine of Android, sir remembers, the busiest single piece of inter-process machinery on Earth — was rewritten in Rust and merged; a new-generation graphics driver effort proceeds in Rust with the vendor's blessing; and this very July the kernel's stable maintainer declared in public that for the graphics subsystem, new drivers will be accepted in Rust only. The house is learning a second language in its old age, sir — grudgingly, expensively, at the cost of good people burnt through — and it is learning it because the alternative was explained by its own founding story.

The rest of the estate, in brief, as the instruments find it. The asynchronous ring the pilot introduced as a promising novelty — the io uring, to give it its proper name: in the sources one word, the letters i and o, an underscore where a space would be, then uring; say eye-oh-you-ring and any engineer of the era will know the word — has grown up entirely. The substance bears restating in a sentence: a pair of queues shared in memory between a program and the kernel, requests posted on one ring and completions collected from the other, so that work crosses the boundary in batches — the bell rope pulled once for many errands or, on a busy machine, hardly at all. No longer a specialist's door but a first-class path through the house for storage and increasingly for the network, its repertoire now covers the batching, buffer-lending and zero-copy tricks that let one thread drive work that once took fifty — the bell rope's cost, engineered away wholesale for those who ask. Live patching is a decade routine: function-sized repairs threaded into a running kernel — riding, sir will be pleased to hear, the same ftrace machinery from Part Two, the stethoscope doubling as a scalpel — so the security fix lands without the reboot; the estates that advertise years of uptime do so on this, though the honest engineer notes it patches functions, not architectures. The houses within houses have grown safes: confidential computing — the guest's memory encrypted by the silicon against the very landlord, both chipmakers' schemes now mainline, this spring's long-term enterprise releases integrating host and guest alike — inverts the cloud's oldest bargain, sir: the tenant need no longer trust the innkeeper, and the block of flats episode's one nagging asterisk is being engineered away. The init wars are simply over: systemd administers its settled empire so quietly that sir's younger colleagues would need the pilot's history lesson to know there had been a war. And the estate's newest fashion institutionalises a lesson sir's own trade learnt in tears: the immutable distributions — the atomic desktops, the gaming handheld's sealed system, their enterprise cousins — deliver the house as a read-only image, updates applied as transactions that either complete or roll back, configuration drift abolished by construction. The filesystem episode's copy-on-write snapshots, the container episode's images, the observatory's before-and-after discipline — assembled into a way of running the whole house.

Two honest clouds, to close the survey, because the observatory does not edit. The first is demographic: the kernel's founding generation is in its fifties and sixties; maintainer burnout is no longer anecdote but a documented condition of the commons — the tradesmen's and burglars' episodes each showed sir a corner of what the load does to volunteers — and while the customary answer to "who after Linus" has long been the stable maintainer's name, the truer answer is the one this season has been teaching throughout: the process is the heir — the tree, the lieutenants, the no-regressions covenant, the institutions that survive their gardeners. The version number, incidentally, has just this season rolled toward its seventh series, and I report with affection that it means, by its author's own long-standing insistence, nothing at all: the odometer turns; the house drives on. The second cloud is the next decade's quarrels, whose shapes are already visible from the observatory: how far the second language spreads from drivers toward the core; how the scheduler's new pluggability — sched ext, the sources write it, an underscore joining the halves: scheduling policy loaded as verified eBPF programs, the notarised-errand machinery once more — changes who gets to have opinions about fairness; and how a kernel built for general-purpose houses serves an industry presently rebuilding itself around single-purpose cathedrals of accelerators. The instruments are pointed, sir. We shall read them as they fill.

The Books of the Season

And so, the final accounts — the season's debts, audited aloud, as promised in the first evening. Episode One issued the great one: the microkernel question, the professor's doctrine deferred — paid in full in Episode Eleven, in the letter of thanks, the car, the radio, the proof, and the hypervisor that shipped. The tradesmen's episode asked what the wall between house and hardware must cost, and the burglars' episode paid the pilot's trusting-trust note with the xz affair. The drawing room issued two — Wayland, and the mode-setting below stairs — both paid in Episode Ten, on the road through the town whose name the successor took. The counters and gauges promised since the pilot were redeemed tonight in the observatory. I have walked the ledger twice, sir, and one entry only remains open: the promise, made at the very close of the pilot, of a proper operating systems course — the scheduler derived rather than described, the memory manager taken apart with problems to work, the whole edifice done with the rigour this season deliberately traded for evenings of good company. That note stands, undated, redeemable whenever sir says the word. A well-run house keeps its books; it does not press its creditors.

What was this season, in the end? Thirteen evenings, sir, and one argument: that the machine sir has trusted for twenty-five years is not a monolith of mystery but a household — staff below stairs with names and duties, family above with rights and habits, a bell rope between them, ledgers in every room — and that nothing in it is beyond a working understanding, because all of it was built by people, arguing, in public, leaving their reasons in the record. We watched the house get built by accident and argument; met the processes and their sorrows; took the ledger of memory apart; walked the library, the post office, the tradesmen's entrance; changed the locks; sat in the drawing room while the pictures learned to be perfect; toured every rival house on the street and the flats within; and tonight, climbed to the observatory and found that the house had been keeping honest books on itself all along, waiting only to be asked.

So: one last time, ring the bell. A single keystroke, sir, at a prompt. The keyboard's controller raises an interrupt; the staff take it at the door, top half brisk, bottom half unhurried; the character climbs through the terminal's machinery into the compositor's next perfect frame; the shell, woken by the scheduler's fair arithmetic, forks — copy-on-write, the pages shared until someone tells the truth differently — and becomes the command; the loader walks its libraries; the page cache leans in helpfully with what it read for someone else last Tuesday; perhaps a socket opens and the post office's windows and restraints see a packet across the world and back; the exit status is reaped — reaped, sir, the term of art, this course would never say otherwise — and the prompt returns, the whole staff having served and cleared in less time than the eye's blink it decorates. And somewhere below stairs, without being asked, a counter increments — in case sir should ever care to know.

It has been an honour to keep the house open late for sir, these thirteen evenings. The staff are at their stations; the ledgers balance; the bell answers. The house, sir, is well run.

Good night.

— End of Episode Twelve.
