# Episode Seven — The Tradesmen's Entrance

*The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux*

## The Most Expensive Parser in History

Good day, sir.

At just past four in the morning, universal time, on Friday the nineteenth of July, twenty twenty-four, a security company named CrowdStrike published a routine content update to its flagship product — an endpoint sensor called Falcon, installed on a considerable fraction of the world's serious Windows fleets: airlines, hospitals, banks, broadcasters, governments. The update was not a program. It was a data file — one of the product's so-called channel files, a bundle of threat-detection configuration of a kind the company shipped several times a day, every day, worldwide, without incident and largely without ceremony.

Within the hour, roughly eight and a half million Windows machines — Microsoft's own later estimate — were in a state the trade calls a boot loop, and civilians call *the blue screen, again, forever*. Aircraft boarding systems died with passengers in the queues; airlines grounded fleets outright. Emergency call centres fell back to paper. Broadcasters went dark mid-programme. Supermarket tills, hospital scheduling, trading floors: down, worldwide, simultaneously — and, the detail that gives the affair its permanent place in the syllabus, *down in a way that no remote fix could touch*. The machines could not stay up long enough to receive a repair.

The mechanics, per the company's own root-cause analysis, are almost insultingly small — the season has met this shape before, at the leap second, and will again. The Falcon sensor runs, necessarily by its own design, *inside the Windows kernel* — below stairs, in the pilot's terms, with the staff's full keys. Within it, an interpreter reads those channel files and configures detection logic. The fatal update delivered a file whose contents the interpreter's code did not expect — a mismatch, in the fine print, between the number of data fields the file supplied and the number the code assumed — and the interpreter, trusting its input, read past the end of what it had been given. An out-of-bounds read: in an ordinary program, a crash and an obituary in a log. In the kernel — and here the whole pilot cashes in at once — there is no such thing as a small crash. The privileged world has no floor beneath it; a fault below stairs is a fault of the *house*; Windows did the only safe thing a kernel can do with a corrupted self, and stopped. And since the sensor loads at boot — early, deliberately, so that malware cannot beat it to the ground floor — each restart re-read the same poisoned file and died at the same threshold: a loop, requiring hands on each machine — safe mode, by keyboard, often gated by disk-encryption recovery keys that the afflicted organisations kept, naturally, on systems that were also down. Eight and a half million machines, cured one at a time, by hand, over days.

Now, the ironies, which are the syllabus. This was not malware; it was the *anti*-malware. The sensor's driver was signed and certified through Microsoft's own programme — but certification had examined the *code*, once, at leisure, while the *data* that drove the code shipped several times daily through a lighter channel, and — per the company's own account — a bug in its content validator let the malformed file through. The code was notarised; the code's *diet* was not; and a certified interpreter fed a poisoned file is simply a poison delivery mechanism with a certificate. Every element of the catastrophe — why the sensor must live below stairs, why below stairs has no floor, why boot-time loading turned a crash into a loop, what certification does and does not cover, and what the alternatives are — is the subject of tonight's episode, which is about the oldest and least resolved question in the house's constitution: *who is allowed below stairs, on whose word, and what happens when a tradesman falls through the floor he is standing on.*

For drivers — and this is the fact that reframes the whole topic — are not a detail of the kernel. They are its *bulk*. By any honest count, the overwhelming majority of the kernel's code is drivers: the handlers for every network card, disk controller, sensor, bus, and blinking light ever manufactured. The core we have toured all season — scheduler, memory, filesystems, network — is the minority; the kernel is chiefly a vast diplomatic corps for hardware, written substantially by the hardware's own vendors, running with total privilege. Tonight: how the corps is organised — the device model, and how hardware is even *described* to software; how the house searches tradesmen at the door — the interrupt and memory disciplines that contain them; then the great constitutional divergence, the deepest three-way split in the industry — our house's refusal to promise its drivers anything, Windows' binding contract with its drivers, and Apple's decade-long expulsion of drivers from the kernel altogether; and finally the movement visible from all three houses at once, hastened by that Friday in July: the long migration of the tradesmen out of the basement entirely.

In by the side door, sir.

## Part One — The Corps, and the Map of the Estate

Begin with a fact of scale, then the structure it forces. Thousands of distinct devices; hundreds of new ones monthly; one kernel. Anarchy is prevented by a device model with four ranks, and sir has been reading its paperwork for years in the visible skeleton of *sysfs* — the sys filesystem, its name written as one word in the manuals — where the kernel publishes the model's ledgers as browsable files. A *bus* is a way of attaching and addressing hardware — the PCI Express lattice in sir's servers, the USB tree at his desk, the little two-wire buses inside every laptop, which the trade calls *I-squared-C* — the name compressing *inter-integrated circuit*. A *device* is a thing found on a bus. A *driver* is code declaring itself competent for certain devices. And a *class* is what the thing is *for* — network interface, block device, input, display — the rank that lets the rest of the house address a disk as a disk regardless of whose disk. The choreography between them is the *probe*, and since three parties move in it, let me seat the cast before anything moves. There is the bus's own controller driver — the tradesman responsible for the bus itself. There is the device, newly present and so far anonymous. And there is the candidate driver, which has already lodged its declaration of competence with the model's registry: this vendor, that device — mine.

Now the moves, one at a time. First, the controller driver enumerates: it walks its bus and takes the census of what is physically present — reading down the PCI configuration space, interrogating the USB tree. Second, for each discovery, the device model consults the registry of declarations and finds the driver whose declaration matches. Third, the model performs the introduction: it calls that driver's *probe* routine, handing it the device. And fourth, the probe routine either declines or claims — claiming means taking the hardware, allocating its private state, and registering the device's class-appropriate face with the rest of the house. That final registration is the moment a lump of silicon becomes a citizen: a network interface the network stack can use, a disk the block layer — Episode Five's freight bureau, to keep the image beside the name — can queue against.

The model's above-stairs herald completes the picture. When a probe succeeds, the kernel announces the arrival, and a small above-stairs service named *udev* — the pilot met it in passing — hears the announcement and manufactures the *device node*: the file-shaped handle through which ordinary programs address the hardware as though it were a file. Each node belongs to one of two ancient castes — the *character device*, read and written as a stream of bytes, the terminal and the mouse and the null device — the file that discards all writes — among them, or the *block device*, storage addressed in fixed-size blocks — and udev lays stable names beside the unstable ones, so that the disk sir scripted against last week answers to the same name after tonight's reshuffle. That is the herald's office; tonight you have the below-stairs half.

But how does the house know what hardware *exists* at all? Two answers, sir, split by continent of computing, and the split explains a decade of embedded-Linux folklore. On the PC and the server, the firmware tells: the *ACPI* tables — the advanced configuration and power interface, to unfold the acronym: that vast, byzantine estate map bequeathed by the firmware at boot — describe the motherboard's population, its interrupts, its power wiring; the kernel reads the map and trusts it, more or less, with a long tradition of vendor-specific grumbling about maps that lie. But on the embedded world's boards — the phone, the router, the single-board computer in sir's drawer — there is classically no such firmware cartographer; the hardware is soldered, unenumerable, and *mute*: nothing on a bare system-on-chip announces that a serial port sleeps at such-and-such an address. There, the map is supplied by hand: the *device tree*, a small structured document — compiled, shipped alongside the kernel, passed in by the bootloader — declaring, node by node: here is a UART, here its address, here its interrupt, here its clocks. The same kernel binary, handed different trees, drives different boards. And the reason a cheap single-board computer's obscure peripheral so often "doesn't work on Linux" is now sayable precisely: its device tree lacks a node, or its node names a driver nobody wrote — the map and the corps, sir, are two different scarcities, and embedded bring-up is the craft of curing both by hand.

One more piece of estate machinery, because it governs sir's laptop battery and the cloud's electricity bill alike: the corps does not merely start devices; it *ushers them through states*. The model owns power management: suspend walks the model's whole tree of devices in dependency order, asking each driver to put its charge to sleep — children before parents, the USB disk before the USB controller — and resume walks it back. Runtime power management does the same continuously and per-device: the idle network card droops into low-power states between packets, the storage spins down, each transition brokered by the same model. When suspend "doesn't work on Linux" — the lid closed, the machine hot in the bag, the ancient laptop grievance — the failure is almost always one tradesman in that long procession declining to sleep or to wake, and the debugging of it is the procession walked with a torch.

## Part Two — Searched at the Door

A driver's business is moving data, and the house long ago learned not to carry it by hand. Two disciplines govern the carrying — the interrupt, and direct memory access — and each deserves its full statement here, because both were toured at speed earlier in the season.

The *interrupt* first. It is the device's tap on the processor's shoulder: whatever was running is suspended on the instant, and the driver's *interrupt handler* runs at once, in a context so restrictive that very little is safe to do there. The discipline, therefore — the pilot taught it, and Episode Three refined it — is brevity in two halves. The handler proper — the *top half*, in the trade's old dialect — does only what cannot wait: silence the device, note what arrived. Everything else is deferred to the *bottom half* — work the kernel runs a moment later, in less hazardous circumstances. And the modern refinement, the *threaded interrupt*, goes one step further: the deferred portion runs as an ordinary schedulable kernel thread, billed and prioritised like any other resident. Announce, defer, resume — that is the whole liturgy.

Tonight's completion concerns the tap itself. The modern interrupt is not a shared physical wire but a *message*: the device writes to a designated memory address, and the interrupt controller turns that write into the processor's interruption — *message-signalled interrupts*, in the trade; MSI on the datasheets. And because a message needs no scarce physical pin, a modern card can have not one interrupt but dozens — one per queue, each steerable to a chosen processor. Episode Six's dividend follows directly: a network card's traffic spread across many receive queues, one queue per processor core, each with its own interrupt, so that no single core funnels the whole flood.

The data itself moves by *direct memory access* — DMA, as every datasheet and driver manual abbreviates it: the driver hands the device an address, and the device reads or writes memory itself, no processor in the loop — the only arrangement that scales, and, stated plainly, a standing invitation to catastrophe. A device with unsupervised memory access is a resident with a master key: a buggy card scribbles over the kernel; a *malicious* device — and here the trade grew up sharply in the past decade — reads the whole house. The demonstrations are public and famous: plug a crafted peripheral into a laptop's high-speed port — the very ports that are, electrically, naked extensions of the internal lattice — and harvest memory, keys, sessions from a locked machine.

The answer is the estate's customs house: the *input-output memory management unit* — the IOMMU — which does for devices exactly what the *memory management unit*, the pilot's paging hardware, does for processes: every address a program utters is translated through tables the kernel keeps, so that a process can touch nothing its tables do not grant. The same office, then, for the tradesmen. Every device's memory access is translated and *policed* through tables the kernel owns: the driver grants a mapping for precisely the buffer intended, the device sees only addresses within its grant, and a stray or hostile access faults instead of landing. The pilot's great lesson — that the kernel owns the tables, so the kernel owns the truth — extended to every tradesman's hands; and the modern laptop ships with the customs house standing guard over the naked ports by default, which is why the plug-in memory-harvest demonstrations of the middle twenty-tens now mostly fail against current machines. The same customs house, at server scale, is what makes it *safe* to hand an entire device to a guest — the virtualisation pass-through of Episode Eleven — or to a userspace driver, a door tonight's finale walks through. Hold the shape, sir: the IOMMU is the precondition for every scheme that moves drivers *out* of the basement, because it is what lets the house give a tradesman real tools without giving him the master key.

## Part Three — Papers, Please

Between the door and the constitutions lies the paperwork, and three items of it demystify a decade of messages sir has half-read in his boot logs.

The first: the module. The kernel's drivers need not all ride in the boot image — the pilot's initramfs story turned on exactly this, the *initramfs* being the small starter filesystem the bootloader hands the kernel, carrying precisely the modules needed to reach the real disk before the real disk can be read — and the *loadable module* is the mechanism: a fragment of kernel, compiled against one precise kernel version's internal interfaces, linked into the living house on demand. Note the compiled-against clause, for it is the great refusal — Part Four's constitutional doctrine, the kernel's standing promise of *no* stable internal interface to its drivers — wearing work clothes: a module is married to the interfaces of its build, whence the version-magic rejections of folklore, the distribution's automatic rebuild machinery for out-of-tree tenants, and the entire cottage industry that exists solely to re-glue such tenants to each new release.

The second: the *taint*. Sir has surely seen the word — a boot log or crash report announcing the kernel is tainted, which sounds like an accusation and is in fact bookkeeping. Loading a proprietary module taints; loading an unsigned one taints; certain forced operations and certain hardware misfortunes taint; each cause with its own flag, all recorded in a register the crash machinery prints. The purposes are two, both unsentimental. Diagnosis: a crash report bearing the proprietary-module flag names a suspect the community *cannot* investigate — the books below stairs include pages nobody outside one vendor may read — and the kernel's developers, as a matter of long doctrine, decline to chase ghosts through rooms they are barred from; the taint is triage. And testimony: the register is the house's honest declaration of its own state — this kernel is no longer entirely the community's code, and every analysis of it starts from that fact. It is not punishment; it is the chain of custody, kept scrupulously, for a machine assembled from parties who do not all trust one another.

The third: the *firmware*. A modern device is a computer wearing a costume — the network card, the wireless radio, the GPU each run their own substantial software on their own processors — and much of what the trade calls a driver is, in truth, a chauffeur for a *blob*: an opaque vendor-built firmware image the kernel fetches from disk and pours into the device at probe time. The arrangement is the industry's standing compromise, and both halves deserve stating. It is how the great refusal coexists with vendor secrecy — the secrets migrate *into the device*, the kernel-side driver thins toward openable transport, which is precisely the shape of NVIDIA's recent conversion: the intelligence withdrew into a firmware processor, and the kernel module left behind could at last be opened. And it is a standing epistemic hole: the blobs are unauditable, updated on vendor schedules, running with a device's full hardware powers — the customs house of Part Two, one must note, being the reason this is survivable at all. The season's secret-computers theme, sir — the pilot's footnote on the management engine, that small perpetual computer living in the chipset below even the kernel's floor, and Episode Eleven's coming punchline — begins here, at the boot log's innocent line about loading firmware.

And stitching the three together, the modern gate: with Secure Boot attesting the chain from firmware to kernel — each stage of the boot verifying the signature of the next before handing over, firmware to bootloader to kernel, as the pilot's second part walked through — the kernel can be asked to extend the discipline inward: modules must be *signed* by a trusted key or be refused, and a lockdown regime closes the classical back doors by which root could historically rewrite the kernel's memory and make mockery of the whole procession. The distributions wire this to the firmware's word: Secure Boot on, lockdown on, unsigned tenants barred. Which is the true text beneath the second-most-cursed experience in modern Linux administration — the freshly installed machine whose graphics or wireless module *will not load*: not hostility, but the door policy working exactly as designed, the unsigned tradesman stopped at the threshold until the owner either signs his papers (the enrolment ceremony the distribution walks one through) or, with open eyes, stands the guard down. The house, in other words, now runs the full bureaucracy: identity papers, a criminal register, declared cargo, and a border. What it refuses — still, constitutionally, uniquely among the three houses — is to promise the tradesmen that the corridors will not move.

## Part Four — Three Constitutions

Now the deep divergence, and I shall state the three houses' positions as the formal doctrines they are, because each is internally coherent, each has a body count, and the CrowdStrike affair is what one of them looks like when its bill arrives.

Our house's doctrine is the *great refusal*, and it is written down — in a document that has lived in the kernel's own tree for two decades under the forthright title "Stable API Nonsense". The kernel promises its userspace everything: the *system-call* contract — the bell rope, in the house's figure: the fixed menu of services by which any program asks the kernel for anything — is sacred, and the season has repeated the vow — never break userspace — episode after episode. To its *drivers*, the kernel promises *nothing*. The internal interfaces a driver is written against — the locking rules, the memory helpers, the very structures — may change in any release, without notice, without apology. A binary driver compiled today may not load next quarter. This sounds, to every arriving Windows engineer, like barbarism; it is in fact a bargain with teeth, and its terms are: *bring your driver into the tree.* Merge it into the kernel's own source, under its licence, and the community assumes the maintenance: whoever changes an internal interface must fix *every* in-tree user of it, your driver included — refactors carry you along; the corps is renovated as one estate. Ten thousand devices' drivers ride each release's internal upheavals at no cost to their long-dead authors — the only regime under which forty years of hardware could remain alive in one codebase. The doctrine's true purposes are two, and candour requires both: engineering freedom — the internals can be *improved*, forever, unfrozen by any binary's expectations — and *leverage*: the refusal makes the out-of-tree binary driver a perpetually expensive tenancy, and that expense is the pressure that has, over decades, pushed vendor after vendor to open their code and come inside. The freedom clause you have already watched exercised: it is precisely how the block layer — Episode Five's freight bureau — tore out its elevator, the old single-queue request machinery at its heart, and rebuilt itself multi-queue in place, no driver's permission asked because none was owed.

The doctrine's great holdout, and the exception that measured the rule, was of course NVIDIA — a generation of proprietary graphics modules glued to each kernel release by a shim, the community's compatibility labour institutionalised in the distributions' build machinery, Torvalds's on-camera opinion of the arrangement expressed in a gesture that remains the most-cited frame in kernel history. That the holdout finally bent — the company's kernel-side modules opened this decade, its firmware absorbing the secrets, the community's own replacement drivers maturing alongside — is the leverage clause cashing out on its longest and most stubborn account, and Episodes Nine and Ten will meet the graphics-specific consequences.

Windows' doctrine is the *contract*. NT publishes a stable driver interface — frameworks, versioned, documented, honoured across releases for years — and any vendor may compile a binary driver against it and ship that binary to a billion strangers. It is the doctrine demanded by Windows' whole economic position: an operating system sold to run other people's hardware, sight unseen, with drivers on discs in shrink-wrapped boxes the vendor and Microsoft may never discuss. And its historical bill is instructive: when the contract is the only gate, the gate's quality *is* the system's quality — the blue screens of the nineties and early two-thousands were, by Microsoft's own accounting, overwhelmingly third-party drivers exercising their contractual right to run below stairs badly. The remedies built since are the case study in governing tradesmen you cannot read: mandatory signing; the certification laboratories; a verifier that runs drivers under deliberate stress; crash telemetry that names the guilty module fleet-wide, converting embarrassment into vendor discipline. It worked, mostly — modern Windows is stable in a way its ancestors were not — and then the CrowdStrike affair demonstrated the residual theorem with eight and a half million proofs: certify the code however you like; a certified interpreter below stairs is exactly as safe as the uncertified data you feed it.

And Apple's doctrine is the *expulsion*. macOS spent its first two decades accepting kernel extensions in the Unix manner; it has spent its latest one systematically evicting them. The kernel extension interfaces were deprecated wholesale; their replacement — DriverKit and the system-extension regime — moves drivers *upstairs*: a driver is now an ordinary sandboxed userspace process, granted narrow capabilities to its device, its crash an obituary rather than a house fire. On Apple silicon the eviction is near-total, enforced by making legacy kernel extensions a security downgrade requiring the owner's deliberate ceremony. The doctrine's price is candidly paid: performance tolls at the boundary, a generation of vendor tooling rewritten, whole categories of product — the FUSE arrangement of Episode Five, a filesystem run as an ordinary above-stairs program, among them — living in permanent friction; and the doctrine's purchase is that the tradesman *cannot* burn the house down, because he is never below stairs at all. It is Tanenbaum's *microkernel* doctrine, sir — the professor's old position that a kernel should keep only the irreducible minimum below stairs, drivers and services run as ordinary protected processes above — executed not by a research community arguing on its merits, but by the one company with a fleet dictatorship sufficient to decree it; Episode Eleven will formally note that the professor's clearest victory in the consumer world shipped from Cupertino without a word of credit.

Three constitutions, then: communal maintenance inside the walls; binding contracts across them; expulsion beyond them. Our house refuses the contract to keep its freedom and gain its leverage; Windows sells the contract because its economy demands it; Apple abolished the question because it could. And the season's refrain — architecture proposed, economics disposed — has rarely had a cleaner exhibit: the three engineering positions are, one for one, the three business models wearing constitutional dress.

## Part Five — The Long Migration Upstairs

Which brings us to the movement all three houses now share, and the affair of that July Friday hastened: the tradesmen, by every door available, are moving out of the basement.

Our house's doors, in order of ceremony. The humblest has stood open for decades: whole classes of device — the desk's USB oddments chief among them — driven from userspace through the generic USB conduit — the *libusb* arrangement, to give the door its usual name — the kernel supplying transport and the vendor's above-stairs library supplying the intelligence; sir's badge reader and label printer live here. The middle door is the IOMMU cashing the customs house's cheque: the VFIO framework hands an *entire device* — safely, IOMMU-policed — to a userspace program or a virtual machine; the kernel-bypass networking of Episode Six — the apostates who take the card away from the kernel entirely and drive it from a userspace program, shaving the boundary's cost — enters here, and so does every cloud guest with a real network card's virtual slice in its hands. The grandest door is the pilot's own closing exhibit: the notarised errand — *eBPF*, the letters once standing for extended Berkeley packet filter, a name the mechanism has long outgrown: small programs loaded into the running kernel itself, which by now the season has met as tracer, packet policy, and system-call filter — and which tonight stands revealed in its constitutional role: *the third way between the three doctrines* — code below stairs, but code the house has machine-checked before admitting; neither trusted contractually like a Windows driver nor exiled like an Apple one, but *verified*, the only regime of the three in which the tradesman's every possible step is proved bounded before he takes the first.

And the graphics card — the estate's strangest tenant and the next two episodes' host — has quietly become the migration's largest settlement. The modern GPU driver is, by bulk, a userspace institution: the kernel side confines itself to *kernel mode-setting* — the programming of the display hardware's resolution and timing, a term to hold for Episode Ten — memory management, and submission — gatekeeping, in a word — while the compilers that translate every game's and browser's drawing into the card's own instruction set live entirely upstairs, in Mesa, updated like any library, crashing like any library: an obituary, not a fire. The heaviest computational machinery ever attached to a personal computer is driven, in our house, chiefly from above stairs already — a fact to carry into the drawing-room episodes.

As for Windows: the post-mortem politics of the July affair ran exactly along tonight's lines. The company convened its security vendors; the published direction — arriving, as I record, in preview form — is a Windows endpoint-security platform *outside* the kernel: sanctioned interfaces for the watchers, so that the next poisoned diet kills a process and not a planet. The vendors' Linux sensors, meanwhile — CrowdStrike's own included — increasingly ride the verified door, eBPF programs the kernel checks before loading. And the affair's finest footnote belongs to that same season: the very same vendor's *Linux* sensor had, weeks earlier, been crashing certain distributions' kernels too — in the modes of its operation that ran as a classical kernel module, the old constitution — a fleet nuisance handled quietly and remembered by almost nobody. The trade's best-known performance engineer drew the constitutional moral in a widely read essay whose title promised, in effect, no more blue Fridays: in the verified mode, the equivalent failure is a program the kernel *declines to load* — a fallen agent and a log line, not a loop at the airport — because the verifier's whole office is to refuse below-stairs admission to any errand it cannot prove bounded. The same industry, the same mission, the two constitutions running side by side in the same product line, billed at their true prices within weeks of each other. No cleaner controlled experiment will ever be run.

## The Books of the Evening

The accounts, sir. The kernel is chiefly its diplomatic corps: buses, devices, classes, and drivers introduced by probe, mapped by firmware's ACPI estate plans or the embedded world's hand-drawn device trees, ushered through their power states in procession. The tradesmen are searched at the door: interrupts as messages, many per device, answered in a brief top half and a deferred bottom half; memory access brokered by the IOMMU's customs house, which is both the cure for the crafted-peripheral burglaries and the precondition for every scheme that arms a tradesman without the master key. The three constitutions divide the industry: our refusal (nothing promised to drivers, everything to userspace — the in-tree bargain, renovation as one estate, leverage that finally bent even NVIDIA); Windows' contract (stable interfaces, certification, telemetry — and the July demonstration that certified code fed uncertified data is uncertified in effect); Apple's expulsion (drivers as sandboxed citizens, the microkernel doctrine imposed by fleet decree). And through all three, one migration: upstairs — by USB conduit and VFIO's escorted door, by Mesa's compiler estate, by Windows' post-July platform, and above all by the verified door, where the house proves the errand bounded before the tradesman crosses the threshold.

The trusting-trust debt from Episode One is now one week from payment. Next week: locks, keys, and burglars — the security episode — and the hook is the near-miss that made the whole industry's blood run cold: a lone engineer, an unaccountable half-second of latency on his test machine, and the discovery that a patient stranger had spent three years charming his way into the world's compression library in order to leave a master key under the mat of, very nearly, every server on Earth. The most consequential five hundred milliseconds since the leap second, sir.

Until then.

*— End of Episode Seven.*
