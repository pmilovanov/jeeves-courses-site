Episode Four — The Ledger of Memory

The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux

The Machine That Pretended to Remember

Good day, sir.

Manchester, England, at the turn of the nineteen-sixties. A university team under Tom Kilburn — the same Manchester lineage that had run the world's first stored program a dozen years earlier — is building what will briefly be the most powerful computer on Earth, a machine called Atlas. And they have a problem of the purest kind: memory. Fast store — the magnetic cores a program can compute against — costs a fortune and is therefore tiny. Slow store — the spinning magnetic drum — is capacious and cheap and useless for computing directly. The machine will have a sliver of the one and a warehouse of the other, and every programmer of the era knows what that means, because every programmer of the era spends a substantial fraction of life on the treadmill called overlays: carving one's program into pieces sized to the sliver, and choreographing, by hand, in the program's own text, which piece shall be resident when — shuttling one's own code and data between warehouse and sliver like a man moving furniture between a house and a lock-up, forever.

The Atlas team's idea was to abolish the distinction by lying about it. The programmer, they proposed, should see one level of store — a single large address space, as big as the warehouse — and address it uniformly, as though it were all fast. Underneath, a supervisor program and some purpose-built hardware would maintain the fiction: the address space carved into pages; the sliver of fast core holding whichever pages were lately in use; a reference to an absent page trapping into the supervisor, which would fetch it from the drum, evict something to make room, and resume the interrupted program none the wiser. The machine would move the furniture. Nobody upstairs would ever know the lock-up existed.

Sir will recognise the pilot's great and necessary lie, and the field in time gave it its name: virtual memory — the one-level store being precisely the spacious, private, largely fictitious memory that every process in our house is issued today. Invented — pages, faults, eviction, resumption, the entire apparatus — in nineteen sixty-two, before the terminology existed to describe it. And I linger in Manchester for two details that echo down the whole season. First: the Atlas supervisor did not merely react; it studied. A component the team called, with disarming honesty, the learning program watched the drum's rotation and the pattern of page traffic and timed its transfers to the drum's angle, prediction in the service of pretence — the ancestor, sixty years removed, of every readahead heuristic and reclaim policy tonight's episode will visit. And second: the idea was not believed. A fiction that decides for itself what lives in fast store, against professionals who had always decided by hand? Respectable opinion held that the automatic policy could never match a programmer's knowledge, and respectable opinion took most of a decade to die — it took IBM's laboratories, among others, running the studies that showed the machine's guesses beating the humans' plans on real workloads, before the industry capitulated entirely. By the seventies the lie was orthodoxy; today it is so total that the pilot could present it as the very ground of the house, and sir had used it for twenty-five years without ever being told there had been an argument.

I open here because tonight is the episode about that argument's living descendant. The pilot showed you the mechanism of the lie, and I restate it in a breath, because tonight stands on it. Each process addresses a private fiction, translated to real memory through its page tables — the per-process maps from the addresses a program uses to the physical pages that presently answer for them. A page touched and found absent raises a page fault — the trap by which the kernel fetches what is missing and resumes the toucher none the wiser. Copy-on-write lets two processes share one physical page until either writes to it, the private copy paid for only then. And the page cache — the kernel's communal store of file pages, one copy serving every reader in the house — expands into whatever memory would otherwise sit idle, which is why a healthy machine reports almost nothing free: the cache has eaten it, for your own good. What the pilot did not show you is the policy: the standing decisions, revised thousands of times a second, about which pages deserve the sliver and which are exiled to the warehouse. That policy is a ledger — every page in the house accounted, judged, and ranked by its prospects — and the ledger's keeper faces Kilburn's exact problem at a billion times the scale: predict the future of every page from nothing but its past, cheaply enough that the predicting costs less than the being wrong.

Tonight, then: how the ledger is kept — the lists, the sweep of the clock, and the price of being wrong; how the phone in your pocket, of all machines, taught the mainline kernel a better bookkeeping, and what the old bookkeeping had been getting wrong for twenty years; the delicate matter of writing the ledger back — including the freeze mid-file-copy that sir has cursed at a USB stick about, which I intend to explain and partially exonerate; the seductions and betrayals of the very large page; the geography of memory on machines where distance is real; and, for the comparative choir, three houses that refuse our house's central bet — Windows, which will not overpromise; the Macintosh, which compresses what we would exile; and Android, which has quietly concluded that the warehouse is a trap, and kills instead.

The books, sir. Let us open them.

Part One — The Keeper's Rounds

Begin with what the ledger must decide. Physical pages are the sliver; the demand for them — mapped program pages, heap, the pilot's page cache — is the warehouse, always larger. When a page must be found — a fault to serve, a cache to grow — and none is free, the keeper reclaims: chooses a victim page, evicts its contents, and reissues it. The entire game is choosing well, and the stakes are asymmetric in the nastiest way: evict a page nobody wants again and the cost is nothing; evict a page wanted in the next millisecond and the cost is a fault, a disk wait, and — if the choosing is systematically bad — the death spiral the trade calls thrashing, where the machine spends its whole existence moving furniture and none computing. Kilburn's respectable sceptics were not wrong that the stakes were high; they were wrong that a human could price them.

The ideal policy is known and unbuildable: evict the page whose next use lies furthest in the future — provably optimal, requiring only clairvoyance. The buildable approximation is the one you would invent by Thursday: the past as prophet — least recently used, evict what has gone longest untouched. And here the house's constraints bite at once, because even that is unaffordable at scale. True least-recently-used bookkeeping means recording, on every memory reference, that this page moved to the head of a list — a cost per reference, billions per second, to optimise an event that happens thousands of times per second. The keeper cannot afford to watch the residents that closely; the watching would cost more than the rent.

So the real ledger is a system of cheap approximations, and its classical form — the form our house kept for two decades, and the form still taught — is two lists and a sweep. Every resident page sits on one of two rolls: the active list, the establishment, pages believed warm; and the inactive list, the antechamber of exile, pages under suspicion. Eviction takes only from the tail of the inactive list. Promotion and demotion between the lists is the policy, and the evidence is gathered by the cheapest instrument the hardware offers: a single referenced bit per page, set free of charge by the memory hardware whenever the page is touched. The keeper's rounds — the clock hands the season's plan promised, and for once the house's image is the field's own term: the literature calls this family of policies the clock algorithm, the hand sweeping a dial of pages — consist of sweeping along the lists, inspecting each page's bit, clearing it, and acting on what the interval since the last sweep recorded. Three verdicts cover every case, and I shall take them one at a time. An inactive page whose bit shows touched has answered the suspicion against it; it earns a further term of grace, or promotion back to the establishment. An inactive page whose bit is still clear has been touched by nobody; it drifts a step nearer the tail, and the tail is the drop. And an active page that fails the test patrol after patrol loses its standing and is demoted to the antechamber, where that same slow drift awaits. No timestamps, no per-reference cost — just periodic patrols reading one-bit testimony, the entire memory of the machine graded on a curve whose only datum is touched since I last asked, or not.

Two refinements complete the classical picture, and both correct famous injustices. The first: not all pages testify alike. A page from the page cache — a file's page, restorable from disk at will — is a cheap eviction; an anonymous page — heap, stack, the pilot's category with nowhere to go: memory backed by no file, which nothing on disk can restore — can be evicted only through the swap machinery, which must first write the page out to have anything to recall, at higher cost and higher risk of regret. The keeper therefore keeps the two populations on separate rolls and balances between them by a policy dial — and that dial is precisely the swappiness knob the pilot defended — defended, because swap is not a symptom of failure but the only exit anonymous memory has, and a house without one must keep every cold heap page enthroned in fast store forever: the knob is not "how much to swap" but which roll to lean on when the reckoning comes. The second: the single-use insult. A program streams through an enormous file once — a backup, a scan — and, under naive recency, that parade of never-again pages floods the establishment and evicts the working set of every resident in the house: the most recently touched memory in the machine, and the least deserving. The classical cure is admission standards: new cache pages enter the inactive roll on probation, and only a second touch — proof of re-use — promotes them; the stream that touches once and moves on drains harmlessly out the tail it came in by, having displaced almost nothing. When sir's overnight backup no longer destroys the morning's first hour of responsiveness, that probation rule is the benefactor — though I use the present tense loosely, for reasons the next part will make embarrassing.

And one instrument on the keeper's belt looks forward rather than back, in direct descent from Kilburn's learning program: readahead. The fault that fetches one page of a file is a fact; the keeper's wager is that it is also a trend. Detect sequential reading and the keeper fetches ahead of the asking — doubling its confidence window as the streak continues, abandoning it on the first seek elsewhere — so that a well-behaved sequential reader, after the first stumble, never faults again: the pages are always already arriving. A program's binary, recall, is not read whole at launch; its pages fault in one by one as execution first touches them — demand paging, in the field's term, the fetch deferred until the want is proven — which is half of why enormous binaries start faster than their size suggests, as the pilot promised; readahead is the other half, and the drum's angle, sir, is still being watched.

Part Two — The Phone That Retrained the Butler

For twenty years the two-list ledger was simply how Linux remembered, and its adequacy was the quiet kind that never makes the news. What finally indicted it was the least serverly hardware imaginable: the telephone, and the cheap laptop — machines with little memory, no patience, and a user whose thumb is a latency instrument of terrifying precision.

The indictment, drawn up by engineers on Android and ChromeOS staring at fleet-wide traces, had two counts. First: the testimony was too thin. One referenced bit, sampled at patrol intervals, cannot distinguish the page touched once from the page touched a thousand times within the interval — the establishment and the drifter give identical evidence — so under pressure, precisely when discrimination matters most, the classical keeper grades a vast population on a pass-fail quiz and evicts, with some regularity, the working set of the application under the user's very thumb. The symptom has a name in the trade: the refault — a page evicted and then wanted again shortly after, the ledger's own confession of error, and the fleets showed refault storms exactly at the painful moments. Second count: the patrols themselves. The sweeping — walking lists, testing and clearing bits, page by page under memory pressure — burns processor at the worst possible time; the keeper doing his rounds ever more frantically as the house fills, stealing cycles from the residents whose memory he is trying to save.

The reform, built principally at Google and merged into the mainline kernel at the end of twenty twenty-two, is called the multi-generational least-recently-used framework — MGLRU in the trade's shorthand, the letters standing for exactly those words, and that is the name to search; the multi-generational ledger, in the house's own books — and its two ideas are exactly the two counts answered. Instead of two rolls, generations: a series of age cohorts, youngest to oldest, with every page assigned to the generation in which it was last seen used. The patrols are restructured to age cohorts wholesale rather than interrogating pages retail, and the eviction question stops being "has this page been touched since I last asked" and becomes "which generation is old enough to cull" — finer-grained age, gathered more cheaply, with the working set visible as the young cohorts' population rather than inferred from one saturating bit. And woven through it, the refault elevated from symptom to instrument: the modern keeper tracks, for evicted pages, how long exile lasted before the page was recalled — the refault distance — and uses it as a live measure of its own error rate, tightening or relaxing its aggression accordingly. Kilburn's learning program, you notice, reborn at last with a memory of its own mistakes: the ledger now grades not only the pages but itself.

The results, on the machines that prosecuted the case, were the kind that close arguments — measurably fewer kills of the foreground application, measurably fewer janks of the scrolling thumb, single-digit-percent gains that at telephone scale are counted in billions of small mercies — and the server world, invited to the same table, found the new books served it too. I tell the story at this length, sir, for the season's own reasons. This is the largest revision of the kernel's memory policy in two decades, and it did not come from the server room that pays the kernel's bills; it came up from the pocket — from the smallest, crossest, most latency-jealous machines in the fleet — and was found general. The pilot's maxim about clever implementations under simple contracts has a sociological corollary tonight: the contract stays still while the cleverness underneath is renegotiated by whichever hardware is currently suffering most, and for the past decade the chief sufferer, and therefore the chief reformer, has been the machine in your pocket.

Which brings us, conveniently, to the ways the other houses keep these same books, because the comparisons are unusually honest here — every house faces the identical arithmetic, and their divergences are pure philosophy.

The Macintosh answers eviction with compression. Since the middle twenty-tens, macOS's first response to pressure is not exile but compaction: pages going cold are compressed, in memory, into roughly half their former space — the machine's own fast store made to serve as its first-line warehouse, with decompression on recall costing microseconds where the disk would cost milliseconds. Only under deeper pressure does compressed matter spill to disk. It is a straightforward purchase of memory with processor cycles, made at a moment in hardware history when cycles grew cheap faster than memory grew large — and our house promptly adopted the same trade in its own idiom: zram, a compressed block device made of RAM, deployed as the swap target on Android, ChromeOS, and a very large share of Linux desktops, so that "swapping to disk" on those machines is, in fact, swapping to one's own compressed memory. The pilot's defence of swap acquires its modern footnote here: on half the Linux fleet, the warehouse is the sliver, folded cleverly in half.

Windows, meanwhile, dissents at the root, and the dissent is the most instructive comparison in tonight's episode. Our house, the pilot confessed, overcommits: promises address space freely, bets that programs ask for more than they use, and when the bet fails sends the out-of-memory killer to shoot the fattest innocent in the room. Windows refuses the bet categorically. Every allocation of private memory is commit-charged: counted, at grant time, against a hard limit — physical memory plus the paging file — and when the sum of promises would exceed the limit, the allocation fails, then and there, politely, to the program that asked. No killer, because no lie: the system never promises what it could not, in the worst case, actually deliver. And each philosophy generates its own characteristic misery, which is how you know the choice is real. Windows programs meet allocation failures that almost never mean the machine is out of memory — merely out of promises — and a paging file sized too small produces mysterious refusals with gigabytes visibly idle; whole generations of Windows lore concern the sizing of that file. Our programs never see an allocation fail — and then one afternoon the database is dead on the floor and the logs say the kernel shot it. Honest refusal with false alarms, against generous promising with occasional executions: two complete moral systems, sir, each finding the other's failure mode barbaric. And standing quietly behind our house's choice, like a founding uncle nobody may criticise, is the fork call. Fork, as Episode Two established, duplicates a whole address space on paper only — copy-on-write defers the real expense, on the bet that most of the copy will never be rewritten — and a strict accountant would have to price every fork at the full size of the parent, which would make the largest processes the least able to fork. The generous promise is what makes fork affordable; refuse the one and you have priced out the other.

And Android — running our very kernel — has concluded that at the pocket's scale the whole eviction question is misframed, and built its answer above stairs. A telephone holds dozens of applications "running"; the platform's actual contract is that only a handful matter — the one under the thumb, the music, the navigation — and everything else is reconstructible: applications are required, by platform law, to save their small essential state and be killable without ceremony. So Android's memory manager of last resort is not the kernel's killer but a userspace daemon — the low-memory killer daemon, lmkd — which watches pressure and kills by policy: cached and background applications first, by a ranking the platform maintains continuously, the foreground essentially never. And what does it watch? Not the free-memory figure the pilot taught you to distrust, but the pressure-stall gauge the pilot commended — pressure stall information, to give the kernel's full name for the instrument: its own confession of the time processes lost waiting on memory — fed to a daemon that begins culling the reconstructible the moment stall time crosses thresholds tuned per device. The pilot's two lessons — free memory is a vanity metric, stall time is the honest one — are, on four billion devices, wired directly into the trigger of the gun. Death, on the telephone, is not a disaster; it is the routine reclaim path, domesticated by a platform contract that makes resurrection cheap. The out-of-memory killer's injustice, which the pilot mourned, turns out to be curable — not by better aim, but by an entire social order built around acceptable murder.

Part Three — Writing the Books Back, and the Freeze at the Stick

The ledger so far has evicted as though eviction were free, and for clean pages it nearly is. But a dirty page — modified in memory, its disk copy stale — cannot be dropped; it must first be written back, and the writeback machinery is where tonight touches the daily texture of sir's life most directly.

The pilot laid the foundation: writes go to the cache and the call returns; background threads carry dirty pages to disk in due course; durability on demand is a separate, explicit purchase — the call named fsync, the letter f before the word sync, by which a program insists the disk truly hold its bytes before the call returns. Tonight, the governor on that arrangement. The house cannot let dirt accumulate without bound — a crash forfeits everything unwritten, and a reclaim that keeps meeting dirty pages is a reclaim stuck behind a disk. So the keeper enforces dirty thresholds: a fraction of memory beyond which the background writers stir, and a higher fraction at which the house takes the gloves off — and the glove removal is the part civilians never suspect: past the hard threshold, the kernel makes the dirtiers themselves pay. A process writing furiously into the cache is throttled — its writes made to wait, in proportion to its own contribution to the dirt — the polluter taxed at the point of pollution until the writers below stairs catch up.

Now, the stick. Sir copies a large file to a cheap flash drive. The progress bar sprints to nearly done in seconds — then freezes, interminably, at the threshold of victory; the copy claims completion and the eject takes a further geological age. No mystery survives tonight's equipment. The sprint was the cache absorbing the file at memory speed — gigabytes of instantly dirty pages destined for a device that writes at a crawl. The freeze at the end is the dirty threshold arithmetic arriving: the copying process, having outrun the stick by orders of magnitude, is finally throttled to the stick's true pace, and the progress bar — which measured writes into the cache — stands revealed as having measured a fiction all along. And the endless eject is the honest remainder: the unmount is the one operation that must flush every page and cannot lie about it. The traditional infuriation — "why does it say done when it is not done?" — has the traditional answer: done meant handed to the staff, as it means everywhere in the house; the stick is merely the one place slow enough that the difference between handed-over and delivered becomes a lived experience. The partial exoneration I promised: on any respectable disk the same fiction plays out beneath perception, and the cache that infuriates at the stick is the cache that makes everything else feel instant. The stick is not where the design fails; it is where the design becomes visible — and machines are rarely forgiven for becoming visible.

One more ledger-keeping duty belongs here, glimpsed in the pilot and painful in practice: the very large page, and what it costs to keep the promise.

The arithmetic that recommends huge pages the pilot already gave you, and the instrument at its centre deserves its proper name: the translation lookaside buffer — the TLB, in everyone's speech — the small hardware cache of recent address translations, the processor's crib sheet for the page tables, consulted on every reference so that the tables themselves need not be. The buffer is small, its misses are a stealth tax on big-memory work, and one two-megabyte page occupies a single entry in it where five hundred small pages occupy five hundred. Databases and hypervisors duly reserve explicit huge pages — a fixed pool, set aside at boot, guaranteed and never fragmented, the respectable form of the medicine. The house, wishing to democratise the benefit, added the transparent form: the kernel, unbidden, assembling ordinary process memory into huge pages behind the scenes, gluing small pages into large wherever alignment and luck allow. And the transparency, sir, was for years a famous betrayal — the reason half the database installation guides on Earth open with the incantation disable transparent huge pages, cargo-culted by thousands who could not say why. You can say why, with tonight's ledger in hand. A huge page must be physically contiguous — five hundred and twelve real pages, adjacent, aligned — and a machine that has run for months has memory like a well-used pegboard: plenty of holes, none of them adjacent. Manufacturing contiguity on demand means compaction — the kernel shuffling live pages to consolidate free space, at allocation time, on the allocating process's own clock — and the daemon that glues pages in the background competes for the same locks and cycles. The visible symptom: rare, unpredictable stalls of breathtaking length in programs that touched memory at exactly the wrong moment — latency spikes orphaned from any cause visible above stairs, the pilot's invisible-cost genus at its purest. The modern settlement is a compromise dial — assemble huge pages only where processes explicitly advise, through the memory-advice call the manuals spell madvise, the letter m fused to the front of the word advise, by which a program volunteers its intentions for a span of memory without commanding anything; and defer compaction away from the allocation path — and the episode's moral is one the season keeps re-earning: transparent is the most expensive word in systems engineering; every fiction is paid for by somebody, at a time of the fiction's choosing.

Part Four — Private Ledgers, and the Art of Choosing the Victim

Before geography, a domestic matter the pilot left at the introduction stage: the control group's memory books. The control group, to restate the institution itself, is the kernel's enclosure for rationing a whole family of processes at once — processes gathered under one named account, and the account, not the process, made the unit the house meters. Tonight, its memory books, which is to say the machinery by which the one great ledger is partitioned into household accounts — and the full anatomy, at last, of the number one hundred and thirty-seven.

A memory control group is a private ledger within the keeper's public one: every page charged, at allocation, to the account of the group whose process touched it first — heap and stack, naturally, but also, and much less obviously, the page cache: the files a container reads are charged to its account, which is the resolution of a mystery every container operator meets — the workload that "uses" a modest heap yet shows a memory figure climbing relentlessly toward its limit. It is not leaking; it is reading, and its cache is billed to it. The account has three settings worth sir's memory. The hard limit is a wall: at the wall, the keeper runs reclaim within the account only — the group's own cache dropped, its own cold pages swapped, the whole eviction apparatus of Part One executed in miniature against one household — and if reclaim inside the walls cannot find the page, the killer is summoned to that household: the out-of-memory execution scoped to the group, one tenant shot in his own rooms, the rest of the house unaware. The instrument of execution is the kill signal — sig-kill, to give it its name, the ninth on the signal roll and the one no process may catch or refuse — and the shell's convention for reporting a death by signal is one hundred and twenty-eight plus the signal's number: one hundred and twenty-eight and nine make the famous figure. That is the container exit of one hundred and thirty-seven in its natural habitat: not the machine out of memory — the machine may be swimming in it — but one account overdrawn, its balance enforced by the only currency the kernel ultimately accepts. Beneath the wall sits the high watermark, the civilised alternative: cross it and the keeper does not kill but harries — throttling the group's allocations and pressing reclaim on it continuously, degradation instead of execution, the preferred instrument of platforms that would rather a service limp than vanish. And on the protective side, a floor: memory-low, a promise that this account's pages shall be spared reclaim while the system can possibly look elsewhere — the mirror image of a limit, used by the desktop distributions that finally learned to fence the user's session away from the browser's appetite, and by operators who fence the database's cache against the backup job's flood. The pilot's swappiness dial, you recall, chose which roll to lean on; these three dials choose whose rolls, and together they turn the keeper from a single magistrate into a system of household courts.

And the killer itself, since we are at last equipped to watch it choose. The score the pilot summarised — chiefly, how much memory would this death return — is computed per candidate and then bent by the adjustment knob the pilot mentioned — the oom score adjustment, to give its proper name, oom being the trade's own contraction of out-of-memory, the three words fused by underscores in the actual books — whose range runs from never choose me at one extreme, through neutrality, to choose me first at the other. The practical liturgy on sir's own machines writes itself from those two facts. The database gets a strong negative adjustment — not immunity, which is reserved and dangerous, but a heavy thumb on its side of the scales — while the batch jobs and caches around it are marked cheerfully expendable; the system's own critical daemons ship with their adjustments already set by the distribution, which is why the machine survives its own crises with such suspicious poise. Android, we said, ranks every application continuously; the ranking is written through this very knob — the platform adjusting each process's score as it moves between foreground and background, so that lmkd's policy and the kernel's last-resort killer agree, at every instant, about the order of sacrifice. The moral of the pilot — the largest process is executed for a crime committed elsewhere — thus acquires its practical appendix: the injustice is not repealed, but any operator who knows the two knobs can aim it, and the difference between a fleet that shoots its databases and a fleet that sheds its caches is, in the end, four lines of configuration written by somebody who once heard this episode.

One footnote on the swap machinery completes the domestic tour, because two pieces of folklore die cheaply here. The exiled page's passage is not a simple write-and-forget: an evicted anonymous page passes through the swap cache, a staging ledger where it remains readable while its disk copy is written — so a page recalled during its own eviction is retrieved from memory at no cost, the keeper's regret rate quietly subsidised. And the ancient debate between the swap partition and the swap file — over which whole forum threads have been fought since the nineties — is settled and has been for years: the kernel maps a swap file's blocks once at activation and thereafter writes to it as directly as to any partition; the performance distinction is extinct, and the file's flexibility — resized, added, removed at will, no repartitioning liturgy — won on every other axis. Sir may retire that particular inherited opinion with full honours.

Part Five — The Geography of Memory

The last room tonight abandons a pretence the whole episode has quietly maintained: that memory is one place, equally distant from everywhere. On sir's larger servers it is not, and the ledger knows it.

A modern two-socket machine is two computers wearing one case: each processor package with its own memory controllers and its own banks of physical memory, joined by a fast interconnect. Any processor can reach any memory — the address space is unified, the lie holds — but local memory answers in perhaps half the time of memory across the link, and the difference, applied to every reference of a memory-hungry workload, is not a rounding error; it is tens of percent of throughput. The trade calls the arrangement non-uniform memory access — NUMA — and the keeper's response occupies three escalating tiers. Placement first: by default, a fault is served with a page from the node where the faulting thread is running — memory born local, by the first-touch rule the pilot described: no physical page is chosen at reservation, only at the first touch, and it is chosen from where the toucher stands — with the pleasant corollary that a well-pinned workload (pinning, from Episode Three: the binding of a thread to named processors, so that it cannot wander) stays local for free. Policy second: workloads with opinions may bind memory to nodes explicitly, or interleave deliberately across them — the administrator's tools sir has met in database tuning guides. And patrol third: because threads migrate and workloads drift, the kernel runs a standing corrective — automatic NUMA balancing — whose method rewards a slow walk-through. Periodically, the kernel unmaps a small sample of a process's pages. Nothing is lost by this; the pages stay resident; only the translations are withdrawn. The process, touching one, takes a fault that costs almost nothing — a hinting fault, the kernel calls it — and the fault is a datum: this thread, on this node, wants that page, on that node. Gather enough such testimony and the remedy writes itself: migrate the page toward its actual user, or nudge the scheduler to move the thread toward its memory, whichever costs less. The technique deserves a beat of admiration: the kernel deliberately induces tiny, harmless faults as a survey instrument — the fault, which the pilot promoted from error to mechanism, here promoted once more to census-taker. The ledger does not merely rank pages by age; on big iron it ranks them by address, and the keeper walks the halls with a map.

I close the geography with its modern frontier, one sentence deep and pointed squarely at a later episode: the industry is presently stretching this same machinery over memory attached across fabrics — pools of RAM shared between machines, distances longer still, the tiers multiplying — which is to say the ledger's future is more geography, not less, and the house that learned to grade pages by age and place will spend the next decade grading them by postcode. The cloud, as ever, is the old problems at new distances.

The Books of the Evening

Balance the accounts, sir. The lie of one-level store — virtual memory, as the field now writes it — was born in Manchester with a learning program at its side, disbelieved for a decade, and is now the ground beneath every house standing. Its policy is a ledger kept by cheap testimony — referenced bits, patrols, probation for the once-touched, separate rolls for the restorable and the exiled — because perfect bookkeeping would cost more than the errors it prevents. The books were rewritten this decade by the pocket: generations for rolls, the refault turned from symptom to instrument, the keeper grading himself — the deepest policy reform in twenty years, driven up from the crossest hardware rather than down from the grandest. Writing back is governed by thresholds that end, at the extreme, in the polluter throttled at the pen — the USB freeze not a failure of the design but the design, for once, standing where you can see it. The huge page is a bargain with fragmentation whose transparent form taught the industry what the word transparent costs. Geography is real: pages have addresses in both senses, the keeper migrates them toward their users by inducing faults as a survey, and the distances are about to get longer. And around our house's central bet — promise freely, kill when wrong — stand the dissenting houses: Windows refusing ever to overpromise and paying in false refusals; the Macintosh folding its warehouse into compressed halves of the sliver, a trade our zram promptly copied; Android, atop our own kernel, domesticating the killer itself — pressure-stall on the trigger, resurrection cheap by decree, death as a working part of the machine.

Next week we descend below the ledger entirely, to the shelves themselves: the filesystem and the disk — the library below, where the pilot's inodes and journals acquire their full machinery, where copy-on-write rebuilds the librarian's entire craft, and where the block layer, once the house's cleverest sorter of spinning platters, has spent a decade learning that the new shelves need no sorting at all. The hook is the quietest heist in the history of deployment: the night a company converted a billion telephones to a new filesystem, in an update, without losing one — having secretly rehearsed the conversion, on your phone and everyone else's, without telling anybody.

Until then, sir.
