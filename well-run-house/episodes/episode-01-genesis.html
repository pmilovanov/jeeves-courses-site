Episode One — Genesis, or How the House Was Built

The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux

The Professor and the Student

Good day, sir.

On the twenty-ninth of January, nineteen ninety-two, a professor of computer science at the Free University of Amsterdam sat down at his terminal and posted, to a public newsgroup he himself presided over, a message titled — with the serene confidence of a man grading a paper — "Linux is obsolete."

The professor was Andrew Tanenbaum, and he had standing. He was the author of the standard textbook on operating systems, and of a small teaching system called MINIX that accompanied it — a miniature Unix, legally clean, that a student could read in its entirety. His argument was not foolish, and I want that on the record before the laughter starts, because we shall spend a whole episode near the season's end discovering how right he nearly was. He argued that the debate among people who design operating systems was over: the future belonged to microkernels — small, elegant systems in which the kernel does almost nothing and everything interesting lives outside it — and that writing a large, old-fashioned, everything-in-one-basket kernel in nineteen ninety-one was, in his words, a truly poor idea. A giant step back into the nineteen-seventies. He remarked, with the twinkle of a man who knows exactly where the knife is going, that had the author of this Linux been his student, he would not have received a high mark for the design.

The author of this Linux was a second-year student in Helsinki named Linus Torvalds, and his reply began — I am selecting the printable portions — by conceding that the professor's job was being a professor, and suggesting that this was a fine excuse for the shortcomings of MINIX. He then defended his design on the merits, with a confidence entirely unsupported by his circumstances: he was twenty-two, his operating system was a few months old, it ran on precisely one kind of processor, and his adversary had literally written the textbook.

The flame war that followed is one of the founding documents of our profession, and rereading it now produces a sensation I can only call dramatic irony in industrial quantities. Because you know how it ends, sir. The student's obsolete, old-fashioned, poorly graded kernel is presently running the overwhelming majority of the world's servers, every Android telephone in every pocket, the top of every list of supercomputers, most of the world's embedded machinery, and — a detail I shall serve properly in a later episode — it is running inside most of the machines that run its competitors. And the professor's beautiful doctrine, meanwhile, did not exactly lose the argument. It went underground, and won a different war, in places you would never think to look. Your car believes in microkernels. So does the secret second computer that lives inside your computer. But that story is a debt I am issuing now, formally, payable in Episode Eleven, when we tour the other houses.

Tonight is about where our house came from. And I want to make the case, before we begin, that this is not the dispensable "history bit" that one skips to get to the machinery. The pilot toured the house as it stands; this season takes the rooms one by one; and the very first thing to understand is that nothing about the house was inevitable. Every load-bearing wall we examined — the system call, the one bell rope by which any program asks the kernel for any service; the process, the running program as the kernel keeps account of it, with fork, which makes a new process by duplicating the one that asks; the file, and the descriptor, the small numbered handle by which a process holds whatever it has open — was an argument somebody had, in a particular room, against a particular alternative, under particular constraints of money and law and personality. The season's whole thesis is that an operating system is not a monument but an argument. Tonight you hear the argument's opening rounds — and you learn why, of all the houses built in that great quarrelsome century, the one you live in was raised by a student in a bedroom rather than a corporation in a tower.

We have four stories to tell, and they interlock like a good farce: the attic workshop where Unix was invented, more or less illegally, inside a corporation that had just sworn off software; the lawyers whose settlements and lawsuits decided, far more than any benchmark, which systems lived and which died; the prophet in Cambridge who built an entire cathedral of software and left out only the kernel; and the student who supplied the missing stone without quite meaning to. Then we shall stand the rival houses up — the one Microsoft built out of Digital's exiles, the one Apple bought for a song from its own founder — and watch the war for the server room that our house, to everyone's astonishment including its own, won outright.

In we go, sir — by the attic stairs.

Part One — The Attic at Bell Labs

Every telling of this story begins in the same place, because it must: a computing centre in Murray Hill, New Jersey, at the end of the nineteen-sixties, in the research arm of the American telephone monopoly.

Bell Labs had spent the late sixties as a partner in a grand project called Multics — a joint venture with MIT and General Electric to build the operating system to end all operating systems. Multics was to be a computing utility: one enormous machine serving a whole city, the way the power station serves it, with every modern idea baked in — virtual memory; files that were memory; security in rings, privilege arranged in concentric grades, the innermost trusted with the machine itself; continuous operation. If some of those ideas sound familiar from the pilot, they should; Multics is the ancestor that half the pilot's furniture came from, rings included. But it was also late, over budget, and grinding toward a kind of magnificent bureaucratic completeness, and in nineteen sixty-nine Bell Labs pulled out.

Which left a group of researchers — among them Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie — in an interesting position. They had tasted interactive computing and liked it very much. They had also just lost the machine it ran on. And their employer, burnt, had no appetite whatever for funding another operating system.

What happened next has hardened into legend, and the legend is, for once, substantially true. Thompson found a little-used PDP-seven — a small, already obsolescent machine from Digital Equipment Corporation — and began writing, ostensibly for the purpose of running a game he had written called Space Travel, the essential organs of an operating system: a filesystem, a process layer, a shell. His wife took their new son to visit relatives for a few weeks; Thompson, by his own account, allotted one week each to the operating system, the shell, the editor, and the assembler. A colleague, in a pun on the grandeur of Multics — whose name unpacked to the Multiplexed Information and Computing Service — called the result Unics, the Uniplexed version: the castrated Multics, one of whatever Multics had many of. The spelling drifted, and the name stuck.

Now, sir, the temptation at this point in the story is to marvel at the speed and move on. Resist it. The speed was the design. Multics had attempted everything and arrived nowhere; Unix attempted almost nothing, and the almost-nothing it attempted was chosen with a taste so exact that we are still living inside the choices. You met them all in the pilot without knowing their provenance. Everything is a file: that is Unix deciding that one interface, however humble, beats a dozen specialised ones. The process that forks: that is Thompson choosing the simplest possible mechanism for making new processes — duplicate the one you have — and accepting the strangeness as the price of the simplicity. The pipeline: that arrived a little later, urged by their colleague Doug McIlroy, who had been preaching for years that programs should be like segments of a garden hose — screw them together and let the data flow. When Thompson added the pipe in an evening, and the vertical bar notation with it, the whole laboratory reorganised its habits within weeks. Small tools, each doing one thing, composed at the shell: the philosophy you have practised for twenty-five years was a doctrine before it was a habit, and it was a doctrine of poverty. They had a tiny machine, no budget, and no mandate; every feature had to pay rent.

Two further strokes completed the founding, and the second is the one that decided everything.

The first: in nineteen seventy-three or so, Ritchie and Thompson did something considered close to mad at the time — they rewrote the operating system, the kernel itself, in a high-level language. Operating systems were written in assembly; everyone knew this; assembly was what the machine spoke, and a kernel that was not written in it would be too slow. Ritchie had been evolving a language for the purpose — he called it C, being the successor to one called B, our profession's naming genius already fully formed — and C was exactly as high-level as it needed to be and not one inch more. It has been described, with affection and accuracy, as a language that combines the power of assembly with the convenience of assembly. But a kernel written in C could be read. And, decisively, it could be moved. An operating system had always been a property of a machine, as a dialect is the property of a valley. Unix in C was the first operating system that could emigrate — and within a few years it had proved it, when the group ported the whole system to a machine of an entirely different make and architecture, an exercise undertaken partly to flush out every place the code had quietly assumed its birthplace. Portability was not a feature added to Unix; it was a discipline beaten into it, early, while it was still small enough to teach.

Two footnotes from the attic, both of which you have already met without their pedigrees. The clocks of the pilot's fifth part count their seconds from the start of nineteen-seventy for no deeper reason than that the young Unix needed an epoch and its authors chose the round number just behind them — the entire digital world now reckons time from a date selected, roughly, as "recently, but not awkwardly so", and the deadline of twenty thirty-eight that I gave you in the pilot — the year the traditional signed thirty-two-bit count of those seconds runs out of room, on the enormous population of small devices still keeping it — is the bill for that convenience, arriving with interest. And when Thompson and Ritchie received their profession's highest honour in nineteen eighty-three, Thompson's acceptance lecture — Reflections on Trusting Trust — was a cheerful demonstration that a compiler could be taught to insert an invisible back door into every program it compiled, including the next generation of itself, whereupon the treachery vanishes from all source code everywhere and lives on only in the binary, undetectable by any amount of reading. You cannot trust code you did not create yourself, he concluded — total trust rests on the tools, and the tools' tools, all the way down. File that lecture carefully, sir. Episode Eight opens with the day, four decades later, when somebody very nearly did it to the entire world at once.

And the second stroke: the law. Here is the beautiful accident on which our whole world turns. AT&T, the parent company, operated under a consent decree — an antitrust settlement dating from nineteen fifty-six — which forbade it from entering the computer business. It was a telephone monopoly, and it was to remain only a telephone monopoly. So when word of Unix spread, and universities began asking for it, AT&T's lawyers concluded that the company could not sell it as a product, could not advertise it, could not support it. What it could do was license it — source code included, since a tape without support had better come with the means of self-help — for a nominal fee, to educational institutions, essentially as an act of scholarly correspondence.

Consider what this meant in practice, sir. The best operating system in the world was circulating among universities with its source code, for the price of the tape, supported by nobody — which meant supported by everybody. Graduate students read the kernel the way literature students read novels. An Australian academic named John Lions wrote a line-by-line commentary on the sixth edition kernel — the code on one side, the explanation facing it — which the lawyers then suppressed under a later, stingier licence, and which therefore circulated for a decade in photocopies of photocopies, the most influential samizdat in the history of engineering. A generation learned to think in Unix. When that generation graduated and went out to industry, they took the doctrine with them.

An operating system spread by lawyers' accident, taught from forbidden photocopies, improved by its own students. No product manager, no roadmap, no marketing department — and by the end of the seventies it was quietly everywhere that mattered. Then AT&T was broken up, the consent decree dissolved, and the lawyers discovered that the scholarly correspondence had been, all along, a product. The moment they reached to take it back is the moment our story properly begins.

Part Two — The Licence and the Lawyers

The university that mattered most was Berkeley.

Thompson spent a sabbatical year there in the mid-seventies, teaching the system he had built, and left behind him a culture. A graduate student named Bill Joy began collecting improvements, fixes, and new tools onto tapes and mailing them out — the Berkeley Software Distribution, BSD, run with the energy of a one-man publishing house. Joy in those years was a phenomenon; the tools he wrote or shepherded — the vi editor among them, and the C shell — are in your muscle memory today. And when the American defence research agency went looking, at the turn of the eighties, for a home for its new internetworking protocols, it chose Berkeley Unix, and funded the work properly.

Pause on that one, sir, because the pilot walked its consequences without naming its origin. TCP and IP — the transmission control protocol and the internet protocol, to give the initials their full names the once — were put into Unix at Berkeley in the early eighties, and the interface the Berkeley programmers invented for them — the socket, opened and read and written and closed like everything else in the house — became, simply, how software speaks to a network. Not how Unix software speaks; how software speaks. When we tour the network stack in Episode Six you will hear the whole story; for tonight the point is narrower and sharper. Berkeley's sockets spread to every operating system on Earth, including the ones built expressly to bury Unix. Microsoft's own version is called Winsock — Windows sockets — the ancestry confessed in the name, like a family nose.

And here I must lay in a fact so contrary to the received picture of the eighties that listeners have accused me of invention, so let me state it plainly and dare you to check it. For a stretch of the early eighties, the largest vendor of Unix on the planet, by machines shipped, was — Microsoft. The company licensed Unix from AT&T in nineteen seventy-nine, dressed it for microcomputers, and sold it as Xenix; there were years when the smart money assumed Xenix, not DOS, was Microsoft's operating system of the future, and for a while the company's own developers did their serious work on it. When IBM came shopping for something small and cheap for its personal computer, history swerved down the fork you know; Xenix was handed off to a partner in Santa Cruz called SCO, a name I ask you to pocket for later, because it returns at the end of tonight's story as a revenant, and the revenant is funny. But mark the might-have-been: the Unix wars to come were very nearly a civil war fought inside Microsoft's product line, and the company that would one day call our house a cancer spent its youth selling the family's furniture.

Through the eighties, then, there were two great streams of Unix: AT&T's, now commercialised after the breakup of the Bell System — System Five, the ancestral line, sold with contracts and roadmaps — and Berkeley's, flowing free to anyone with an AT&T source licence, which universities had and workstation vendors acquired. The two streams diverged — different init systems, different printing systems, different directory layouts, a schism whose scar tissue you have personally palpated any time you have wondered why some commands take dashes and some do not, or why there are two families of everything. The workstation industry of the eighties — Sun the greatest of them, co-founded by the same Bill Joy — built on the Berkeley stream; the staid commercial establishment bought the AT&T stream; and the two blocs eventually organised themselves into rival consortia and fought what the trade press called, without much exaggeration, the Unix wars. The spectacle repays a paragraph, because it explains the world Linux was born into. And the sides deserve placing before they move, because they are not the sides you expect: Sun, the greatest house of the Berkeley stream, crossed the aisle and allied with AT&T itself, the ancestral owner — and it was against that alliance, not against Berkeley, that the rest of the industry took arms. When AT&T and Sun allied to unify their systems in the late eighties, the other vendors — IBM, Digital, Hewlett-Packard, each selling its own proprietary Unix — took fright at the prospect of their common ancestor and their fiercest rival holding the standard between them, and formed the Open Software Foundation to build a counter-Unix; AT&T's camp countered with a consortium of its own; and for several years the industry's giants poured money into the vital work of ensuring that Unix meant two things rather than one. Wits of the period rendered the Open Software Foundation's initials as "Oppose Sun Forever". Meanwhile a committee of engineers, working at one remove from the war, defined the neutral ground: POSIX — the portable operating system interface, as the letters roughly unpack — a specification of what any Unix must look like to a program — the calls, the shell, the tools — regardless of whose it was. POSIX is why your shell scripts run everywhere today, and its committee-brokered compromises are why they are as baroque as they are. A standard, as ever, is a peace treaty, and peace treaties enshrine the war.

The deeper lesson of the Unix wars is the one Torvalds would later refuse to relearn. The commercial Unixes fragmented because each vendor's differentiation was its business model: your Unix had to be incompatible enough to hold your customers and compatible enough to poach your rival's. The GPL, when it arrived — the GNU General Public License, whose story is a few minutes ahead of us — dissolved that trap by force of law — nobody could hold a private fork of the commons and sell the difference, so the rational strategy flipped from differentiation to contribution, from hoarding improvements to racing them upstream. The licence did not merely permit cooperation; it made cooperation the only game with a payoff. Economists took decades to model what every kernel contributor understood by instinct.

And then, at the start of the nineties, came the endgame nobody expected: the lawyers returned, and destroyed the wrong system.

The Berkeley people had spent years replacing AT&T's code, file by file, with their own — partly on principle, partly because everyone could see where matters were heading. By nineteen ninety-one they had released a version they believed was essentially free of AT&T's property; a small company called BSDi formed to sell a complete Unix for the new cheap Intel machines, and advertised it with a telephone number one digit — forgive me: one step — removed from AT&T's own. AT&T's Unix subsidiary sued, in nineteen ninety-two, over the code and the trademark and the trade secrets, and the suit hung over Berkeley Unix for two years like a frost. Workstation vendors shied. Projects stalled. The energy of the freest, most inventive stream of Unix was diverted into depositions precisely — precisely, sir — during the two years in which a Finnish student's hobby kernel went from a toy to a movement. The suit settled in early nineteen ninety-four, mostly in Berkeley's favour, a handful of files removed, and the free descendants of Berkeley Unix — FreeBSD, NetBSD, and their kin — emerged blinking into a world in which the niche they had been born to fill was already occupied.

I know of no cleaner demonstration, anywhere in the history of our field, that the fate of software is decided in courtrooms and licence texts at least as often as in benchmarks. Hold that thought through everything that follows: the house you live in stands on land cleared by other people's lawsuits.

Part Three — The Cathedral Without a Kernel

Now to Cambridge, Massachusetts, and the strangest and most consequential figure in this entire story.

Richard Stallman was a programmer at the MIT artificial intelligence laboratory — by every account one of the finest of his generation — and he watched, through the late seventies and early eighties, the world I have just described to you closing. Software that had circulated as correspondence became product. Colleagues signed non-disclosure agreements and would no longer show him their code. A printer arrived whose driver he could not fix, because the driver had become a secret. Where a normal man would have grumbled and adapted, Stallman concluded that an entire moral order was being extinguished, and announced — in September nineteen eighty-three, on the newsgroups — that he would build a complete operating system that no one could ever close, and give it away. He called it GNU, an acronym unpacking to "GNU's Not Unix" — a name that contains itself, which tells you most of what you need to know about the culture — and it would be Unix-shaped, because Unix was the best design going and because compatibility was the Trojan horse: make it Unix-shaped and the Unix people can defect to freedom without rewriting anything.

It is fashionable to find Stallman difficult, and the fashion has its reasons. But I put it to you, sir, that the two most consequential pieces of engineering in this whole saga are Ritchie's C and Stallman's licence — and the licence is the deeper of the two.

The GNU General Public License, first issued in complete form in nineteen eighty-nine, is a legal machine built from the enemy's own parts. It uses copyright — the very instrument of enclosure — to enforce openness. You may copy this software, it says; you may change it; you may sell it; but if you distribute it, you must pass these same freedoms along, source code included, and any work you build upon it inherits the same terms. The licence propagates, in other words, exactly like the software it protects — copyleft, the culture came to call the device, copyright turned precisely about. Its critics called that clause viral, and meant the word as a slander; Microsoft's chief executive would one day call Linux itself "a cancer", in roughly the same spirit and to roughly the same effect. Both metaphors concede the essential point: the thing spreads, and what it touches stays free. Stallman had discovered how to make generosity legally hereditary.

Through the eighties, GNU built the cathedral: the C compiler, GCC — the GNU C Compiler, as the letters then read — which broke the compiler vendors' pricing overnight and became the anvil on which a thousand systems were forged; the editor Emacs, about which I shall say only that its partisans and I have corresponded; the debugger; the shell — bash, the Bourne again shell, the pun fully intended and fully licensed; the core tools, each a free replacement for a Unix original, frequently better than the original. By nineteen ninety-one the toolchain was complete and self-hosting. A programmer could compile, edit, link, and debug entirely inside GNU.

One piece was missing. The centre. The kernel.

And here the cathedral's tragedy: the GNU kernel, called Hurd, had been designed — and I want you to hear this with Episode Eleven already ringing in your ears — as a microkernel system, the professionally fashionable choice, the very doctrine Tanenbaum would shortly preach at Torvalds. A small message-passing core, Mach, from Carnegie Mellon, with the traditional kernel's organs re-imagined as a flock of user-space servers — a herd of gnus, hence the name. It was elegant. It was correct by the standards of every operating systems conference of its day. And it was hard — for when the kernel's organs become separate processes exchanging messages, the coordination miseries of a distributed system move into the machine, and Episode Three will show you what those miseries are made of — and it slipped, year upon year, a decade upon a decade. It exists today; it is still, gamely, not finished. The cathedral stood complete around an empty crossing, waiting for a keystone nobody could cut.

Which brings us, at last, to the bedroom in Helsinki.

Part Four — A Hobby, Nothing Big

In the spring of nineteen ninety-one, Linus Torvalds was a second-year computer science student at the University of Helsinki with a new personal computer — one of the cheap machines built on Intel's three-eighty-six processor — and a copy of Tanenbaum's MINIX running on it, because MINIX was what a student could legally have. He wanted to reach the university's Unix machines from home, so he wrote himself a terminal program. Being the sort of young man he was, he wrote it not as a program but as two bare tasks scheduled on the raw hardware — one reading the modem, one writing the screen — his own tiny scheduler switching between them. Then he wanted to download things, so the terminal program grew a disk driver and a filesystem. There is a moment, familiar to a certain kind of engineer, when one looks up from a growing pile of perfectly reasonable individual decisions and discovers one has accidentally built the foundations of an operating system. Torvalds has said the realisation arrived roughly then: what he had was nearly a kernel, and the interesting question was whether to finish it.

On the twenty-fifth of August, nineteen ninety-one, he posted to the MINIX newsgroup — the professor's own parish notice-board, which adds a certain retrospective spice — a message of legendary modesty: he was doing a free operating system, "just a hobby, won't be big and professional like gnu", for three-eighty-six clones only, and what features would people like to see? Note, sir, the standard against which he measured his unambition: gnu. The cathedral was the big professional thing his hobby would never be. Version zero-point-oh-one went quietly onto a university server that September — the filename gave the world the word linux, a name Torvalds had thought too vain to use and a friend administering the server thought too good to waste. It was crude past describing: it ran on exactly his hardware, its filesystem was MINIX's, chunks of it were in assembly that could never move anywhere else. Tanenbaum's technical indictment, five months later, was in every particular true.

And none of the particulars mattered, because of three things the indictment could not see.

First, the licence. The earliest Linux carried Torvalds's own home-made terms, which among other things forbade charging money for it. Within a few months — persuaded, in part, by encounters with the GNU world and its arguments — he re-issued it under Stallman's GPL. He has called that decision, more than once, the best thing he ever did. Consider the machinery it engaged, sir. The GPL meant every contributor kept their rights while guaranteeing everyone else's; no company could ever seize the commons and close it; and the entire completed GNU toolchain — compiler, shell, libraries, tools, the whole cathedral — could be assembled around the new kernel into a working system immediately. The keystone had found its cathedral, or the cathedral its keystone; either way the resulting arch is what the world, to Stallman's standing and not unreasonable irritation, calls simply "Linux".

Second, the timing — which is to say, other people's misfortunes. Survey the field in nineteen ninety-two with the eyes of a talented young programmer wanting a free Unix on cheap hardware. Berkeley's system: magnificent, mature — and under a lawsuit whose outcome no one could predict; who builds a house on disputed land? And the disputed land had a specific, poignant tenant: a Berkeley couple named Bill and Lynne Jolitz had spent years porting Berkeley Unix to the same cheap three-eighty-six machines, publishing the entire odyssey as a magazine serial, and released their free three-eighty-six BSD mere months after Torvalds's first tarball. On paper it was no contest — two decades of Berkeley engineering against a student's term project. But the Jolitzes' system ran under the lawsuit's shadow, its releases came slowly and its authors held them close, and the crowd that gathered around it splintered within the year into camps that became FreeBSD and NetBSD — inheriting, along with the code, the old Berkeley instinct for the curated release over the open bazaar. The Unix historians' verdict, with which I concur, is that Linux did not beat BSD on the merits; it beat BSD on availability — of the code, of the process, of the licence's promises — during the eighteen months when availability was everything. The Hurd: pedigreed, promised — and perpetually next year. Commercial Unix: thousands of dollars, on workstations costing tens of thousands. MINIX: available, but its author held it deliberately simple, for teaching, declining the patches that would make it a production system — the very patches a horde of newsgroup readers was aching to write. Linux was unencumbered, alive, hungry, and there. The talent flowed downhill into it like water.

Third — and this is the one the professor could least have been expected to grade — the development model. Torvalds turned out to possess, alongside adequate taste in kernels, a genius for a thing nobody had ever quite done at scale: running a software project as an open bazaar. Release early; release often — sometimes more than once a day in the hot years. Accept patches from anyone on merit and mood. Let a hierarchy of trusted lieutenants condense out of the mailing list by demonstrated competence rather than appointment. Treat forking as a civil right and re-merging as the reward for being right. The essayist Eric Raymond later framed the contrast as the cathedral and the bazaar — the closed workshop releasing polished stone against the noisy market that outbuilds it — and whatever the essay's simplifications, the phenomenon was real and Torvalds was its first great conductor. Given enough eyeballs, went the era's slogan — Linus's law, the essayist christened it — all bugs are shallow. You met the refutation of the strong version of that claim in the pilot — the flaw disclosed as Dirty COW, a race in the kernel's copy-on-write machinery that sat in plain sight, in code thousands had read, for over a decade — but the weak version moved the world: a bazaar with thousands of stallholders ships more, faster, than any workshop, and what it ships, its users can fix.

I owe you one aside here, because it completes the portrait and you use the result daily. The bazaar ran for a decade on mailing lists and tarballs, then on a proprietary tool whose licence eventually became intolerable to the community's principles; and in two thousand and five, in the ensuing crisis, Torvalds sat down and wrote, in a matter of weeks, a version control system to run his bazaar — distributed like the bazaar itself, every clone sovereign, merging cheap because merging was the daily bread. He named it git, British slang for an unpleasant person, and remarked that he names all his projects after himself. Two bad-tempered fortnights of work, and the tool now underlies essentially all of the world's software collaboration, our own repository included. Some men are quotable; Torvalds is forkable.

By the mid-nineties the hobby had distributions, and the word deserves a moment because the institution it names is peculiar to our house. A kernel is not a system; sir knows this from the pilot's long boot. Somebody must marry the kernel to the GNU userland, a package manager, an installer, and ten thousand decisions of arrangement — and in our world that somebody is not the kernel's author but a separate estate entirely. The earliest packagings were literally stacks of diskettes assembled by enthusiasts; out of one neglected such effort a Moorhead student named Patrick Volkerding tidied Slackware, which survives to this day as a kind of living museum of the old ways, administered by hand and proud of it. Weeks later Ian Murdock founded Debian — the name splicing his own with his girlfriend Debra's, romance immortalised in a package repository — and wrote a manifesto declaring that a distribution could be built as a commons: no owner, a constitution, a social contract with its users. Red Hat took the other road: a company, a product, and eventually the discovery that one could sell, for real money, not the free software itself but certainty about it — support, certification, a decade of unchanging interfaces for enterprises that upgrade with glacial dignity. When Red Hat floated on the stock market in the giddy summer of nineteen ninety-nine its shares did things that made the newspapers; the bazaar had acquired a ticker symbol. The commonwealth and the corporation remain the two poles of our world — your Ubuntu is a corporate cultivar of Debian's commons, which is a sentence containing most of the politics of modern Linux — and the tension between them, productive and permanent, is the bazaar's own governance writ large.

By the late nineties, in any case, the bet on freedom was being called. And that war deserves its own telling.

Part Five — The Other Houses, and the War for the Server Room

Because while the student was assembling his bazaar, the towers were building too, and to understand the shape of the war you must see what they built.

In Redmond: Microsoft, owner of the desktop through DOS and Windows, knew perfectly well that its foundations were sand — a single-user system with no memory protection worth the name, the division of the processor into a privileged kernel mode and an unprivileged user mode — the pilot's two humours — barely enforced. In nineteen eighty-eight it hired Dave Cutler from Digital Equipment — the architect of VMS, the great commercial rival of Unix, a man whose contempt for Unix was open and of long standing — and set his team to build a proper kernel: portable, secure, pre-emptive, written new from the ground. Windows NT shipped in nineteen ninety-three, and I shall say now what partisans of our house rarely say: it was, and is, a serious operating system. Its inheritance is VMS, not Unix — you can hear the accent in its asynchronous input and output, its priority scheme, its device stack; the pilot's remark that NT has no fork, and builds processes empty rather than duplicating them, is VMS talking — and the old joke that the letters W-N-T stand one alphabetical step after V-M-S, as HAL stood one step before IBM, is an observation Cutler's team enjoyed rather than planted. NT took the desktop's revenue and came for the server room.

In Cupertino, meanwhile: catastrophe, resolved by shopping. Apple spent the nineties failing to build a modern operating system — the project was called Copland, and its collapse is a business-school parable — and in december nineteen ninety-six it bought, for something over four hundred million dollars, the company Steve Jobs had founded in exile: NeXT. NeXT's system, NeXTSTEP, was a Unix — Berkeley userland riding on the Mach microkernel, the very Mach of the Hurd's dreams, with an object-oriented layer above it of genuine brilliance. That purchase returned Jobs to Apple, and NeXTSTEP, renamed and re-skinned over five patient years, shipped in two thousand and one as Mac OS X. Sit with that, sir: the machine on which so much modern software is written is Berkeley Unix wearing a polo neck, running on the microkernel that GNU could never finish, sold by the company Apple bought instead of the elegant also-ran BeOS — whose partisans mourn it yet, and whose orphaned ideas, one of them called Binder, will resurface inside your telephone in Episode Eleven. The seventies never ended; they were merely acquired.

So: the war for the server room, at the century's turn. NT advancing with the desktop's treasury behind it; commercial Unix — Sun above all — defending the high ground at high margins; and up through the floorboards, unbudgeted and unstoppable, the bazaar. The web was the accelerant. Every request for a page needed a server; the server everyone reached for was Apache, free, on Linux, free, on the same cheap Intel boxes Windows ran on; the acronym LAMP — Linux, Apache, the MySQL database, the scripting languages — named the standard kit from which the entire first internet economy was assembled. Netscape opened its browser's source in nineteen ninety-eight and made the bazaar respectable dinner-table conversation; leaked Microsoft strategy memoranda from that Halloween — the Halloween documents, as the trade promptly christened them — conceded, in private, that the free systems were a threat the company could not out-feature, and mused about fighting them by entangling the open protocols — embrace, extend, extinguish, and the third word was the strategy. IBM, with the timing of an old fighter who knows when the crowd has turned, announced at the millennium that it would put a billion dollars behind Linux — the establishment's seal on the bazaar. Sun wobbled between embracing and suing and did not survive the indecision as an independent house. And Microsoft's chief executive, in two thousand and one, delivered the "cancer" verdict I quoted earlier — the sound, we can now say, of a tower discovering that the tide does not negotiate.

And since every act of this drama has featured lawyers, the war got its lawsuit too, and it is the comic relief. In two thousand and three the remnant of SCO — the Santa Cruz name from the Xenix days, by then a husk holding what it believed were the old AT&T Unix rights — sued IBM for billions, alleging that Linux was stuffed with misappropriated Unix code, and for good measure warned thousands of corporate Linux users that they owed it licence fees. For a season the industry held its breath; Microsoft, in what observers were too polite to call a coincidence, bought a SCO licence it did not obviously need. Then the case fell apart in slow motion, magnificently. The infringing code could not be produced in court. And midway through, a judgement in a parallel case pulled the floor away entirely — and the chain of title deserves to be walked slowly, sir, because it is the whole joke. AT&T, you recall, had once owned Unix outright. In the early nineties it sold the Unix business to Novell. Novell, a few years later, sold that business on to Santa Cruz — but the contract, read closely in court, had conveyed the business and retained the copyrights. Novell still owned them. SCO, all along, had never owned the copyrights it was suing over at all. The plaintiff had spent years suing the world over property that turned out, on inspection of the deed, not to be his. The husk went bankrupt; the claims shambled on through the courts for years after, a lawsuit outliving its own corpse; and the practical effect on Linux was the opposite of the intent — enterprises that had hesitated now had a court-tested answer, and the last legal cloud over the commons burnt off for good. The house that lawyers accidentally built, lawyers deliberately failed to demolish. There is a symmetry in that I decline to spoil with further comment.

You know the ending in any case, because you administer it. Linux took the server room so completely that the victory stopped being news; took the embedded world; took, via Android, the telephone in your pocket — four billion or so instances, the largest deployment of any kernel in history, wearing a userland we shall examine with raised eyebrows in due course; took the supercomputer list to the last entry; took the cloud, which is to say became the substance the cloud is made of. The desktop it never took, a defeat with instructive causes we shall touch when we tour the drawing room in Episode Nine. And then the coda that no one in nineteen ninety-two could have written without being escorted from the building: Microsoft, under new management and honest accounting — the majority of its own cloud's rented machines running Linux — declared its love, joined the kernel's contributors, and in twenty nineteen shipped, inside Windows itself, a genuine Linux kernel, so that the heirs of NT now carry the student's hobby about with them as standard equipment.

As for the professor: no unkind fate befell him, and one small vindication should be entered in the minutes. Tanenbaum went on, in the middle two-thousands, to rebuild MINIX as a genuinely self-healing microkernel system — drivers that crash and are resurrected by a supervising server without the machine noticing — and thereby to demonstrate in the laboratory everything he had claimed in the flame war. Where that demonstration ended up — inside whose silicon, running in how many hundred million machines, unknown even to its author — is the very hook on which Episode Eleven hangs, and I shall not bleed the joke tonight. Let the record show merely that in the long quarrel between the professor and the student, history, with uncharacteristic diplomacy, arranged for both to win — and took care that neither victory looked anything like the one its owner had argued for.

How Windows came to ship Linux — and what it tried first, and why the first attempt is the best process-model lesson money can buy — is precisely where we open next week.

The Books of the Evening

Let us balance the accounts.

The house was raised by accident four times over. An antitrust decree made Unix a gift because it could not be a product; a lawsuit froze the gift's finest heirs at the exact moment a successor was needed; a cathedral was built complete but for its keystone, on a doctrine too elegant to finish; and a student in a bedroom, aiming at a terminal program, supplied the keystone under the one licence that guaranteed nobody could ever take it away. Talent mattered; Thompson's taste, Ritchie's language, Stallman's legal machine, Torvalds's bazaar. But at every fork of history it was licences, lawsuits, price and timing that chose which talent won. Architecture proposed; economics disposed. Keep that maxim, sir — it is the season's refrain, and Episode Eleven will test it against every house on the street.

One debt stands open on tonight's page: the microkernel question — whether the professor's doctrine was wrong, or merely early, or quietly victorious in disguise — payable when we tour the other houses. And one small joy to carry out: the next time you type the two letters of the little command that lists a directory — the one whose journey the pilot followed through the whole house — know that you are operating, in direct unbroken descent, the attic machine of nineteen sixty-nine — the hose-segments of Doug McIlroy screwed together exactly as preached, on a kernel graded insufficient by its examiner, distributed under a licence written by a prophet, running on hardware the whole arrangement was once too grand to notice.

Next week, sir: the process, done properly — the fork window revisited with adult intent, the loader in full, Microsoft's doomed and heroic attempt to teach NT to fork, and the reason every application on an Android telephone is the same process wearing four billion masks.

Until then, sir.
