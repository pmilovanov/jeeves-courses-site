Episode Eight — Locks, Keys, and Burglars

The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux

Five Hundred Milliseconds to Midnight

Good day, sir.

In late March of twenty twenty-four, a database engineer at Microsoft named Andres Freund was running performance tests on a Debian machine when he noticed something a less exacting man would have shrugged at: logging in over ssh was taking about half a second longer than it ought. Five hundred milliseconds — an eternity to a man who benchmarks databases for a living, and an irritation calibrated precisely to catch one. He looked closer. The ssh daemon was burning odd amounts of processor before authentication. A memory-checking tool threw errors inside a compression library that had no earthly business being interesting. He kept pulling the thread, on his own time, out of professional annoyance — and what came out of the wall was the nearest miss in the history of computer security.

The compression library was xz: a quiet, universal utility, the sort of infrastructure nobody thinks about, linked into a great deal of the Linux world's plumbing. Its two most recent releases contained a back door. Not a bug — a door, installed with extraordinary craft. When the library found itself loaded into an ssh daemon — which happens on the major distributions through a chain of intermediaries — it quietly rerouted the daemon's signature-checking machinery, so that a connection presenting credentials signed with one particular private key, and only that key, would be granted the ability to run commands as root before authentication. A master key to essentially every Linux server on Earth that shipped those versions, held by whoever, somewhere, owned the matching secret.

And the installation of that door, sir, is what earns the affair its place in this syllabus, because it was not a hack of any computer. It was a hack of the bazaar itself.

The library's founding maintainer, Lasse Collin, had kept it for years — alone, unpaid, and, by his own account in that period, under strain. Into his mailing list around twenty twenty-one came a helpful new contributor under the name Jia Tan: patient, competent, unfailingly courteous, tireless. And alongside — the detail that chills every practitioner who reads it — came pressure: other correspondents, never seen before or since, complaining that the maintainer was too slow, that patches languished, that the project needed fresher hands. A claque, in retrospect almost certainly the same operation, manufacturing the social conditions for a succession. The weary maintainer, harried by strangers and grateful for the one tireless helper, did the human thing. He shared the keys, and then largely ceded them. By twenty twenty-three, Jia Tan was cutting the releases.

The door followed — and it was hidden where nobody was looking. Not in the source code that anyone could read on the project's public repository, but in the build machinery: an innocuous test file, nominally a sample of corrupt compressed data, from which subtly amended build scripts — present only in the packaged release bundles, never in the repository one browsed — extracted and assembled the payload during compilation. Read every line of the source and you would find nothing. The treachery lived in the making.

Which is precisely where Ken Thompson told us to look, forty-one years earlier. Episode One booked that debt: his lecture on trusting trust, the demonstration that a compiler can be taught to insert a back door into every program it builds — including its own successor — whereupon the treachery vanishes from all source code everywhere and survives only in the binary. You cannot trust code you did not create yourself, he concluded; total trust rests on the tools, and the tools' tools, all the way down. Somebody has now run that play at civilisation scale, against a volunteer, and very nearly won.

The ending you know from the opening. The door reached the testing channels of the major distributions — the bleeding edge, not yet the stable servers — and was caught, weeks short of universality, because the payload's own start-up cost annoyed exactly one man with a profiler and the temperament to chase half a second. The industry's honest verdict was two words: we got lucky.

Luck is not a security architecture. Tonight, the architecture: the four-decade war over memory corruption and the defences now riding invisibly in every process sir owns — two of which this season has already handed him unlabelled; the house's inner doors, and the retirement of the cargo cult that switches them off; the treachery of the silicon itself, reopened from the pilot with its mechanics this time; and last, the supply lines — Thompson's debt paid in full, and the uncomfortable economics of a civilisation resting on unpaid volunteers. Witnesses throughout: the telephones, which quietly rebuilt themselves around a threat model the desktop still declines to admit.

To the wall, sir.

Part One — The Forty Years' War

Date the modern era from an evening in November nineteen eighty-eight, when the internet's few tens of thousands of machines began falling over in unison. The cause was a self-replicating program loosed by a Cornell graduate student with more curiosity than judgement, and among its several routes of travel was tonight's ancestor: it fed a network service more input than that service's buffer had room for.

Understand what that means in the house's own terms, because the whole war follows from it. A program's working stack holds, side by side, the local buffers a function is using and the bookkeeping the machine needs to get back where it came from — in particular, the return address: the note saying where to resume when this function finishes. Write past the end of a buffer and you are not merely spoiling data; you are, a few bytes later, overwriting the note. And a function's polite return then becomes a jump to wherever the attacker's overflow said. Deliver code in the same input, aim the note at it, and the machine executes the burglar's instructions with the service's own credentials. The technique was systematised for a general audience in a nineteen ninety-six article in the underground journal Phrack under a title that became the most consequential tutorial ever published — on smashing the stack, for fun and for profit — and for a decade thereafter it simply owned the world.

The defences arrived in layers, each provoking its own countermeasure, and the ladder is worth climbing rung by rung because sir is standing on all of it right now.

The canary. Since the return note lies beyond the buffers, place a secret random value between them, and have every function check that the value is intact before trusting its note. An overflow that ploughed through to the note must have crushed the canary en route; the program halts with its dignity rather than dancing to the burglar's tune. Compilers have emitted this since the late nineties, and it is on by default in everything your distribution ships. And where does the secret come from? Episode Two told you without telling you: the kernel's hall-table letter to every new process carries a few bytes of fresh randomness, delivered gratis at exec. That is the canary's egg, laid before the program's first instruction runs.

No execution. The classical attack ran code out of a data buffer, so once the processors could express the distinction, memory pages were marked writable or executable and never both — the pilot's page tables earning their keep again. Stack and heap lose the right to run; delivered code becomes inert cargo.

The conscription of the innocent. The burglars' answer was the cleverest idea of the era: if you cannot bring code, use the code already present. The victim's own program and libraries are full of short instruction sequences ending in a return — a handful of useful operations, then a jump back to whatever the stack says. Overwrite the stack not with code but with a list of addresses of such fragments, and each fragment's return feeds the next: a program assembled entirely from the victim's own dismembered parts, executing the burglar's intent without a single byte of foreign code ever running. The technique was formalised in two thousand and seven under the name return-oriented programming, and its scholarly title — on the geometry of innocent flesh on the bone — tells you the authors knew exactly how macabre a thing they had found. Its arrival meant that no-execute alone was never going to be enough.

Randomisation. Which is the reply: if the attack is a list of addresses, make the addresses unknowable. Address space layout randomisation — pioneered by an out-of-tree hardening project and mainlined in the middle two-thousands — shuffles the placement of the stack, the heap, the libraries, and, for programs built for it, the executable itself, afresh on every exec. The burglar's carefully assembled list of fragment addresses is valid for one process on one run of one machine, and worthless everywhere else. The kernel randomises its own layout too, for the same reason and against the same attacks one storey down. Randomisation does not close the hole; it converts a reliable exploit into a lottery, which — combined with the crash-and-restart behaviour of most services — is often enough. The counter-counter-measure, inevitably, is the information leak: a separate bug that discloses one real address, from which the whole shuffled map can be reconstructed, which is why apparently harmless leaks are treated with such gravity by people who do this for a living.

The hardware joins in. The current rung is enforcement in silicon. Recent processors keep a shadow stack — a second, protected copy of return addresses that ordinary writes cannot reach — and check the two against each other on every return; a corrupted note is caught by the machine itself. Others cryptographically sign pointers, so that a forged address fails its signature check. Control-flow integrity, in compiler and processor together, is the present frontier: the observation, arrived at after forty years, that the correct defence is not to guess which writes are malicious but to make the program's legitimate control flow explicit and refuse everything else.

Two morals before we go inside. First: not one of those layers fixes the underlying fault, which is that the languages the house is written in permit a program to write past the end of a buffer at all. Every rung of that ladder is a mitigation — a raising of cost — and the reason the industry is presently so exercised about memory-safe languages is that thirty-odd years of mitigation have arrived, by common consent of the people who count vulnerabilities, at about seventy per cent of serious flaws are still memory-safety flaws. Episode Twelve will meet the consequence in the kernel's own most divisive project of the decade. And second, the shape of the whole war: every defence was answered, every answer was defended, and the ladder ratchets in one direction only. Security below stairs is not a state to be achieved, sir; it is a rent to be paid.

Part Two — The Inner Doors

Now the house's own locks, and the doctrine behind the one sir has been advised, by a thousand forum posts, to switch off.

The classical Unix permissions of the pilot — owner, group, others; read, write, execute — are what the trade calls discretionary access control, and the word carries the whole limitation. The owner of a file has discretion over it: he may open its permissions to the world, and the system will help him do it. Which means the system's security is the sum of every user's judgement, and — far worse — that a program running as you has all your authority. Your web browser can read your private keys. Your text editor can post your documents to the internet. Not because anyone permitted it specifically, but because permission is attached to you, and the program is wearing your coat.

Mandatory access control inverts that. Policy is set centrally, by the administrator, and no user's discretion can override it: this program, in this role, may touch these files and no others, whatever its owner wishes. The idea comes out of defence work — the multi-level security tradition — and it arrived in our house by a route that tells you something about the house's governance. When the American National Security Agency offered its implementation for the kernel in the early two-thousands, Torvalds declined to bless any single security model, and instead required that a framework be built: a set of hooks throughout the kernel at every point where a security decision is made, against which any number of rival modules might be written. The Linux Security Modules framework is the diplomatic solution to a doctrinal quarrel — the house refusing to legislate on security philosophy, and providing sockets for whichever philosophy the administrator prefers.

Two philosophies duly occupied it. SELinux — the agency's own — labels everything: every file, every process, every socket carries a security context, and policy is written in terms of those labels. What may this type of process do to that type of file? The strength is that the policy follows the object rather than its name: rename the file, move it, hard-link it, and its label goes with it. The weakness is that the policy language is genuinely difficult, and a mislabelled file produces a denial that looks, from above stairs, like insanity — the permissions are plainly correct, the owner is plainly right, and the operation is plainly refused. AppArmor — the rival, favoured by SUSE and Ubuntu — takes the pragmatic road: policy by path. This program may read these directories and write those. Vastly easier to author and reason about; and structurally weaker, since paths are not identities and the equivalence of two names for one file, which Episode Five taught you to expect, is exactly the seam a determined burglar works at.

So: the cargo cult, retired. When a service is denied a file it plainly owns, and the ordinary permissions are plainly in order, the mandatory layer is nearly always the author of the difficulty — and the received remedy is to disable it, which is roughly the security equivalent of removing the smoke alarm because it keeps going off while you cook. What the mechanism actually offers is an audit trail: every denial is logged, with the subject's label, the object's label, and the permission refused — which is to say the log contains, in every case, the exact sentence of policy that is missing. The tooling will read those denials and generate the rule for you. The distinction I would press on sir is that between permissive mode — where the policy is evaluated and denials logged but nothing is actually blocked, which is the correct instrument for diagnosis — and disabled, which is the correct instrument for never learning anything again. Ten minutes in permissive mode with the audit log will tell you precisely which label is wrong; and the reason to spend those ten minutes is that this layer is, in practice, the thing that most often converts a compromised service into a contained compromised service.

Which brings the telephones to the stand, and they have the strongest testimony of the evening. Android runs SELinux in enforcing mode, across the entire system, for every application, and has done for over a decade — a scale of mandatory access control deployment that the server world has never come close to. And beneath it sits the more radical decision, taken at the platform's birth: every application is a different user. Android took the pilot's oldest, dullest machinery — the numeric user identifier, invented for time-sharing systems where the users were people — and repurposed it wholesale, giving each installed application its own identity and thus its own private storage, invisible to every other application by the ordinary permission rules of nineteen seventy. The great isolation problem of the personal computer — that all your programs run as you, and can therefore do anything you can — was solved on the telephone by the simple expedient of declaring that they are not you. Add per-application sandboxes, seccomp filters of the pilot's kind, and an entitlement system on the Apple side where every sensitive capability must be declared in advance, signed, and granted, and you have the reason the security posture of a mid-range telephone is, by any honest reckoning, considerably better than that of the workstation on which it is developed.

The desktops' own answer has been slower and is instructive precisely because it is incomplete: the sandboxed application formats — the packaging systems that run a program inside the pilot's namespaces with a declared set of permissions — plus the portal mechanism that lets a confined program ask, through a broker, for the one file the user chose rather than the whole filesystem. It is the telephone's model arriving on the desktop by a different road, twenty years late, and hobbled by the fact that the desktop must keep running four decades of software that assumes it may read everything. Episode Ten will show you the same argument fought out over the screen itself.

Part Three — The Treachery of the Silicon

The pilot introduced Meltdown and Spectre as the reason system calls got dearer in twenty eighteen. They deserve their mechanics, because they broke something the whole season has been standing on.

Modern processors are not the orderly clerks of the textbook. To keep their pipelines fed they speculate: on reaching a branch they predict which way it will go and race ahead down the predicted path, doing real work — loading memory, computing — before the condition is even resolved. If the guess was right, the work is banked and time is saved. If wrong, the results are discarded and the processor rewinds. This has been the foundation of processor performance for thirty years, and its correctness argument was always: the discarded work is invisible, therefore it does not matter.

It was not invisible. The discarded work leaves a trace — in the caches. A speculative load that turned out to be forbidden is rolled back architecturally, but the cache line it fetched remains warm, and a program can measure warmth by timing its own subsequent accesses. Meltdown used this to read kernel memory from an ordinary program: speculate past a permission check, use the forbidden byte to select which of two hundred and fifty-six cache lines to touch, then time all of them and see which is warm. The forbidden byte is recovered, one byte at a time, from a mechanism that architecturally never happened. Spectre, the deeper family, does the same trick by training the branch predictor so that some other victim — the kernel, another process, a sandboxed script's host — speculates its way past its own bounds check on the attacker's behalf.

The two consequences are worth stating plainly. The first, practical: the mitigation for Meltdown was the pilot's page-table separation, and its cost fell on every boundary crossing — which is the direct ancestor of Episode Six's amortised bell rope and Episode Two's cheaper batching. A hardware defect from nineteen ninety-five arrived in twenty eighteen and reshaped the software architecture of the following decade. The second, philosophical, and this is why I give it a part of its own: the boundary the entire house is built on turned out to be an architectural fiction that the implementation did not honour. Every wall in this season — the two humours, the page tables, the sandboxes, the containers, the virtual machines — is enforced by hardware that, it emerged, leaks across those walls through channels its own specification did not describe. The industry's response has been a permanent tax: mitigations in microcode, in compilers, in kernels, costing a few per cent here and rather more there, forever; and a new and now-standard question in the design of anything privileged, which is what does this leak in time?

The wider family deserves a sentence, because it did not stop with speculation. The house's silicon has proved leaky in other directions: memory cells so densely packed that hammering one row can flip a bit in its neighbour — a fault injected by mere reading, which was demonstrated to yield privilege escalation and has since become a standing consideration in memory design. The moral, sir, is not that computers are hopeless. It is that abstractions are load-bearing until they are not, and that below the lowest layer this course has toured, there is another one, made of physics, with its own bugs.

Part Four — The Supply Lines

Which returns us to the mailing list where we began, and to the debt from Episode One, now payable in full.

The xz affair was not an isolated stroke of malice; it was the sharpest instance of a structural condition the trade has been documenting, with increasing alarm, for a decade. Consider the ancestry. In twenty fourteen, Heartbleed: a flaw in OpenSSL — the library encrypting a very large share of all web traffic — that let any client read chunks of a server's private memory, keys included. When the world went looking for the organisation responsible, it found a project sustained by a handful of people and a budget best described as embarrassing. Six months later, Shellshock: a flaw in the Bourne-again shell — the pilot's own bash, from Episode One's cathedral — that had sat quietly for twenty-five years and could be triggered, on many systems, straight off the internet through a web server's environment variables, chaining the season's own machinery (Episode Two's environment, inherited at exec) into a remote command execution. And in twenty twenty-one, Log-four-Shell: a logging library used by essentially every Java application in existence, in which the act of writing a log line containing attacker-chosen text could be made to fetch and execute code from the network. Christmas was cancelled that year for a large part of the profession.

The cartoon everyone circulated in the aftermath is the honest architecture diagram of our field: a towering, precarious edifice of modern digital infrastructure, all of whose weight rests on one small brick, captioned as a project some unregarded person in Nebraska has been thanklessly maintaining since two thousand and three. It is funny because it is a specification.

Now, the intelligent objection, which sir may already be forming: does this not simply indict the bazaar? Episode One celebrated the model — code open, contributions from anyone, trust by demonstrated competence — and here is that same openness serving as the attack surface. I want to answer that carefully, because the glib answers are both wrong.

The glib pro-bazaar answer is that many eyes make bugs shallow. Tonight's evidence refutes it directly: the many eyes did not find this; one man's profiler did, by accident, and only because the door was slightly slow. The glib anti-bazaar answer is that proprietary development would have prevented it. That is refuted just as directly: a patient operative acquiring maintainership of an under-resourced project is a strategy that works at least as well against a company — indeed the identical strategy, applied to employees, is the oldest one in espionage; and closed development would have removed the one property that actually saved us, which is that once suspicion existed, the entire history — every commit, every mailing list message, every release artefact — was public and reviewable, and the affair was fully anatomised by strangers within days. The correct verdict is narrower and less comfortable than either slogan: the bazaar's transparency is what made the forensics instant and the remediation global, and the bazaar's economics — critical infrastructure maintained by unpaid, unsupported, exhausted individuals — is what made the attack cheap. Openness was the defence. Under-investment was the vulnerability. They are separate variables, and the industry spent thirty years enjoying the first while declining to pay for the second.

What is actually being done falls into three honest categories, and I shall not oversell any of them.

Making the build trustworthy. The reproducible builds movement — a serious, decade-old engineering programme — aims at the property that anyone may take the published source, build it, and obtain a bit-for-bit identical artefact to the one shipped. Achieve that and the xz trick dies at the root: the release bundle can no longer differ from the repository without anybody noticing, because everybody's build would disagree. Several distributions are now most of the way there. It is the closest thing the field has produced to an answer to Thompson's lecture — not a refutation, since the compiler itself remains in the trust chain, but a way of making divergence visible, which is the practical part.

Knowing what one is running. The bills of materials now mandated in various jurisdictions — an inventory of every component inside a piece of software — are widely mocked as compliance theatre, and are frequently exactly that. But the Log-four-Shell fortnight, in which a substantial fraction of the world's engineers could not answer the question do we run this library, and where, made the case that the mockery cannot: you cannot patch what you cannot enumerate.

Paying the volunteers. After Heartbleed, an industry consortium was formed to fund critical infrastructure; after xz, the funding bodies multiplied — a German public fund for sovereign technology, an industry programme pairing money with security engineering for the most critical projects, and rather more corporate attention to the question of which of their dependencies is one exhausted maintainer away from an incident. It is genuine, it is far too small, and it is the only structural fix on the list. Every other measure raises the attacker's cost; this one raises the defender's capacity, which is the variable that was actually missing on that mailing list in twenty twenty-one.

The Books of the Evening

Let us balance them, sir.

The war over memory is forty years old and ratchets one way: the note on the stack overwritten; the canary laid by the kernel at exec; execution withdrawn from data; the burglar conscripting the victim's own instruction fragments; the map shuffled on every run; and now the silicon itself checking returns and signing pointers — every rung a raising of cost, none of them a cure, and seventy per cent of serious flaws still of this one family. The inner doors are mandatory where the classical permissions are discretionary, mounted on a framework the house built rather than pick a philosophy; the denial in your log is a missing sentence of policy, not an enemy, and permissive mode is the instrument that reads it aloud. The telephones went furthest — every application its own user, mandatory policy enforced fleet-wide, capabilities declared and signed — solving by decree the desktop's oldest hole, that your programs wear your coat. The silicon proved treacherous beneath every wall the season has toured, and the tax is permanent. And the supply lines are the soft flank: the door hidden in the making, exactly where Thompson said to look; transparency the reason we could dissect it in days, under-investment the reason it was cheap to attempt; reproducible builds, honest inventories, and — the only structural repair — actually paying the people holding the bricks.

Episode One's debt is discharged. The microkernel question remains outstanding, at compound interest, for Episode Eleven.

Next week we leave the cellars for the drawing room, and the mood lightens considerably. The pilot never once explained how a picture reaches your screen — and the answer turns out to be a system designed at MIT in the middle nineteen-eighties for a world of expensive shared machines and cheap terminals, whose central architectural wager was that drawing should happen over a network, and which is still, four decades on, running underneath a great many of the machines listening to this. How X came to be, why its client-and-server arrangement is confusingly backwards, why every window on your screen was for thirty years permitted to read every keystroke you typed into every other, and how the other houses — Windows drawing through its message pump, NeXT drawing in PostScript, a Finnish telephone drawing a whole interface in eight megabytes — did it differently.

Until then, sir.

— End of Episode Eight.
