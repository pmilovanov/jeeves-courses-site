# Episode Eleven — Other Houses, and Houses Within Houses

*The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux*

## A Letter of Thanks

Good day, sir.

In November of twenty seventeen, Professor Andrew Tanenbaum of the Free University of Amsterdam — sir will remember him from the very first episode of this season, seated at his terminal in January of nineteen ninety-two, grading a Finnish student's kernel and finding it obsolete — published an open letter to the Intel Corporation. Its tone was that of a man determined to be gracious about an extraordinary discovery, and making sure the world watched him being gracious about it.

The discovery was this. Security researchers had been excavating the Intel Management Engine — a small, secretive computer that lives *inside* the chipset of essentially every Intel machine built in the last decade and a half: its own processor, its own memory, its own network access, running before the machine boots, running after it shuts down, running whether the machine's owner knows it or not, with a station below the operating system and out of its sight. Sir's Linux servers have one. Sir's laptop has one. And inside that hidden second computer, the excavators found, Intel had quietly installed — MINIX. The professor's own teaching system, the little microkernel he had written so that students could read an entire operating system in a term; the system whose licence he had, on principle, made permissive precisely so that anyone might do anything with it. Intel had taken him at his word, told him nothing, and shipped his classroom exercise inside very nearly every computer on Earth. The letter's finest sentence observed, with the driest possible courtesy, that it would have been nice if somebody had mentioned it; its finest fact was the arithmetic: by any honest count, MINIX was now probably the most widely deployed operating system in the world on such machines — ahead of Windows, ahead of macOS, ahead, sir, of Linux. The student's kernel had won the visible world; the professor's had, without his knowledge, won the invisible one underneath it.

I promised, nine episodes ago, that this debt would be paid in full. The genesis episode issued it in so many words: *your car believes in microkernels; so does the secret second computer that lives inside your computer* — the very Management Engine of tonight's opening — payable, its terms ran, in Episode Eleven, when we should tour the other houses. And behind the debt stands the argument of nineteen ninety-two, whose substance bears restating for the record: the professor read the Finnish student's brand-new kernel and pronounced it, in a public posting titled exactly this, *obsolete* — obsolete because it was *monolithic*, one program holding every power in one address space, when the future, he ruled, belonged to the *microkernel*. Tonight we discover how that argument *actually* came out — not as folklore, in which the professor simply lost, but as engineering, in which the verdict is stranger and far more instructive. Tonight is that night. We shall state the microkernel case fairly and price it honestly; follow Mach through tragedy into the biggest estate in consumer computing; visit the message-passing house that ships in sir's motor car, the kernel that was mathematically proved, the fortress at Redmond read without prejudice, the telephone empires that rose on eight megabytes and fell on plenty; take apart Android — the deepest study of our own kernel wearing entirely foreign dress; and finish inside the houses within houses, where the cloud rents its rooms and the old argument returns wearing a hypervisor's uniform. The thesis of the evening I shall state now and prove by tour: **architecture proposes; economics disposes.**

## Part One — The Doctrine, and the Tragedy of Mach

State the professor's case properly, because the pilot's cartoon of it will not survive this episode. The microkernel doctrine says: the kernel — the one program with total power, where every fault is fatal and every line is trusted — should contain only what *cannot* live anywhere else. Address spaces. Threads and their scheduling. And a way for processes to send one another messages — *inter-process communication*, in the trade's term of art; *message-passing* for short, and both names will earn their keep tonight. Nothing more. Everything sir has spent ten episodes watching the kernel do — filesystems, network stacks, device drivers — becomes, in this doctrine, a flock of ordinary processes: each in its own address space, each holding only the privileges of its office, each speaking to the others by message. The promises are real and sir can price them by now. A driver that crashes is a *process* that crashes: restart it, as you would any misbehaving service — no kernel panic, no boot loop. The tradesmen's episode alone is half an argument for the doctrine — device drivers, that episode testified, being the bulk of the kernel's code and the birthplace of most of its faults, foreign workmanship living below stairs with full privileges — and the CrowdStrike affair the other half: one malformed file, fed overnight to a security driver resident in the kernels of some eight and a half million Windows machines, and by morning the airlines, the banks, and the broadcasters were down with them. Least privilege by construction — the filesystem server cannot touch the network card's registers, because it has no such mapping. And a kernel small enough to *read* — or, as we shall see, to prove.

The price is equally concrete, and sir has already paid it in miniature, in the episode on concurrency and the episode on the bell rope's cost — the bell rope being, I remind sir, the *system call*: the one gate through which any program asks its kernel for any service, each pull paid for in a crossing between the program's world and the kernel's. A monolithic kernel's subsystems call one another the way functions call functions: a jump, shared memory, done. Make them separate processes and every such call becomes at least one round of message-passing — which means crossings between address spaces, scheduler involvement, and the quiet tax we met in the memory episode: caches and *translation lookaside buffers* — the processor's small store of recently translated addresses — repeatedly flushed of one context to make room for another. Let me walk one errand through that toll slowly, the cast announced before anything moves. Three players: your program, above stairs; the filesystem, now an ordinary process; the disk driver, a third process beside it. Your program wants one block of a file. It sends its request to the filesystem process — that is one crossing of address spaces, and the scheduler must now bring the filesystem on stage. The filesystem does not hold the block; it sends a request of its own to the driver process — a second crossing, a second change of players. The driver fetches the block and replies to the filesystem — a third. The filesystem replies to your program — a fourth. Four crossings, four flushings of those caches, for one block of one file — where the monolith would have spent a single system call and, within the kernel, plain function calls. Multiply by every disk block, every packet, every keystroke. The doctrine's whole fate, sir — hold this — turned on the size of that tax, and on whether anyone could shrink it.

Now, the tragedy in three acts. Mach — the name to conjure with — began at Carnegie Mellon in nineteen eighty-five, under Richard Rashid and, among others, a graduate student named Avie Tevanian; remember him. Mach set out to be the foundation beneath Unix: tasks, threads, and *ports* — message channels held like property, sendable, transferable, a genuinely beautiful abstraction sir has met in spirit already, for it is a *capability* — the locksmith's episode's good idea, worth its one-sentence restatement: a token that at once names a thing and confers the right to use it, unforgeable, passable from hand to hand, revocable by its issuer — here wearing a postal uniform. Act one: Mach with Unix still resident in the kernel worked and spread — respectable systems were built on it. Act two: the true microkernel — Unix expelled to userspace as a server, the doctrine finally practised as preached — arrived at the start of the nineties, and the tax came due: the messages were slow, the crossings many, the benchmarks unkind, and the trade's enthusiasm cooled measurably. The GNU project's Hurd — the free system's own kernel, sir will recall from the genesis episode: begun before Linux, unfinished decades after, the empty crossing in an otherwise completed cathedral, the very vacancy the Finnish student's kernel filled — chose exactly this design in exactly those years; no more need be said. Act three is the strange afterlife. Tevanian went to NeXT, and NeXT's system took Mach not as a microkernel but as a *foundation wall*: Mach's tasks, threads and ports, with the BSD organs moved back *into the same address space* — all the abstractions, none of the isolation, and therefore none of the tax. When Apple bought NeXT at the end of nineteen ninety-six, that hybrid — Mach and BSD sharing one roof, Tevanian now running Apple's software — became the core of Mac OS X, and it is the core of every Apple machine today: sir's colleague's MacBook, every iPhone in every pocket, all of them running a kernel whose deepest layer is Mach, whose security model to this day rests on Mach's port rights. The doctrine's flagship never conquered as a microkernel, sir. It moved back in with its parents — and inherited the biggest estate in consumer computing.

## Part Two — The Houses That Shipped It Anyway

But the folklore says the doctrine failed, and the folklore is wrong three times over, so let me show sir the houses where it shipped.

The first is in the driveway. QNX was founded in nineteen eighty by two graduates of the University of Waterloo, and it is the message-passing house that simply *worked*: a kernel of a few dozen kilobytes offering little beyond scheduling and synchronous message-passing — *send, receive, reply*, the rendezvous — with drivers, filesystems, network stacks all ordinary restartable processes, exactly as the doctrine prescribes. Those three words are the entire protocol, and they repay one slow walk with the cast named before anything moves. Two players: a client — the instrument cluster, say, wanting a sensor's reading — and the server process that owns it. The client *sends* its request, and stops dead: blocked, going nowhere until answered. The server *receives* the request, does the work, and *replies*. The reply wakes the client, which carries on as though returning from an ordinary function call. No queues, no mailboxes standing between them: the two meet, exchange, and part — hence the *rendezvous* — and the kernel's entire share of the business is to carry the message and swap which of the two stands on stage. Two things made QNX legend. The first was that the tax never sank it: synchronous message-passing, done with fanatical care on a small kernel, proved fast enough for *hard real time* — the world where a late answer is a wrong answer, which no general-purpose house we have toured will promise. The second was a party trick from nineteen ninety-nine that closed arguments for a decade: a single one-point-four-four megabyte diskette — sir remembers diskettes — that booted, on any ordinary PC of the day, into a complete graphical desktop: windowing system, text editor, TCP/IP networking, a web *browser* and a web *server*, all of it served by that tiny kernel and its flock of processes, from a floppy disk. The industry that could not fit its device drivers in forty times the space took note. Where QNX went was everywhere failure is unaffordable and nobody sees the computer: industrial control, rail, medical instruments, and above all the motor car — instrument clusters, driver assistance, the software that must not hiccup at speed — until today it rides in well over two hundred and fifty million vehicles. If sir's car was built in the last decade, the odds are excellent that the microkernel doctrine drove him home from the dealership. And in twenty ten, a fading telephone company in Waterloo bought the firm for its second act — a thread I shall pick up in the telephone part of our tour.

The second house is in the pocket — beside the monolith. In the middle two-thousands a German-Australian line of work descended from Jochen Liedtke's L-four kernels — written, on the page, as the bare letter L against the numeral four — Liedtke being the man who re-examined Mach's corpse and returned the verdict that changed the field: the tax was not the doctrine's essence but its *implementation*; a microkernel admitting only what it must, engineered without sentiment, could pass messages an order of magnitude and more faster than Mach ever did — found its way into Qualcomm's telephone radio chips. The signalling stack of a mobile telephone is another world where lateness is failure and certification is law, and there the L-four family quietly shipped by the *billion* — passing a billion and a half units a decade and a half ago and scarcely slowing since. Which yields my favourite sentence of the season so far, sir: for years, the typical smartphone carried both verdicts of the nineteen ninety-two debate in one casing — a monolithic Linux running the glamorous half, and a microkernel running the radio beside it — each occupying precisely the territory where its own economics win.

And the third — the proof, in the exact sense. In two thousand and nine an Australian team took a kernel of the L-four line, seL-four — printed as one word: a small s, a small e, a capital L, and the numeral four — all ten thousand-odd lines of it, and did something never before done for a general-purpose operating system kernel: they *proved it correct* — a machine-checked mathematical proof, from abstract specification down to the C implementation, that the code does what the specification says and nothing else. No proof of that kind will ever be written for Linux or for Windows; at tens of millions of lines the question is not hard but meaningless. Only a kernel small enough to state could be proved, which is to say: only a microkernel. It ships today where the stakes are the grimmest — defence, aviation, the places that do not hold press events. And the professor's own MINIX, reborn in two thousand and five as a serious research system — complete with a *reincarnation server*, a process whose office is to notice dead drivers and quietly restart them, resurrection as a system service — found, as we heard in tonight's opening, the strangest berth of all: chosen by Intel, for its smallness and its permissive licence, to run the hidden second computer inside the first one. Below every Linux machine on such silicon, the microkernel keeps its own house, unseen. The doctrine did not lose, sir. It went where the monolith could not follow: into the car, the radio, the certifiable, and the concealed.

## Part Three — The Fortress Read Fairly

Now the house this course has teased for ten episodes, visited honestly. In nineteen eighty-eight Microsoft hired David Cutler away from Digital Equipment of Maynard, Massachusetts — the architect of VMS, the operating system of DEC's empire, a man with a reputation for shipping and a temper the trade still tells stories about — and set him, with his team, to build Windows a real foundation: portable, secure, multi-user at the bone, and owing nothing to the DOS in the basement. The result was NT, first shipped in nineteen ninety-three, and its design deserves a fairer reading than our world's folklore gives it. It was written first not for Intel's flagship but for a new processor codenamed *N-Ten* — the name NT itself, by the most credible telling, rode along from that codename before marketing found "New Technology" in the letters — precisely so that no one could smuggle in an architecture assumption; a hardware abstraction layer walled the machine's particulars off below; and above it Cutler built a kernel and an *executive* — scheduling, memory, I/O, security as distinct components — with the operating system's *personalities* hosted on top as servers: the Windows interface itself merely one such personality among several, beside a POSIX one and an O-S-two one. Sir will recognise the silhouette: that is a microkernel's idea of how to organise a house, drawn by a man who then, having priced the tax with cold eyes, kept the executive's organs resident in kernel space, callable at function-call prices. Hybrid, honestly so — the same settlement Tevanian's people reached with Mach, arrived at independently in the same years. The trade did eventually notice one small thing: increment each letter of VMS by one and the alphabet yields WNT. Cutler never dignified the observation; I merely place it, sir, beside the fact that Windows is, by lineage of designer and design, the continuation of Maynard, Massachusetts by other means.

What NT was *not*, at birth, was pretty on the price list: it wanted memory the consumer machines of nineteen ninety-three did not have, and for years it lived in the server room while the DOS-descended Windows kept the family trade. The two lines merged at the millennium; every Windows since is NT. And the season has already shown sir the fortress's two great architectural compromises in motion: the drawing episode told how nineteen ninety-six moved windowing and graphics *into* the kernel for speed — the bright line breached by decree, paid for in a decade of driver blue screens — and the tradesmen's episode told the counter-migration, the modern fortress coaxing drivers back out toward userspace and, in the same spirit, later hoisting much of the graphics driver itself back above stairs. Read as a forty-year arc, sir, NT is the whole argument of this episode in one house: born with microkernel bones, compromised toward the monolith wherever the tax bit, and now, decades on, migrating back outward as the economics shift again. Architecture proposes; economics disposes — and re-proposes, and re-disposes.

## Part Four — The Telephone Empires

Two houses rose and fell in pockets, and each teaches a lesson the desktop never could.

The first sir knew as a Psion, and the drawing episode showed its window server; here is the rest of the house. EPOC — later Symbian — was built in Britain in the middle nineties, in C-plus-plus, for machines with four or eight megabytes of memory and batteries measured in penlights, and it is the finest example our tour offers of *discipline as architecture*: every one of its famous peculiarities is a poverty made load-bearing. It could not afford threads in quantity, so it invented the active object — many little event-handlers taking polite turns on one thread, concurrency as cooperative scheduling above stairs; sir met the same idea wearing modern dress in the post office episode's event loops. It could not afford to leak so much as a kilobyte on a device that might run for weeks, and the C-plus-plus of the day could not afford exceptions, so it built the cleanup stack — a household protocol by which every acquisition is registered before it can be abandoned, so that any failure unwinds tidily; error handling as *etiquette*, enforced by convention and code review. Its strings were counted, bounded descriptors, because a house with eight megabytes does not tolerate the *buffer overflow* — the walk-off-the-end blunder, a program writing past the end of the space it was given — whose forty-year harvest we saw in the burglars' episode. And structurally it was the microkernel *style* in miniature: a modest kernel below, and the services — file server, window server, font and telephony servers — as separate processes speaking client-to-server, the doctrine adopted not from ideology but because isolation is cheap insurance when a crash means a customer's telephone dying in a shop. In the middle two-thousands its second-generation kernel went further with a nanokernel fine enough to carry *hard real time* — so that one processor could run the smartphone and the telephone's own signalling side by side; where the Qualcomm arrangement put the microkernel beside the monolith, this house fused the two temperaments into one kernel, a genuine technical feat. On these bones Nokia built an empire: at the zenith, roughly half the world's smartphones — by some counts more — ran Symbian. What killed it was the repeal of its constraints. Memory got cheap; the iPhone and Android arrived assuming plenty and spending it on developer comfort; and every discipline poverty had taught — the ceremony of the cleanup stack, the dialects of its strings, the austerity of its frameworks — turned overnight from survival skill into tax on the only resource that now mattered, which was the affection of application developers. In February twenty eleven Nokia's new chief circulated the memo the trade will quote forever — the company as a man on a burning oil platform in the North Sea, choosing between flames and freezing water — and jumped: not to Android but to Windows Phone, and the empire went from nearly a third of the market to under one part in twenty within three years. Symbian deserves this sentence spoken without irony, sir: it was the best engineering answer ever given to constraints that then stopped existing.

The second empire is Waterloo's, in two acts. Act one, the fortress of Java: the BlackBerry began as a pager with ambitions, and its operating system put a Java machine at the heart of the house — applications in Java, memory-safe by construction (no raw pointer to misuse, no buffer's end to overflow — the burglars' episode nods approvingly), frugal with battery, and welded to the company's true product, which was never the handset but the *service*: push email through BlackBerry's own network operations centre, encrypted end to end, auditable, certifiable — the reason it conquered the boardroom, the government, and, sir may recall, the sight of a President of the United States declining to give his up. Act two: the world changed in two thousand and seven, and Waterloo — to its credit — diagnosed its own foundation as unfit for the touch era and went shopping for a new one. It bought QNX, our Part Two hero, in twenty ten, for some two hundred million dollars, and built upon it BlackBerry Ten, launched with two handsets in January twenty thirteen: and I shall say for the record that it was an *excellent* operating system — the microkernel below, restartable services, a gesture-driven interface of real elegance, true multitasking the fruit of QNX's process model. It was also five years late, and its shelves were bare. Developers were two armies deep elsewhere; the compatibility layer BlackBerry Ten offered for running Android applications read, to every developer who saw it, as the white flag it functionally was; within three years the company was shipping handsets running Android itself, and the handset story ended. But note, sir, the estate's strange afterlife: the fortress's real crown — QNX — thrives, in the cars; the empire died and its *kernel* inherited the roads. Between them, the telephone empires state this episode's thesis with brutal economy: operating systems do not win on architecture. They win on the economics of the people who build *for* them — and Symbian taxed those people, and BlackBerry starved them, and both were out-competed by houses that made developers rich and comfortable atop kernels of no greater merit.

## Part Five — The Deepest Study: Linux in Foreign Dress

Which brings the tour home by the strangest door, because the house that made developers richest and most comfortable — the most widely deployed general-purpose operating system of this age — is *ours*, wearing clothes we would not recognise. Android runs the Linux kernel, sir: the same scheduler the pilot watched, the same memory ledger, the same bell rope. Almost nothing above the kernel is what this course has toured. There is no GNU in it: its C library is bionic, a lean BSD-descended construction with no ambition to host sir's desktop software. There is no X and no Wayland; the drawing episode showed sir SurfaceFlinger — Android's own compositor, the single service that gathers every application's finished pictures and assembles the screen from them — composing from birth. There is no systemd; a spare init of its own reads the house rules. Applications are written to a Java-descended world and run on ART, the Android runtime — which replaced the earlier Dalvik machine in Android five, in twenty fourteen, trading just-in-time interpretation for compilation ahead of time and, later, a shrewd blend of both guided by what the user actually does. And two of its organs pay debts this course has been carrying for weeks.

The first is zygote — the memory episode's phone-that-retrained-the-butler, now seen whole. That episode showed the death side of Android's retraining: the memory manager of last resort made a userspace daemon that culls cached applications by policy, platform law having required every application to save its essentials and die without ceremony. Here is the matching birth side. Android starts one template process at boot, loads into it the entire framework every application will need — thousands of classes, the common resources — and then *forks it* for each new application: the process episode's copy-on-write economics — fork's standing bargain, restated: the child *shares* its parent's memory pages rather than copying them, and pays for a private copy only of a page one side actually writes — deployed at planetary scale, so that a telephone launches an app not by loading a world but by budding one off a pre-warmed parent, the framework's pages shared read-only across every application in the house. The fork call, sir — the nineteen sixties' strangest inheritance — turns out to be the reason your telephone feels quick.

The second is binder, and binder is this episode's thesis rendered in code. Every service call in Android — an application asking the location service where it is, the activity manager marshalling a screen, near enough every permission check sir's telephone performs — rides a kernel driver called binder, which provides synchronous message-passing between processes, carrying not just data but *handles* — references to objects and services that can be passed, held, and revoked like property. Sir has heard that described twice tonight: it is a port, it is a capability, it is the microkernel's message bus — grafted, as a driver, into the side of a monolithic kernel. And its pedigree is a novel in itself: designed at Be, the nineties' great might-have-been; carried through Palm's last act; open-sourced as OpenBinder; and brought to Google by the engineer who had shepherded it the whole way, Dianne Hackborn, whereupon it was rebuilt and became the spine of the most numerous operating system on Earth. The orphan of two dead houses, sir, adopted by the one that conquered. Around it, Android's security model is the locksmith's episode made flesh: every application its own user account, the old multi-user machinery of the timesharing seventies repurposed so that each app is a *stranger* to every other — plus, in the modern era, an SELinux regime run in full enforcement — *mandatory access control*, to give the school its true name: the system's own labels on every process and file, not the owner's discretion, deciding who may touch what.

One more Android chapter belongs to this course, because it answers a question Episode Seven left deliberately hanging. That episode's bargain, restated: Linux promises driver writers no stable interface inside the kernel — the internal doors may be moved in any week's renovation — and offers the in-tree bargain instead: bring your driver into the kernel's own tree, and the kernel's maintainers will carry it through every renovation thereafter. The hanging question: what happens to that bargain when the kernel's users are a thousand handset makers who never send their code upstream? For its first decade, Android's answer was misery: every phone a private fork of the kernel, drivers welded to one framework version, updates dying in the channel between Google, the chip vendor, and the handset maker — the fragmentation sir remembers as "your telephone will receive no more updates" one year after purchase. The repair, begun in twenty seventeen under the name Treble, was to do the un-Linux thing *one storey up*: draw a hard, versioned seam between the Android framework above and the vendor's world below — a stable vendor interface, so the framework can be replaced without touching the vendor's drivers. Android, in other words, built for itself, at the userland boundary, precisely the stable interface Linux refuses to offer at the kernel boundary — refuses, sir will recall, *on principle*, the in-tree bargain — and the great refusal and the great workaround now coexist in the same telephone, each correct at its own layer. I know of no neater demonstration that these architectural questions have no once-for-all answers — only settlements, renegotiated per house and per decade.

## Part Six — Houses Within Houses

And so to the final door, which opens inward. Everything this season has toured assumed one kernel per machine, staff below and family above. The last great movement of our era dissolves that assumption: the machine itself becomes a room that can be rented out many times over — houses within houses — and the innkeeper's art is called virtualisation.

The theory is old and elegant, and it has a cast of two, named before anything moves: the *guest*, an entire operating system convinced it owns a machine; and beneath it the landlord that actually does — the *hypervisor*, or, in the literature's older and fuller style, the *virtual machine monitor*; the landlord is my gloss, the other two are the names to search. The guest executes normally, at full native speed, until it attempts something *sensitive* — touching hardware, changing the machine's fundamental state. At that instant the hardware must *trap*: stop the guest mid-gesture and hand control to the hypervisor. The hypervisor performs the operation on a pretend machine kept in software. Then it resumes the guest, none the wiser. Trap-and-emulate; and a paper of nineteen seventy-four — by Gerald Popek and Robert Goldberg, whose names the trade attaches to the test to this day — set out the exam a processor must pass for it to work: every sensitive instruction must trap when executed without privilege. Intel's architecture, sir will by now not be astonished to hear, *flunked* — a handful of its instructions behaved differently for the unprivileged instead of trapping, silently, which is worse — and so the modern era opened with heroic fraud: VMware, at the turn of the millennium, virtualising the unvirtualisable by rewriting the guest's own instructions on the fly — *binary translation*, the trade's name for the fraud. Cambridge's Xen, in two thousand and three, chose candour over fraud — modify the guest kernel itself to *ask* the hypervisor for sensitive services — and paravirtualisation carried the first age of the cloud on its back. Then the silicon repented: in the middle two-thousands Intel and AMD added the missing trap architecture, the processor at last passing the exam set for it thirty years earlier, and the fraud and the candour could both retire.

What Linux did next is the whole course in one move. Rather than write a hypervisor to stand *beneath* the kernel, the kernel-based virtual machine — *KVM*, as the whole trade writes and says it; announced in late two thousand and six by an engineer named Avi Kivity, merged with almost indecent speed at the start of two thousand and seven — turned Linux *itself* into the hypervisor: a module teaches the kernel to drive the new silicon, and then each guest operating system is simply — sir may say it with me — *a process*. Each virtual processor is a thread, scheduled by the same scheduler as sir's compiler; guest memory is pages in the same ledger; the guest's pretend devices are served by an ordinary userspace program. Every mechanism this season toured, redeployed without modification one storey down. That is why Linux owns the cloud, sir: the cloud is a block of flats, millions of machines each renting rooms to strangers, and the landlord in nearly every building is the kernel this course has been touring all along — with the tenancy taken to its logical extreme by the likes of Firecracker, Amazon's little Rust-built landlord, which on top of that same kernel machinery spins up thousands of deliberately tiny guests per machine — *microVMs*, in the trade's own word — each booting in around an eighth of a second, each costing a few megabytes of overhead — so that a single rented *function call* can have its own private operating system for the duration of a heartbeat. The flat, shrunk to a bedsit, let by the minute.

And here the season's oldest argument comes back through the door wearing a landlord's uniform. Look at what a hypervisor is: a small privileged core; isolated domains that cannot touch one another; all interaction mediated by traps and messages; drivers and policy pushed out of the core into service domains. That is the microkernel's job description, sir, item for item. The trade noticed long ago: a paper from the Xen circle in two thousand and five bore the mischievous title "Are Virtual Machine Monitors Microkernels Done Right?" — and answered yes — whereupon the microkernel school's own dean replied under the identical title with the question mark doing rather different work, and the exchange has been cited at both ends ever since. The joke beneath both papers is the one I promised sir at the top of the season: *a hypervisor is a microkernel that shipped.* The industry that declined, in nineteen ninety-two, to run its device drivers as isolated message-passing processes, now runs entire operating systems as isolated message-passing processes, bills by the hour for it, and calls it the cloud. The professor's doctrine, refused at the front door, was carried in through the tradesmen's entrance by men with invoices.

## The Books of the Evening

The accounts, sir, and tonight they settle a nine-week debt. The doctrine stated fairly: a minimal kernel — address spaces, threads, messages — with the household's organs as restartable, mutually suspicious processes; its promises real (containment, least privilege, provability) and its price real (the message-passing tax, paid in crossings and cold caches). Mach: the flagship that sank as a microkernel and reigns as a hybrid — its ports and tasks the living foundation of every Apple machine, the doctrine's abstractions inherited without its isolation. The houses that shipped it whole: QNX, the rendezvous that drives two hundred and fifty million cars and once served a desktop from a diskette; the L-four line, Liedtke's proof that Mach's tax was implementation rather than essence, shipped by the billion in the telephone's radio — both verdicts of nineteen ninety-two riding in one pocket; seL-four, the kernel small enough to *prove*; and MINIX itself, keeping Intel's hidden second house below every ledger this course has audited. The fortress read fairly: Cutler's NT, microkernel bones priced against the tax and settled into an honest hybrid — VMS continued by other means, breached toward the kernel in the nineties, migrating back out ever since. The telephone empires: Symbian, discipline as architecture, killed by the repeal of its constraints; BlackBerry, the Java fortress that bought the right kernel five years too late — together the proof that houses win on developers' economics, not on kernels. The deepest study: Android, our own kernel in foreign dress — bionic, zygote's copy-on-write budding, ART, the per-app stranger model — with binder, the twice-orphaned capability bus, grafting the microkernel's spine into the monolith; and Treble building, one storey up, the very stable interface the kernel refuses below. And the houses within houses: trap-and-emulate's exam, flunked and finally passed; Xen's candour; KVM's audacity — the guest as process, the kernel as landlord, the cloud as a block of flats let down to the bedsit — and the old argument returned in uniform: the hypervisor as the microkernel that shipped.

The debt from Episode One stands paid in full. Architecture proposes; economics disposes — and the disposition is never final: the doctrine that lost the desktop won the car, the radio, the proof, the hidden engine, and, by the back door, the cloud itself.

One episode remains, sir, and it is the capstone. Next week we climb to the observatory: how a running house is *seen* — the counters behind every figure this season has quoted, the tracing machinery that can put a stethoscope to any function in the kernel while it runs, the verified errand-runner from the pilot grown into the observatory's great instrument, flame graphs and how to read one aloud, and the honest gauges of a house under pressure. The hook is a man in a San Francisco datacentre in two thousand and eight, leaning into a rack of disks and *shouting* at them — and the latency chart shuddering in answer. Then the state of the house as I record, told without varnish — the Rust question and what the argument has cost, the new asynchronous machinery grown up, the succession — and the season's debts audited aloud, down to the last. The tour ends where it began: with the house, the staff, and the bell.

Until then.

*— End of Episode Eleven.*
