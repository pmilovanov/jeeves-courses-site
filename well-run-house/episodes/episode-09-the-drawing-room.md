# Episode Nine — The Drawing Room, or X and the Making of Pictures

*The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux*

## The Next Letter of the Alphabet

Good day, sir.

In June of nineteen eighty-four, at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, a problem of institutional diplomacy was quietly becoming a problem of software. The Institute had launched, the year before, a grand undertaking called Project Athena — a campus-wide computing environment for teaching, underwritten over many years and many millions by two corporate patrons, Digital Equipment and IBM, who happened to be bitter rivals selling mutually incompatible machines. MIT, with the serenity of an institution that intends to outlive its benefactors, had made vendor independence a founding principle: whatever Athena built would run on everyone's hardware or it would not run at all. Which was a splendid principle, and left somebody with the job of making graphics — the screens, the windows, the mice, the whole bright modern surface of computing — work identically across machinery that agreed on almost nothing.

At the Laboratory for Computer Science, a researcher named Bob Scheifler had been contending with a window system called W, written at Stanford for an experimental operating system called V — the sort of naming scheme that tells you nobody expected posterity to be watching. W worked, but it worked *synchronously*: each request to draw waited for its answer before the next could be sent, and across a network that discipline was death by round trip — every little ask paying the wire's full crossing, there and back, before its successor could so much as depart, so that a screenful of drawing was billed not at the wire's bandwidth but at its *distance*, over and over — a lesson about latency that sir will recognise from our evening in the post office. Scheifler took W, spent a fortnight rebuilding it around a different temperament, and announced the result to his colleagues in a message of magnificent modesty: "I stole a fair amount of code from W, surrounded it with an asynchronous rather than a synchronous interface, and called it X." Performance, he noted, appeared to be roughly twice that of W. The name was simply the next letter of the alphabet.

Forty-two years later, sir, the fortnight's work is still with us. The X Window System outlived the workstation vendors who adopted it, outlived the consortium formed to govern it, outlived Project Athena itself, and runs at this moment beneath a great many of the machines listening to this episode — including, if sir's household is anything like most of its generation, some of sir's. It survived not because it was beautiful — we shall be hearing expert testimony to the contrary — but because of three decisions taken in that founding moment: that pictures would travel over a wire; that the system would provide *mechanism, not policy*; and that the screen would belong to a server. Each of these was brilliant in nineteen eighty-four. Each had become a liability by the time sir's present machines were built. This evening is the audit: how the drawing room of the house actually works, why its arrangement is backwards to every newcomer's intuition, how it was extended decade after decade past anything its authors intended — and why, in the end, the people who knew it best concluded it could not be mended, only succeeded.

One name to hold on to before we begin. Among Scheifler's earliest and closest collaborators, co-author of the protocol and of the principles that shaped it, was a young man named Jim Gettys — the same Jim Gettys who turns up in this course once already, a quarter-century further on, debugging his own home connection and discovering that the internet's plumbing had silently filled with standing queues: *bufferbloat*, he named the affliction — buffers everywhere grown so deep that packets stood in them for whole seconds, delay that served nobody, poisoning every interactive thing. The man helped put the world's pictures on the wire, and then spent his later years finding out what the wire had been doing to everything else. Few careers bracket our subject so neatly.

## Part One — What a Screen Is For

Begin below the surface, with the inheritance. The pilot traced how the terminal — the teletype, then the glass teletype — shaped everything about how Linux speaks: a keyboard going in, a stream of characters coming out, the whole conversation conducted in text. A framebuffer breaks that contract. A framebuffer is a rectangle of memory whose contents *are* the screen — every dot on the panel a few bytes in a grid, redrawn from memory dozens of times a second. Nothing about a framebuffer is a conversation. It is a surface, and it is *one* surface, and every program in the house that wishes to show anything must somehow share it.

From that single awkward fact, every display system ever built inherits the same four duties. First, **arbitration**: one screen, many programs, and something must decide which rectangles of the surface belong to whom — the drawing room is shared, and the furniture must not be dragged through the guests. Second, **input routing**: one keyboard and one pointer, many possible recipients, and every keystroke poses the question *who hears this?* — a question with, as we shall see, real security consequences. Third, a **drawing service**: programs must be able to make marks — text, lines, images — without owning the hardware themselves, because two programs poking the same video card unsupervised is the paint-and-wallpaper version of the shared-ledger catastrophes we met in the episode on concurrency — two writers, no lock, each update trampling the other's half-finished arithmetic. And fourth, **policy**: what a window looks like, how it is moved and resized and stacked, what a titlebar is, what clicking *means*. The first three duties are mechanics. The fourth is opinion.

X's founding act — the decision from which everything else this evening follows — was to take up the first three duties and flatly refuse the fourth. Scheifler and Gettys wrote the principle down in their early papers, and it became scripture: *provide mechanism, not policy.* The server would put pixels where clients asked and deliver input where the rules directed, and it would hold no opinion whatever about appearance or behaviour. It did not know what a titlebar was. It did not care which window was "in front" beyond keeping an ordered stack. A companion principle was still more austere: do not add functionality unless a real application cannot be completed without it. The system was to be a substrate — deliberately, militantly unopinionated — because it had to serve two feuding vendors in nineteen eighty-four and every unforeseeable interface fashion afterwards. Hold that decision in mind, sir. It is the reason X conquered the world, and the reason the world eventually left.

Now the arrangement that confuses every newcomer, gloriously and forever: in X, the machine with the screen runs the *server*, and the applications are the *clients* — even though sir is sitting at the server and the application may be down the hall. Intuition rebels; intuition is wrong, and the cure is to remember what the words actually mean. A server is the process that *owns a resource* and takes requests for it. The X server owns the screen, the keyboard, and the pointer — the actual hardware of the actual room sir is sitting in. An application wanting to draw is asking a favour of whoever owns the glass. So the program — the client — connects to the display server over a socket — speaking what the trade calls, with no more imagination than it spent on the system's name, the *X protocol* — and sends requests: create me a window; draw this text; tell me when the pointer enters. Back the other way flow events: a key went down, a button was pressed, and — a detail with a long tail — *part of your window has become visible and must be repainted*. The protocol names that message an *exposure event* — a term to pocket, for it recurs wherever screens are shared — and it exists because in the original economy of the system the server kept no *backing store*, no memory of what obscured windows looked like; memory was dear, and redrawing was the client's problem.

The stroke of genius was that the socket did not care where the client lived. The wire between client and server could cross the campus. That was the whole point — that was Athena: the program runs on the big shared machine in the basement of one building, and the picture appears on the cheap terminal in front of the undergraduate in another. Pictures over the wire, interleaved from many clients at once, each request a few bytes of drawing command rather than a cargo of pixels. And Scheifler's asynchronous temperament made it tolerable: clients stream requests without waiting on answers, round trips are spent only when a question genuinely needs asking, and the latency of the wire is hidden rather than multiplied. Network transparency, the trade came to call it, and for a decade and a half it was the feature every other house envied: the same program, unmodified, drawing on the glass in front of you or on glass a continent away.

One more founding fact, easily missed because it is an absence. None of this lives in the kernel. Linux's kernel does not know what a window is. The X server is a process — above stairs, in our house's terms, family rather than staff — that happens to have been granted access to the video hardware. The entire edifice of the graphical desktop is, from the kernel's standpoint, just some programs talking through sockets. Keep that in view for the comparative portion of the evening, because the house at Redmond answered the same question the opposite way, below stairs, and both houses paid — differently — for their choices.

## Part Two — The Butler Refuses an Opinion

If the server holds no opinion about titlebars, sir may reasonably ask who does — because his screen visibly has them. The answer is the most elegant single consequence of mechanism-without-policy: the **window manager**, and the crucial fact about it is that it is *not part of the system*. It is an ordinary client. It connects over the same socket, speaking the same X protocol, as a text editor, with no special privilege whatever. What it has instead is a standing arrangement.

The mechanism is called substructure redirection, and rendered into household English it is this: the manager says to the server, *when any program asks to place or resize a window on this screen, do not obey — inform me instead, and I shall decide.* From that one hook, everything follows. Three parties figure in what happens next, so let me set them out before anything moves: the *application*, which wants a window and knows nothing of the other two; the *server*, which owns the glass and has renounced all opinion; and the *window manager*, whose opinion is the whole of the law. Now the volley, one stroke at a time. The application asks the server for a window at such-and-such a place — the ordinary request, exactly as though no manager existed. The server, bound by the standing arrangement, does not obey; it forwards the request to the manager and awaits instruction. The manager consults its own policy — its opinion — decides where the window shall actually go, usually wraps it first in a decorated frame of its own construction, and then directs the server; and the server obeys the *manager's* version of the request, not the application's. The application, mark, is never told any of this. It asked; a window appeared; whether its wishes were honoured or overruled it has no way of knowing. The titlebar is not the application's and not the server's: it is the manager's window, drawn around the tenant like a picture frame around a canvas. The butler, in short — seating the guests, announcing precedence, imposing the etiquette of the room upon a company that arrives with none of its own.

And because the butler is merely a client, the household can dismiss him and engage another *while the party is running*. Replace a spartan manager with a baroque one and every window on the screen is re-dressed in the new establishment's livery, applications none the wiser. Whether focus follows the mouse or waits for a click; whether windows snap to grids or float free or tile edge-to-edge like parquet — policy, all of it, above stairs, swappable at whim. Sir may recall the little ecosystem this bred — the austere tiling managers beloved of programmers, the ornate desktops, the wobbling windows we shall come to presently — and the reason Linux could breed such an ecosystem while Windows and the Macintosh each had exactly one window policy apiece is precisely that in X the policy was never in the platform at all.

The bill for this liberty arrived on two invoices. The first is that when a system declines to have an opinion, everyone else must have one, and their opinions must be made to agree. Clients and managers needed conventions — how a window states its title, how it asks to be full-screen, how it indicates it would rather not be resized — and the conventions were duly codified in a document called the Inter-Client Communication Conventions Manual, a text of such reputation that its own community spoke of it the way one speaks of a difficult relative, and the chapter of the trade's great book of complaints devoted to X — we shall meet that book again shortly — reserved its purest venom for this manual. The second invoice was the **toolkit wars**. X ships no buttons. It draws lines and blits images; a button — its bevel, its press, its habits — is some library's opinion, and in the late eighties and early nineties the workstation vendors fought a decade-long war of opinions: Motif, the boardroom-grey toolkit of the Open Software Foundation, against OpenLook from Sun and AT&T — the Unix wars of the pilot's telling, that decade in which the vendors splintered one operating system into incompatible fiefdoms and lost the field to newcomers while they quarrelled, fought again in miniature, in widgets. Both armies were eventually routed by two toolkits from our own world: one written to build a free image editor and named after it — the GIMP toolkit, GTK — and one produced by a small Norwegian firm, Qt, whose licensing terms were free-ish rather than free and thereby begot a schism: one desktop project built on the Norwegian toolkit, a rival desktop founded in objection to its licence, and the GNOME-and-KDE division of the Linux desktop persisting to this day — a licence quarrel wearing an interface. Where policy is homeless, sir, it does not disappear. It multiplies.

Since we are in the room where sir actually lives, one household fossil deserves its label. Sir has surely noticed that his Linux desktop possesses *two* clipboards: the formal one — the *clipboard selection*, in the conventions' own name — worked by explicit copy and paste, and the informal one whereby merely *selecting* text arms it, and the middle mouse button discharges it wherever the pointer happens to hover. That second channel is the **primary selection**, and the pair exist because the protocol — true to doctrine — provides only the mechanism of selections, plural, named, and negotiable; which selection means what was left to convention, and convention, being convention, settled on two. The middle-click paste under sir's finger is a committee compromise of the nineteen-eighties, still discharging faithfully every day. I confess a certain fondness for it — the informal channel is genuinely quicker in a terminal — but as architecture it is exactly what it looks like: an opinion the system refused to have, fossilised in mid-disagreement.

Say this for the doctrine, though, before we turn to its troubles: it *worked*. Every vendor could drape its corporate look over the common mechanism, which is why they all signed up; and every look of the past forty years — workstation austerity, nineties chrome, the flat fashions of the present day — has come and gone over the same protocol. The mechanism outlived every opinion laid upon it. As wagers go, that one paid.

## Part Three — The Extension Era, and the Palace Coup

The protocol that all of this rides upon froze astonishingly early. Version ten had escaped the campus and revealed every deficiency that only strangers find; version eleven was designed in the open, on mailing lists bridged to the newsgroups of the young internet — a genuinely novel way to build software in nineteen eighty-six — and released on the fifteenth of September, nineteen eighty-seven. And then, in the sense that matters — the wire format, the core verbs — it never changed again. The X of this morning is X version eleven. A client written against that protocol during the Reagan administration can, in principle and generally in practice, still connect to the server on sir's machine and draw. As feats of compatibility go it stands with anything in this course, alongside the kernel's own never-break-userspace covenant — the standing vow, sir will recall it restated season-long, that an interface once shipped to programs is honoured forever, however inconvenient it becomes.

But four decades of change had to go *somewhere*, and the somewhere was the protocol's one great release valve: **extensions**. At connection, a client may ask the server: do you happen to speak such-and-such? — and a server may offer whatever dialects it has. Through that hatch, decade by decade, came everything the world of nineteen eighty-seven had not imagined. A shared-memory extension — it carries MIT's name in the registry to this day — so that a client on the *same* machine could pass images through memory rather than laboriously down a socket — note, sir, the first quiet crack in network transparency: the fast path now only worked locally. A conduit for OpenGL — *G-L-X*, the registry styles it — so the era of three-dimensional hardware could reach the screen. An extension to resize and rotate displays while the session ran — *R-and-R*, the manuals compress it, resize-and-rotate run together — because monitors had learned to be plugged in and unplugged. Video overlay — *X-Video* — for the era when merely *playing a film* taxed a machine. The frozen core acquired, over time, some thirty-odd resident dialects — and with them a certain baroque quality, the protocol equivalent of a Georgian house re-plumbed by every subsequent century.

Two extensions matter above all the rest, because between them they quietly replaced the system's entire theory of drawing.

The first arrived in the year two thousand, from Keith Packard — remember the name; it recurs — and was called Render. Render was, in effect, a signed confession. The core protocol draws like nineteen eighty-seven: hard-edged lines, opaque fills, and text set in fonts that lived *in the server*, named by strings of fourteen hyphen-separated fields that sir may remember scrolling past in ancient configuration files, rendered without smoothing and shipped as bitmaps. By the late nineties every civilised system smoothed its text, and X could not. Render's remedy inverted the founding economy: the *client* would now rasterise its own type and shapes — the *FreeType* library, the type-rendering engine above stairs whose name says exactly what it is, doing the actual work — and ship the results to the server as little images called glyphs; and the server's remaining job was **compositing**: layering translucent images upon images according to the algebra of Porter and Duff, the same mathematics beneath every modern interface. Packard's design notes credited, among its inspirations, the windowing system of Plan Nine — the research system Bell Labs built to succeed its own Unix, met in passing at the season's start, and which had done pictures simply and well while the rest of the world was still arguing with its font servers. Under Render, anti-aliased text arrived at last; the server-side font machinery sank into disuse; and the server took its first long step from *artist* toward *print shop* — no longer drawing to speak of, merely combining what cleverer hands delivered.

The second confession came in September of two thousand and four, was called Composite, and finished the thought. With Composite, a manager may ask the server to redirect windows away from the screen altogether: each window paints into its own private off-screen buffer, whole and unclipped, and the assembly of the visible screen becomes the business of — note the recurring melody, sir — *yet another ordinary client*, the **compositing manager** — the *compositor*, the trade promptly shortened it — which gathers everybody's buffers and paints the final tableau, nowadays through the graphics card. A sibling extension, named *Damage* with the trade's usual bluntness, reports to the compositor which windows have changed since its last pass, so that it need not repaint the whole tableau for every blink of a cursor. And because the compositor is just a client with everyone's windows in its hands, it may do as it pleases on the way to the glass: translucency, shadows, thumbnails, the scaling and spinning of live windows. There followed, around two thousand and six, the era sir may remember with a smile — the compiz springtime, windows wobbling like jelly as they moved, workspaces on the faces of a spinning cube, the Linux desktop briefly and gloriously the flashiest thing in computing, to the undisguised envy of owners of operating systems that merely worked. I do not mock it, sir: beneath the jelly, the architecture had genuinely changed. The server no longer even assembled the screen. It had become a broker of buffers — a landlord collecting rooms while a tenant did the decorating.

But mark what compositing-by-extension could *not* fix, because it is the crack through which the next episode enters. The compositor was bolted on, not built in. An application drew in its own time; the server copied in its own time; the compositor assembled in *its* own time; and the monitor refreshed to its own metronome — four clocks and no conductor. Frames tore: sir has seen it, the horizontal shear across a scrolling page or a panning scene, top half of the glass showing one moment and bottom half another. Frames arrived late, or twice, or half-painted; gamers cursed the extra beat of lag the compositor levied and turned it off; and none of it was truly curable, because the protocol's deepest assumptions — clients drawing at will, a server ignorant of the monitor's heartbeat — were laid down before the problem existed. The phrase to hold for next week is the one the successor project took as its vow: *every frame is perfect.* X, constitutionally, could not swear it.

Meanwhile — and this is the palace coup of our chapter's title — the code itself changed dynasties. Through the nineties and into the new century, the implementation of X that mattered — the one Linux actually ran — was XFree-eighty-six, a volunteer project grown from a humble PC port into the de facto steward of free-world graphics. Its governance had grown less liberal than its licence: a self-perpetuating core team, releases slow, ambitions modest, and its most productive contributor — the same Keith Packard of Render and much else — expelled from the inner circle in the spring of two thousand and three for the crime, roughly stated, of organising the future outside the committee's walls. The following February the project contrived its own funeral: it stapled onto its licence a new credit clause — an advertising requirement of the old Berkeley style — which the Free Software Foundation judged incompatible with the GPL. In our world, sir, the licence is load-bearing masonry; you do not casually mortar a new clause into it while the tenants are home. Within weeks the dormant X-dot-Org name was revived as a foundation under the banner of freedesktop-dot-org; the exiled and the exasperated moved as one; a fully free release shipped that April; and by the following year essentially every distribution had changed allegiance. XFree-eighty-six remained nominally alive for a few years more, a project in the condition of a grand house with the family gone — lights on in one wing, nobody receiving. The lesson was governance, not graphics: in a commons, the licence and the temperament of the stewards *are* the architecture, and a fork is the community's vote of no confidence. And the new stewards — Packard and the circle around him — were precisely the engineers who then built the extension era's crowning works, looked hard at what they had built them *onto*, and began, quietly, to draw conclusions. We shall hear those conclusions next week, from their own mouths.

## Part Four — The Room Where Every Door Stands Open

We spent last week among locks and keys, sir, so the shape of this part will be uncomfortably familiar. The drawing room has no locks. It was designed in nineteen eighty-four, for a building full of colleagues, and its trust model is exactly that: a single flat domain. To connect to the display is to hold full citizenship in it. The protocol distinguishes clients one from another — it numbers them, tracks their resources — but it does not *isolate* them. Every right in the room is a right of every client in it.

Let me itemise what citizenship confers, because the itemisation is the indictment. Any connected client may enumerate every window on the display, read their titles, inspect their contents — which is to say, take a screenshot of anything, at any time, without ceremony. Any client may ask to be told of the pointer's travels anywhere on the screen: there is a beloved toy, as old as the system, a little pair of cartoon eyes — *x-eyes*, one word in the catalogue — that swivel to follow the cursor wherever it goes — charming in nineteen eighty-eight, and rather less charming once one states its operating principle, namely that any program may watch the pointer *everywhere*. Any client may register to observe keystrokes bound for other windows, or grab the keyboard outright and receive everything; there is a diagnostic tool, standard equipment on sir's machine, that will cheerfully print every keystroke on the display to a terminal, and it is not exploiting anything — it is using the protocol *as documented*. And any client may inject synthetic input: type into other programs, click their buttons, work their menus, puppet the whole room.

So the sentence to be said plainly is this: under X, for some three and a half decades and counting, *every client is a keylogger by right*. Run a program — any program, the shabbiest utility from the farthest repository — and while it is connected to your display it can read every password you type into every other window. There was an attempt at gradations in the nineties: a security extension defining *untrusted* clients with clipped privileges, which is what the secure shell's *X forwarding* — the arrangement that carries a remote program's windows home over the encrypted connection — politely engages by default — whereupon so many applications break, in so many ways, that virtually everyone reaches for the *trusted* flag, ships full citizenship across the network to whatever runs on the far machine, and thinks no more about it. The locks were offered as an aftermarket accessory, they pinched, and the household declined to wear them.

Two consequences deserve their own lines. The screen locker, first. A lock screen under X is not a fortification; it is an ordinary client that has grabbed the input and papered the glass — a guest standing very earnestly in front of the door. If it crashes — and screen lockers are written by mortals — the paper falls and the room stands open; a whole genus of lock-screen bypass bugs over the years reduces to precisely this, and the trade's eventual verdict was that a trustworthy lock cannot actually be *built* on this protocol, only approximated. Second, the server itself. The process holding this undefendable room ran, for most of its history, as root — an enormous, elderly body of code, parsing requests from every quarter, which for decades also programmed the graphics card's registers *itself*, from userspace, because the kernel offered no such service. The pilot's rule of the bright line — the family above stairs, the staff below, and hardware touched only below — was, in the matter of graphics, simply waived: here was a member of the family with root's keys and its hands directly in the machinery. When it fell over, it could take the console with it — sir may recall the ritual, in the bad old years, of a dead black screen, a blind walk to another machine, and a login across the network to shoot the server and hope. Moving mode-setting below stairs, into the kernel where it belonged, is a story of the twenty-tens and next week's plumbing; I book it now as a debt.

And while the doors stood open, the room's great founding virtue was quietly ceasing to be true. Network transparency decayed by a hundred small mercies. The shared-memory extension moved image traffic off the wire — locally. Direct rendering — the *direct rendering infrastructure*, in the trade's full style — went further: for three-dimensional work the client talks to the graphics card *directly*, kernel-brokered, bypassing the server and therefore the network entirely — the fast path forgot the wire ever existed. And the toolkits completed the migration: modern GTK and Qt do not send drawing commands at all in the old sense; they paint every pixel themselves, above stairs, and hand the server finished rectangles. Which means the celebrated wire now mostly carries — bitmaps. Forward a modern browser across the ocean and X will dutifully ship it to you as a slideshow of screenshots, worse than the purpose-built remote-desktop protocols, because it is *pretending to be a drawing protocol while actually being a picture chute*. What survives of the old magic — and I concede it still earns its keep, sir; I have used it this year — is the odd remote terminal window and dialogue box: the eighties workload, for which the eighties design remains perfectly adequate.

Let us have the balance honest, though, because ridicule is cheap and the ledger is not one-sided. The great book of complaints of nineteen ninety-four — *The UNIX-Haters Handbook*, a work of loving malice by refugees from finer operating systems — devoted a chapter to our subject under the title "The X-Windows Disaster," and pronounced it "the Iran-Contra of graphical user interfaces: a tragedy of political compromises, entangled alliances, marketing hype, and just plain greed." Quite so, and yet: the disaster ran everything, everywhere, for forty years. It carried the free desktop from before Linux existed until well after sir's machines stopped needing to care. Its refusal of policy is *why* a thousand interface experiments could happen at all — why the tiling managers and the accessibility tools and the automation harnesses exist; the very promiscuity we have spent this part deploring is what the screen readers and test rigs were built on, which is exactly what made the doors so hard to close afterwards. Nothing about the room was malicious. It was a faithful copy of the trust of its era, maintained with heroic patience by a tiny and dwindling crew, decades past its design life. The indictment is not that anyone blundered, sir. It is that the building's virtues and its vices grew from the same root — mechanism without policy, trust without walls — and no amount of extension could dig the root out.

## Part Five — How the Other Houses Drew

Now the promised look over the hedges, because nothing locates X's peculiarity like the neighbours' answers to the same four duties.

Redmond first. A Windows program does not connect to a drawing service across a socket; it *is*, structurally, a subscriber standing at a message pump. Its inner life is a loop — fetch the next message, translate it, dispatch it — and everything arrives as messages: keystrokes, clicks, timers, and, tellingly, the instruction *your window is stale; repaint this rectangle* — which sir will recognise as the exposure event of the X world under another livery. Convergent evolution, and a small comfort: whatever the architecture, a shared screen teaches every house the same lesson — the system must be able to tell a program to redraw itself. Drawing went through GDI, the Graphics Device Interface, a device-independent drawing service of the same generation and general ambitions as X's core protocol. The instructive difference is *where it lived*. Windows NT began, in its portable, principled youth, with windowing and graphics above stairs, in a subsystem process — and every draw call was therefore a trip through the house, process to process, and it was slow; and in nineteen ninety-six, with NT four, Microsoft moved the whole apparatus — window management, GDI, the display drivers it hosted — below stairs, into the kernel, for speed. The bright line was breached *by design*, and the invoice arrived on schedule: for years afterwards, a fault in a graphics driver was not a dead desktop but a dead machine — the blue screens of that era were, by Microsoft's own accounting, overwhelmingly the work of third-party drivers exercising their contractual right to run below stairs badly, and sir will remember from our tradesmen's evening which graphics company's kernel modules stood at the head of that ledger — and the kernel's windowing wing became, and remains, one of the burglars' favourite entrances, a compendium of elderly drawing code reachable from every unprivileged process in the building. Then, in Vista — two thousand and seven, for most of the world — Redmond executed the same turn X was making in the same decade, but by decree: the Desktop Window Manager, a true compositor; every window redirected to its own buffer, held as a texture on the graphics card, the screen assembled by the platform itself on the monitor's own heartbeat. Old GDI programs were not asked to adapt: their drawing is quietly intercepted and painted into memory, and the compositor collects the result — the polite fiction preserved, the neighbours none the wiser. Note the structural moral, sir: Windows retrofitted compositing *too* — nobody's nineteen-eighties architecture foresaw it — but a proprietor with one implementation and no committee could turn the whole estate in a single release. In our commons, the same turn took a schism, a successor protocol, and twenty years — as we shall hear next week.

Cupertino, by way of NeXT, answers differently again: drawing as *language*. The NeXT machines of the late eighties carried Display PostScript, built jointly with Adobe: an application described its appearance in the same tongue the laser printer spoke — genuine programs, resolution-independent, elegant almost to the point of provocation — and paid for it in computing power, which NeXT could afford to spend, and in royalties to Adobe, which eventually nobody could. When NeXT's operating system came home to Apple and re-emerged as Mac OS X in March of two thousand and one, the drawing model was rebuilt around the *shapes* of PostScript without the language or the licence — *Quartz*, Apple named the result: an imaging model drawn to the letter of the PDF specification, Adobe's mathematics without Adobe's tollbooth. And here is the fact worth the detour: from its very first release, before the name Wayland existed and three years before X's Composite extension, the Macintosh screen was assembled by a compositor — every window buffered from birth, the visible screen composed centrally as the *normal course of the platform*, never an extension, never optional. The famous flourish — the window pouring itself into the dock like smoke drawn into a bottle — was the new architecture showing off precisely what buffered, composited windows make trivial and uncomposited ones make impossible. macOS never suffered the retrofit problem, sir. It was born after the lesson, and it is the house the others spent the next two decades quietly imitating.

And then the smallest house, and in some ways my favourite witness. In nineteen ninety-seven a British firm shipped a palmtop — the Psion Series Five — carrying a new operating system called EPOC, built around an ARM processor at eighteen megahertz and four or eight megabytes of memory: a rounding error, sir, on the machines this episode has otherwise discussed. And EPOC's answer to the four duties was — a window server. A true one: a separate server process owning the screen and the input queue, clients sending it requests through message passing, events flowing back — the X arrangement in miniature, arrived at independently, on a machine that fit in a coat pocket and ran for a month on two penlight batteries. When the architecture went on to power Symbian and the Finnish telephones by the hundred million, the window server went with it, drawing every interface that generation of the world carried in its pockets. The witness testifies to two things. First, that the client-server shape for displays is no MIT eccentricity: wherever many mutually ignorant programs share one screen, something server-shaped precipitates out, be the house never so small. But second — and this is the reproach — the small house kept what the big one refused: *policy*. EPOC's server and its interface libraries had opinions — one look, one behaviour, resources measured to the last kilobyte — because on eight megabytes, doctrinal open-mindedness is a luxury item. Mechanism-not-policy, sir, was never the *inevitable* answer. It was a choice fitted to a particular quarrel between vendors in a particular decade — and every other house on the street, from the palmtop to the Macintosh, chose otherwise and lived.

Survey the street from the end of it, then. By the middle twenty-tens, every neighbouring house — Redmond, Cupertino, the telephones we shall properly visit in two weeks — had converged on the same settlement: every window buffered, every frame composed centrally on the display's own heartbeat, clients isolated from one another as a matter of course, and no network transparency to mourn because none had pretended to it. X alone still stood on the original bargain — maximum mechanism, minimum policy, total mutual trust — with compositing bolted to its side and its virtues one by one gone quietly ceremonial. The neighbours had not proved the old house wrong so much as outlived its premises. Its premises, sir, were the eighties.

## The Books of the Evening

The accounts, then. A campus that refused to choose between two vendors needed pictures that belonged to neither, and a fortnight's asynchronous rebuild of a borrowed window system became the free world's drawing room for four decades. Four duties define the office — arbitrate the screen, route the input, serve the drawing, set the policy — and X's constitution was to discharge three and refuse the fourth: mechanism, not policy; the server owns the glass and the devices, applications attend as clients, over a wire that once crossed campuses and latterly mostly crosses a machine's own memory. Policy, refused below, precipitated above stairs: the window manager, an ordinary client with a standing arrangement — substructure redirection — the butler whose opinions are the room's whole etiquette — and around the vacuum of opinion grew the conventions manual, the toolkit wars, the two-desktop schism, and the middle-click fossil under sir's finger. The protocol froze in nineteen eighty-seven and lived on through its extensions: Render, which retired the server from artistry to compositing; Composite, which retired it from assembling the screen at all; a palace coup in two thousand and four that handed the code to the very engineers whose extensions were the confession; and a bolted-on compositor keeping four clocks with no conductor — tearing, lag, and no oath of perfect frames constitutionally possible. The doors: one flat trust domain, every client a keylogger by right, the lock screen a guest before the door, the server elderly, enormous, root, and, for too long, personally working the hardware. And over the hedges: Redmond's message pump and its drawing moved below stairs for speed and paid for in blue screens, then a decreed compositor with the old interface kept as a polite fiction; Cupertino born composited, PostScript's mathematics without its tollbooth; and a British palmtop proving the server shape universal while keeping, on eight megabytes, the very policy the big house refused.

The debt of the evening is stated on its face. The engineers who inherited X — who wrote Render and Composite, who understood the building to its sills — concluded that its faults were not defects but *premises*: you cannot retrofit isolation onto a protocol whose every verb assumes trust, nor perfect frames onto a stack that keeps four unsynchronised clocks. Next week, therefore: the successor. A Danish engineer at Red Hat, a daily commute that passed a small Massachusetts town called Wayland, and a design whose founding vow answers this episode's every complaint at a stroke — *every frame is perfect* — by the simple, radical expedient of abolishing the middleman: the compositor *becomes* the server. What that abolition breaks — which is to say, screenshots, screen sharing, automation, the accessibility tools, every workflow built on forty years of open doors — and the long decade of portals and protocols spent rebuilding trust with locks on it; the bridge that keeps the old world running; the plumbing below stairs — kernel mode-setting, the buffer brokers, the quarrel with NVIDIA — that both worlds now stand on; the affair of the rival that wasn't; and how a telephone operating system from our own family had been composing every frame all along. The moving of a cathedral, sir, one stone at a time, with the congregation still at prayer inside.

Until then.

*— End of Episode Nine.*
