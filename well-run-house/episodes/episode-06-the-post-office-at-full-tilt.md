# Episode Six — The Post Office at Full Tilt

*The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux*

## Four Hundred Yards at Forty Bits a Second

Good day, sir.

In October of nineteen eighty-six, the internet — then a modest federation of university and laboratory networks, cherished by a few thousand researchers — suffered the first of what its historians now call the congestion collapses. The symptom, measured between the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory and the Berkeley campus proper — two sites separated by four hundred yards and a handful of routers — was a drop in useful throughput from thirty-two kilobits a second to *forty bits a second*. Not forty kilobits: forty bits. A factor of nearly a thousand, on a link whose hardware was in perfect health, between institutions that could have shouted the data across the street faster.

Van Jacobson, of the Berkeley laboratory, and Michael Karels, keeper of the Berkeley Unix networking code — you will recall from Episode One that Berkeley's sockets were the internet's reference implementation, so the world's transport behaviour was, in practice, whatever their code did — set out to discover, in Jacobson's later words, why things had gotten so bad. What they found was not a broken component but a *pathological society*: every component correct, per Episode Three's darkest pattern, and the ensemble insane.

The mechanism deserves a full minute, because it is the founding parable of every queue in tonight's episode. TCP promises reliability: send, await acknowledgement, and if the acknowledgement is late — retransmit. Now let a link saturate, ever so slightly. Queues in the routers lengthen; acknowledgements arrive a little later; and some sender's retransmission timer — tuned optimistically, in the era's fashion — expires just before the acknowledgement would have arrived. It resends. The resend is a *duplicate*, pure load, deepening the very queues that caused the lateness — which delays more acknowledgements, which fires more timers, which sends more duplicates. The network's response to congestion was to manufacture more traffic: a positive feedback loop, the civil engineering equivalent of answering a traffic jam by ordering every stationary car to send a second copy of itself. Past a critical load the system did not degrade; it *avalanched*, and settled into a stable catastrophe — the links full, almost none of it useful, forty bits of progress a second seeping through a storm of redundant copies.

The cures Jacobson and Karels shipped — into Berkeley Unix within two years, and thence into every TCP on Earth, where they remain the constitution to this day — amount to teaching every sender two kinds of manners. First: *slow start*. A new connection must not assume its fair share; it begins by sending a trickle, and doubles its confidence each round trip that returns acknowledgements — probing for the network's capacity rather than presuming it. Second, and deeper: *additive increase, multiplicative decrease*. In cruise, a connection grows its sending window by a polite constant each round trip — and at the first sign of loss, *halves* it. Grow by addition, retreat by multiplication: the asymmetry is the entire stability proof, for it guarantees that the population's collective appetite backs away from the cliff faster than it approaches. Add the repaired retransmission arithmetic — timers estimated from measured round trips and their *variance*, not wishful constants — and the principle underneath all of it, which Jacobson called conservation of packets: a connection in equilibrium should inject a new packet only when an old one leaves the network, the acknowledgement stream serving as a metronome — self-clocking, the network's own echo pacing the sender.

Two morals before the tour, sir. The first: the internet you use tonight survives on *voluntary restraint* — every endpoint running, by convention, an algorithm that sacrifices its own throughput the moment the commons shows strain; the most successful piece of cooperative behaviour ever deployed, enforced by nothing but shipped defaults. The pilot showed you that the kernel is a program; tonight begins from the fact that the *internet* is a behaviour, and the behaviour lives in the kernel's transport code — which means the behaviour of the whole world's traffic is, to this day, a Linux subsystem to a first approximation, and evolving it is tonight's closing story. And the second moral: notice *where* the fix went — into the endpoints, not the routers. The network stayed dumb; the intelligence moved to the edges; and that single architectural instinct — put the cleverness in the endpoint's kernel, keep the middle simple — is both why the internet scaled and why, forty years on, the middle grew a fossilising sediment of not-so-dumb boxes that tonight's final part must reckon with.

The route this evening: the socket machinery the pilot left at the doorstep — what the kernel actually keeps per connection, the two windows that govern all sending, and the small famous quarrel between two politeness rules that has cost the industry a cumulative eternity in mysterious forty-millisecond delays. Then the lineage of restraint: from Jacobson's manners to the loss-based dynasty to the model-based usurper now carrying much of the world's video, and the bufferbloat affair — the decade the internet spent hiding a queue in its own plumbing. Then the freight path at modern speeds — the batching, spreading, and copy-dodging by which a kernel built for kilobits carries hundreds of gigabits. And last, the two emigrations: the firewall's civilised successor, and the newest transport's departure from the kernel altogether — the bell rope, middleboxes, and the strange economics of moving the constitution upstairs.

## Part One — The Ledgers of a Connection

The pilot's tour reached the socket as a descriptor and the listen queues at the door; tonight, the household accounts behind an established connection.

For each TCP connection the kernel keeps a pair of buffers — send and receive — and they are the whole story of flow, told twice. Your program's write copies data into the send buffer and returns; transmission is the kernel's affair thereafter, per the pilot's staff-accepting-the-letter. Your program's read drains the receive buffer, where the stack has been assembling arriving segments in order. Everything else is governance of those two pools, by two windows that civilians perpetually conflate and the trade keeps rigorously apart. The *flow-control* window is the receiver's ledger: each acknowledgement advertises how much room remains in the receive buffer, and the sender may never have more than that in flight — protection of the *peer*, a slow reader automatically throttling a fast writer at any distance. The *congestion* window is Jacobson's ledger: the sender's own estimate, by slow start and additive-increase, of what the *network between* can bear — protection of the *commons*. A sender obeys the *smaller* of the two at every instant. One window defends the far house, the other the road; and half of all folkloric "TCP tuning" consists of people adjusting one while their bottleneck is the other.

The buffers themselves were, for a generation, the subject of a whole tuning literature — the receive buffer bounds the achievable throughput on long fat paths, since you cannot have more in flight than you can hold at rest, and the nineties administrator set those bounds by hand from cargo-culted tables. That literature is mostly obsolete and deserves its retirement notice: the kernel has *autotuned* both buffers for two decades — growing each connection's allocation as its bandwidth-delay product reveals itself, within global bounds that scale with the machine — and the standing advice is the season's recurring one: the dials remain, the defaults have long since learned the workloads, and the burden of proof now rests on the hand reaching for the dial.

Now the quarrel, because it is the finest small specimen in networking of correct rules composing into a wrong system — Episode Three's pattern, here reduced to two paragraphs and forty milliseconds.

Rule one, Nagle's algorithm, civilises chatty senders: a program dribbling single bytes — a terminal session, in the rule's natal era — must not launch a packet per byte, forty bytes of envelope for one of cargo; so the rule holds small writes back *while any previous data remains unacknowledged*, coalescing the dribble into respectable parcels. Rule two, delayed acknowledgement, civilises chatty receivers: do not spend a packet acknowledging every arrival; wait a beat — up to a few dozen milliseconds — in the hope of piggybacking the acknowledgement on reply data, or at least thanking two arrivals with one note. Each rule, alone, saves the commons real money. Compose them: a program performs a small write, then another small write, then awaits the reply — write, write, read, the natural rhythm of half the request-response protocols ever designed. The first write departs. The second write is *held by Nagle*, awaiting the first's acknowledgement. The receiver, holding the first parcel and seeing no reply data yet — the request being incomplete — *delays its acknowledgement*, awaiting a beat that cannot arrive, because the sender is waiting on the receiver, who is waiting on the sender. Two politeness rules, deadlocked in mutual deference on the threshold — after you, sir; after *you*, sir — until the delayed-acknowledgement timer expires and the whole exchange lurches forward. Result: request-response transactions mysteriously quantised at tens of milliseconds, on any network however fast, for any protocol that writes headers and body separately. The folk remedy sir has doubtless applied — the no-delay socket option, disabling Nagle — is thus half-diagnosis: the true crime is the *composition*, the modern position is that transactional protocols should disable Nagle and write complete messages in one gesture, and the whole affair is the season's parable in miniature: nothing failed but the interaction, and the interaction is nobody's code.

Two pieces of connection-ledger lore complete the household accounts, both of the kind that surface at three in the morning. The first: the afterlife. Close a connection and its record does not vanish; the side that closed *first* holds the corpse in a state called time-wait for a fixed spell — a minute, give or take, by ancient formula — for two sober reasons: late-wandering packets from the dead connection must be allowed to arrive and die harmlessly, lest they be mistaken for traffic of a *new* connection wearing the same four-part name (addresses and ports, both ends); and the final farewell handshake may need re-answering if its last word was lost. All correct, all venerable — and at industrial scale it becomes the famous affliction: a busy proxy opening thousands of short outbound connections a second accumulates tens of thousands of these well-intentioned corpses, each embalming one local port; the ephemeral port range is a few tens of thousands wide; and the machine begins, with perfect health and empty processors, *refusing to open new connections* for want of names. The remedies sir will recognise as received rituals, now with mechanisms attached: reuse of safely-aged corpses where timestamps prove no confusion is possible; connection pools, which amortise the whole ceremony by simply not closing; and the architectural dodge — more addresses, wider name-space — that the largest fleets quietly employ. The corpse-holding is not a defect; it is the protocol keeping faith with its own ghosts, and the operator's craft is arranging not to die so often.

The second piece is stranger and more consequential: the post office delivers *keys*. The socket interface serves local mail too — the Unix-domain socket, same verbs, no network, the kernel simply shuttling bytes between two residents — and over these local sockets, and only these, one may send a cargo no wire could carry: *a file descriptor*. The kernel performs the transfer as a true transfer of right: the descriptor arrives in the receiver's table, pointing at the very same open file description — the pilot's middle object — position, permissions and all. Sit with the implications, sir, because two seasons of machinery converge here. A process that holds a file open may *mail that capability* to a process that could never have opened it — across privilege boundaries, across container walls if a socket spans them. The privileged broker opens the guarded thing and posts the open door to an unprivileged worker: this is how modern service managers hand pre-opened ports to services (the pilot's socket activation, mechanically), how sandboxed renderers receive exactly the files the user chose in a dialog and nothing else, and — a promissory note for Episode Ten — how a Wayland client receives its window's very buffers: the drawing room, when we reach it, will turn out to run on posted descriptors. The pilot's slogan was that everything is a file descriptor; tonight's amendment is that the descriptor is not merely a handle but a *deed*, and the house has a registry post for conveyancing.

## Part Two — The Lineage of Restraint

Jacobson's manners froze into a dynasty. The founding algorithm — retreat by half on loss, probe by addition otherwise — reigned through the nineties under the name Reno and its refinements; but its throne rested on an assumption worth stating baldly, because dethroning it took thirty years: *loss means congestion*. The queue overflows, a packet drops, the sender hears silence and retreats — packet loss conscripted as the network's only signalling flag.

The dynasty's long middle age was spent patching that assumption's edges. On long fat paths, halving is catastrophe — a transatlantic connection that halves must spend hundreds of round trips clawing back, so the current mainstream king, CUBIC — a Korean academic design, our house's default for nearly two decades and thereby the de facto law of most of the world's traffic — reshapes the recovery: after a retreat it regrows along a cubic curve, cautious near the old cliff-edge, bold far from it, and clock-driven rather than acknowledgement-driven so that distant connections are not punished for their distance. A dozen rival claimants live in the kernel beside it — the pluggable congestion-control interface is a small parliament — but CUBIC's tenure has been the stable centre.

Then the assumption itself failed, publicly, in the strangest way: the network got *too much memory*. Router buffers, priced by the megabyte's collapse, swelled — and a swollen buffer defers the drop. The queue grows seconds deep before anything is lost; loss-based senders, seeing no loss, press on serenely, *keeping the buffer full as a matter of policy* — for a loss-based sender's equilibrium is precisely "fill until it drops". The name the affair acquired — *bufferbloat*, coined around twenty ten when Jim Gettys, debugging his own home connection, found his uploads adding whole seconds of latency to everything else in the house — described a planetary condition: links everywhere carrying, in their queues, standing delay that served nobody, invisible to throughput tests, ruinous to every interactive thing — the video call that stutters while a backup runs, the game that dies when someone streams. Sir has lived this; most of civilisation has.

The repairs came from both ends, and both live in our house. In the middle: smarter queues — the modern discipline, on Linux by default for a decade now, that refuses to be a dumb deep pipe: it keeps *per-flow* queues, serves them fairly so one glutton cannot bury the conversationalists, and — the clever heart, an algorithm called CoDel — watches not queue *length* but queue *sojourn time*, the age of packets as they leave, and begins deliberately dropping early when packets have been standing too long, restoring the very signal the deep buffer had muffled. The home-router firmware movements carried it to the bloated edge, and the difference, on an afflicted line, is not subtle. And at the endpoint: the usurper. BBR — bottleneck bandwidth and round-trip time, from Google in twenty sixteen — abandons the dynasty's founding creed outright. It does not wait for loss and does not interpret it as gospel; it *models* the path — continuously estimating the bottleneck's bandwidth and the route's true round trip — and paces transmission to sit at the model's optimum: the link full, the queue *empty*, the operating point Jacobson's own mathematics had named as ideal and loss-based probing can never rest at. It carries, as I record, the traffic of the world's largest video services and a growing share of everything else; its early versions drew academic fire for shouldering loss-based flows aside at shared bottlenecks — a fairness quarrel the later revisions have laboured to settle — and its deeper significance is architectural, a flag planted for tonight's finale: congestion control has become *a thing operators deploy at their endpoints*, evolving at software speed, no longer a fixed constitution but a competitive frontier — which is only possible, note well, because the endpoints' kernels could be changed. Hold that thought through the final part, where we meet the parts of the network that *cannot* be.

## Part Three — The Freight Path at Speed

The pilot walked a packet up from the ring buffer through the polite adaptive protocol between card and kernel; Episode Three priced the locks; Episode Five's freight bureau taught the season's scaling maxims. Tonight assembles them at line rate, where the arithmetic is brutal: at tens of gigabits, a naive per-packet path — an interrupt, a set of lock acquisitions, a traversal, a wakeup, per fifteen-hundred-byte parcel — spends the whole machine on ceremony. The modern path is a systematic refusal to do *anything* once per packet.

The refusals, in ascending altitude. At the card: coalescing — the receive path gathers consecutive segments of one flow into a single jumbo parcel before the stack sees them, so the traversal, the routing, the socket lookup happen once per *batch*; the transmit path runs the same trick backward, the stack handing the card one great segment and the *hardware* slicing it into wire-sized pieces and stamping the envelopes, checksums delegated likewise — the kernel dealing in wholesale lots, the card retailing. Across the cores: the pilot's one-core-saturated pathology answered in silicon and software — the card itself hashes each flow to one of many receive queues, one per processor, interrupts spread accordingly, each flow's packets always to the same core so that Episode Three's cache locality holds and no lock is shared across a flow; the freight bureau's multi-queue reformation, you notice, arrived in the network years earlier, for the same reasons, under the same maxim: one hand, one ledger. Up the wall: the copies dodged — the classic static-file path, disk to socket, once cost two boundary crossings and two full copies of every byte through upstairs memory; the *sendfile* gesture the pilot's descriptor plumbing made possible tells the kernel to move file pages to the wire directly, the page cache lending its pages to the transmit path, nothing ascending the stairs at all; its modern descendants extend the same courtesy to program memory pinned for the card. And at the summit, the pilot's io_uring: the bell rope amortised, submissions and completions in shared rings, the per-call toll — which Meltdown's locks made dearer, per Part One of the pilot — paid once per batch.

Beyond the last refusal lies apostasy proper, and the house tolerates two sects. The radical one vacates the premises: kernel-bypass frameworks hand the card's queues bodily to a userspace program, which polls them with a dedicated spinning core — no interrupts, no kernel, no sharing, latency in fractions of a microsecond; the trading shops and the telecom packet-cores live here, paying the price of the exit: every service the stack provided — transport, firewalling, the accumulated case law of forty years — must be reimplemented or forsworn upstairs. And the reformed sect, which is our house's own answer, sir has already met at the pilot's close: the express desk — XDP — where a verified program, the notarised errand of the pilot, runs *in the driver itself*, at the earliest instant, dropping the volumetric attack or redirecting the flow before the stack has spent a cycle of ceremony on it; the load balancers fronting some of the largest services on Earth are such programs, and the bypass radicals' best benchmarks are met, for those workloads, without leaving the building. The pattern the season has now seen thrice — the elevator's *none*, the buffers' autotune, and here the path's whole modern history — deserves its aphorism: below stairs, progress is increasingly the art of *not doing things*, and of arranging that what must be done is done in bulk.

## Part Four — Two Emigrations

Two final stories, both departures, both consequences of everything above.

The first emigration is domestic: the firewall's. The pilot showed you firewalling as tables consulted at five fixed moments; the tables' administration, for a quarter-century, meant iptables — a system sir has doubtless cursed with feeling, and whose faults were structural, not cosmetic. Each protocol family kept its own parallel apparatus — one for the old internet protocol, another for the new, more for bridging and beyond — rules duplicated across them by hand; a large ruleset was evaluated *linearly*, every packet interrogated against rule after rule, cost growing with bureaucracy; and updates replaced the *entire table* wholesale, so that busy automation — a container platform reprogramming the doors per deployment, which is precisely modern life — contended and stalled. The successor, nftables, mainlined a decade ago and now the default engine behind even the old command's costume, is the sensible constitution: one engine for all families; rules compiled to bytecode for a small in-kernel virtual machine — a cousin of the pilot's notarised errands, though with its own dialect; *sets and maps* as first-class matter, so that ten thousand addresses are one hashed lookup rather than ten thousand rules; and *atomic, incremental* update, the bureaucracy amendable without dissolution. The migration's sociology is the season's usual: the shim keeps old incantations working, the distributions crossed years ago, and the cargo-cult stratum — tutorials, muscle memory, pasted rules — will take a generation to follow. Meanwhile the container world's dirty secret, which sir's own machines exhibit: the platforms program *both* engines and several philosophies at once, service routing having been quietly annexed in many fleets by the pilot's express-desk programs — the firewall less a wall now than an archaeological site with every stratum load-bearing.

The second emigration is the profound one: the transport itself has left the building. QUIC — standardised at the turn of this decade, born at Google, now carrying an ample share of all web traffic as the floor of HTTP-three — is a full reliable transport: streams, acknowledgements, retransmission, congestion control, the entire Jacobson inheritance — implemented *in userspace*, in libraries, over the kernel's plainest datagram socket. The kernel that perfected TCP for forty years is used, by the newest transport, as a dumb pipe. The reasons are two, and both are season-theses wearing network costume.

Reason one: *head-of-line blocking*, TCP's original sin for the web's shape. TCP sells one ordered byte stream; lose one packet and everything behind it — bytes for unrelated requests multiplexed on the same connection — waits in the receive buffer for the retransmission: one lost parcel stalls the whole van. QUIC multiplexes independent streams *within* its own framing: a loss stalls only the stream it struck. Reason two, the deeper: *ossification* — and here Part Two's flag is collected. The fix for head-of-line blocking could, in principle, have been TCP amendments; such amendments were designed, repeatedly, and they *could not be deployed* — because the middle of the network, dumb by founding doctrine, had silted up with boxes that inspect transport headers: firewalls, translators, load balancers, each encoding yesterday's TCP in silicon and dropping, mangling, or "normalising" anything novel. TCP's wire image had become a public commons on which every middlebox squatted; the protocol was frozen not by any authority but by a million petty inspectors, and the internet's great extensibility story ended at the transport layer decades ago. QUIC's response is deliberate, structural, and slightly ruthless: *encrypt the transport layer itself* — sequence numbers, acknowledgements, the machinery — so that middleboxes see only ciphertext over datagrams and *cannot* ossify what they cannot read; the protocol carries its own privacy as an anti-fossilisation measure, evolvability re-purchased by cryptographic force. And the choice of userspace is the same logic pointed at the endpoints: a transport in the kernel evolves at the pace of the world's slowest fleet upgrade; a transport in a library ships with the browser's fortnightly release — the constitution moved upstairs precisely so that it can be amended.

The bill for the move is the one the season has trained sir to compute before the waiter arrives: the bell-rope toll, per datagram now rather than per stream, and the forty-year path optimisations — the coalescing, the offloads, all of Part Three — built for TCP and initially useless to the émigré. Early QUIC burned multiples of TCP's processor per byte, and the repair programme is the house's present frontier and a neat inversion of the whole episode: the kernel now grows *datagram-batch* offloads and segmentation assists so that the userspace transport can hand it wholesale lots; io_uring feeds the same rings; there are serious proposals, in the standard pattern, to draw the hot path back below stairs once the dust of evolution settles. The transport left the kernel to escape history, sir, and the kernel is now renovating to win it back — which is not a defeat for either party but the house's oldest dance: the boundary between upstairs and down was never a wall; it is a negotiation, renewed each decade, over where cleverness pays.

A closing witness, and the comparative choir earns its keep one more time. NT's waiting philosophy, since its VMS childhood, has been *completion*: hand the kernel a buffer and be told when the work is done — the model its port-of-completion machinery made famous and around which all serious Windows server software is built. Our house's tradition is *readiness*: be told a descriptor is ready, then act — the pilot's one-way-to-wait, epoll as its industrial form. Forty years the two philosophies stood as the textbook contrast — and the pilot's closing novelty, io_uring, is, on inspection, our house adopting completion at last: submissions in, completions out, buffers owned by the kernel meanwhile. Cutler's model, sir, arriving below our stairs a generation late and by convergent evolution — file that beside Windows shipping our kernel, and score the long war, honestly, as each house conceding the other's best point.

## The Books of the Evening

The accounts, briskly. The internet's stability is voluntary restraint shipped as defaults — slow start and add-then-halve born from a forty-bit afternoon in eighty-six, conservation of packets its physics. A connection is two buffers and two windows — the peer's ledger and the road's — with autotuning having retired a literature of dials, and the Nagle-versus-delayed-acknowledgement deadlock standing as the miniature of correct rules composing wrongly. The dynasty of loss — Reno to CUBIC — met its refutation in the bloated buffer; the queues learned to judge by sojourn time and the usurper BBR to model the road rather than crash into it; congestion control became a deployable, evolving thing. The freight path at speed is the art of doing nothing per packet: coalescing at the card, a queue per core, copies dodged, the bell amortised, with bypass as apostasy and the express desk as the reformed answer. The firewall emigrated to one engine with sets, maps, and atomic amendment; the transport emigrated altogether — QUIC escaping the middleboxes' fossil record by encrypting its own machinery, paying the upstairs toll, and drawing the kernel into a new negotiation over the hot path. And the two great waiting philosophies of the age ended their war in mutual adoption.

The season's standing debts hold — the microkernel question at compound interest for Episode Eleven, trusting-trust for Episode Eight — and next week collects a different bill entirely. We go to the tradesmen's entrance: devices and drivers, the code that runs below stairs on the say-so of strangers. The hook is the most expensive parser error in history: a Friday in July of twenty twenty-four, a malformed data file, a driver that trusted it, and eight and a half million machines — airports, hospitals, banks — boot-looping in unison, worldwide, before lunch. What runs in the kernel, sir, and on whose word: the question of the age, and next week's whole subject.

Until then, sir.

*— End of Episode Six.*
