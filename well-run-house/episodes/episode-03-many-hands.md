# Episode Three -- Many Hands, and the Sorrows of Sharing

*The Well-Run House -- A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux*

## The Reboot on Another Planet

Good day, sir.

On the fourth of July, nineteen ninety-seven, an American spacecraft named Mars Pathfinder bounced to a halt on the surface of another world, cocooned in airbags, carrying a rover the size of a microwave oven and a great deal of national television coverage. The landing was flawless. The instruments woke. The data began to flow. And then, a few days into the mission, with the world watching, the lander's computer began -- quietly, repeatedly, maddeningly -- to reboot itself.

No crash dump, no error report; just the watchdog timer expiring, the system resetting, and a day's science lost each time it did. The public was told of "software glitches"; the engineering press speculated freely; and at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, the flight software team began one of the great debugging sessions of our profession -- conducted against a computer that was, at the time, somewhere over a hundred million miles away.

What they had to work with was a duplicate of the spacecraft in the laboratory, and a piece of luck that was not luck at all but engineering: the flight system -- a commercial real-time operating system called VxWorks -- could be run with tracing on, every event journalled. The team drove the replica for days, trying to make it fail as the real one was failing. In the small hours, at last, it did -- after everyone but one engineer had gone home, which is the detail that tells you the story is true. And the trace told them everything.

Here is what was happening on Mars, sir, and I shall tell it in the vocabulary of this house even though the house in question was a different one, because the disease is universal. Three tasks are involved. A low-priority task -- call it the meteorology clerk -- occasionally takes a *lock* on a shared corridor: a data structure through which the spacecraft's components exchange information. A high-priority task -- the bus manager, the most important resident in the house -- periodically needs that same corridor, and if the clerk holds the lock, the manager waits; briefly, by design; no harm intended. But between those two ranks lives a third resident: a middle-priority communications task, long-running and entirely innocent, with no interest in the corridor whatever. And the scheduler -- doing exactly what a strict priority scheduler is sworn to do -- runs the *middle* task in preference to the *low* one, always. So the clerk, holding the lock, never gets a moment on the processor to finish his errand and release it. The manager waits on the clerk; the clerk waits on the scheduler; the scheduler serves the middleman, who outranks the clerk and knows nothing of any of it. The most important task on the spacecraft is starved by the least important, with a bystander as the unwitting instrument.

They called the disease *priority inversion*: the man of highest rank reduced, through a lock, to the effective rank of whoever holds it. On Mars, the deadline-watching watchdog eventually noticed the bus manager missing appointments and did the only thing a watchdog knows: reset the machine.

The cure had been sitting aboard the spacecraft all along. VxWorks mutexes carry an option called *priority inheritance*: while a humble task holds a lock that a grand one is waiting on, the humble task temporarily *becomes grand* -- it inherits the waiter's priority, outranks the middleman, finishes its errand, releases the lock, and subsides to its former station. The option was off -- a global default the team had chosen in development, when the analysis suggested the corridor's locks were held too briefly to matter. The fix, uplinked across the void, was in essence the change of one stored value from off to on: the spacecraft's own debugging shell accepted the patch, the flag flipped, and the resets never returned. The engineer who led the diagnosis, Glenn Reeves, published a gracious account which has become a founding text of the trade; its most quoted moral is that the failure occurred in exactly the place the team had reasoned, months earlier, could not fail badly enough to matter.

I open the concurrency episode here for the obvious reason and one less obvious. The obvious: this is the finest concurrency bug on record -- interplanetary, diagnosed by trace, cured by a single flag, and caused by three innocent components each behaving correctly. The less obvious, sir, is the shape of the thing. *Nothing was broken.* The scheduler followed its rules. The clerk followed his. The middleman did nothing at all but exist. Concurrency's sorrows are almost never a component failing; they are correct components *composed* into a failure -- the whole strictly greater, and stranger, than the sum of its blameless parts. That is what makes this subject the hardest in the house, and it is why tonight proceeds by disaster: each mechanism will be introduced alongside the precise catastrophe it exists to prevent.

The rooms, in order: what sharing memory actually means, and the smallest possible disaster -- two hands, one number; the locks that prevent it, and the sorrows the locks then invent -- deadlock, contention, and our Martian friend inversion, including how our own house came to spend a decade dismantling one giant lock; then below the locks to the futex, the mechanism the pilot's leap-second storm was made of, where we shall pay that old debt properly; and finally the escape routes -- the structures that need no locks at all, including the kernel's most beautiful trick, a library that never closes for refurbishment. At the door I shall name, honestly, the depths I am deliberately not taking you to.

Many hands, sir. In we go.

## Part One -- Two Hands, One Number

Strip everything away and the primal scene is this. A number lives in memory -- a counter, say: visits, or pennies, or packets. Two threads each wish to add one to it. Each performs, invisibly, three steps: read the current value into a register; add one; write the result back. Now interleave them unluckily: both read *five*; both compute six; both write six. Two increments, one effect. A penny has vanished, and no line of either thread's code is wrong.

That is a *race condition* -- the outcome depends on the interleaving, and the interleaving depends on scheduling, load, interrupt timing, the phase of the moon. I want you to notice three properties, because they are the fingerprints of the whole genus. It is *silent*: nothing faults, nothing logs; the books are simply wrong. It is *rare* in inverse proportion to how much you test: the window is three instructions wide, and strikes only when *preemption* -- the scheduler's standing right, which the pilot demonstrated, to suspend a running thread between any two instructions and seat another in its place -- falls exactly there; or, on sir's actual machines, when two processors execute the steps genuinely simultaneously, no preemption required. And it is *unreproducible on demand*, which is the property that breaks engineers' spirits: the bug is not in the code, where you are looking; it is in the *timing*, which is nowhere.

The fix must make the three steps *atomic* -- indivisible, from the Greek for what cannot be cut: either both hands see the whole read-add-write of the other, or neither sees any of it. Modern processors sell atomicity in two sizes, and the whole edifice of tonight stands on them. The small size: single atomic instructions -- add-to-memory as one uninterruptible act, or the subtler *compare-and-swap*: write this new value only if the old value is still what I last saw, and tell me whether you succeeded. Pocket compare-and-swap, sir; it returns in Part Three as the hinge of everything. The large size: when the work to be protected is bigger than one instruction -- a whole structure to reshape, a corridor to walk -- atomic instructions alone cannot cover it, and one builds instead a *critical section*: a stretch of code that at most one thread may occupy at a time, enforced by a lock.

The lock -- *mutex*, for mutual exclusion, and I shall use the term of art from here -- has a contract of surpassing simplicity: acquire it before entering; release it on leaving; if another holds it, *wait*. All the subtlety hides in that last verb, and below stairs there are two ways to wait, whose difference the pilot has already equipped you to price. A *spinlock* waits by asking again -- a tight loop on the processor, burning cycles, checking the lock like a man rattling a doorknob. A *sleeping lock* waits by the pilot's mechanism of virtuous surrender: the thread blocks, the scheduler runs someone else, the eventual release wakes the sleeper. The doorknob is insultingly wasteful -- unless the wait is *very* short, in which case it is brilliant, because the sleeping alternative costs two context switches, and the pilot priced those for you: register state, address spaces, the cache refilled from cold. The kernel therefore uses both with doctrine attached: spinlocks for its own tiny below-stairs critical sections -- held for nanoseconds, never while sleeping, sometimes with interrupts masked, since an interrupt handler contending for a lock its own interrupted victim holds is deadlock in one processor flat; sleeping locks for everything longer. And the modern mutex your programs use is a hybrid: rattle the knob a few times on the chance the holder is quick, then sleep. Optimism first, economics after.

One more primitive belongs in the starter set, because the mutex alone cannot express the commonest wish in concurrent life: *wait until something is true*. The till is empty; the clerk must wait not for a lock but for money to arrive. Polling -- take the lock, peek, release, nap, repeat -- is the doorknob-rattle at a grander scale, and the house's answer is the *condition variable*: a waiting room attached to a stated condition. The clerk takes the mutex, finds the till empty, and performs the one subtle gesture of the piece -- *releases the mutex and lies down in the waiting room as a single atomic act* -- so that no coin can arrive in the crack between his looking and his lying down. The depositor, having added money under the same mutex, *signals* the condition variable -- that is the interface's own verb for the knock on the waiting-room door, and *signal*, with its louder sibling *broadcast* for waking the whole room, are the names every manual will use; the clerk wakes *holding the mutex again*, and -- the detail that separates working code from folklore -- checks the till *again* before proceeding, because between the signal and his rising some other clerk may have emptied it, and because the house explicitly reserves the right to the occasional *spurious wakeup* -- a knock with nobody behind it, permitted by the contract in so many words. Wait in a loop, re-checking the condition: the rule is absolute, it is the first thing every concurrency text says and the first thing every beginner disbelieves, and you now know both halves of why. File also that atomic release-and-sleep: it is the third appearance of tonight's recurring shape -- check and commit as one act -- and when we descend to the futex you will find it is the same machinery all the way down.

So far, one lock and one waiting room, and the world seems manageable. The sorrows begin when there are two locks.

## Part Two -- The Sorrows of Ownership

Give the house two locks and the residents will discover, within the week, the oldest trap in the literature. Thread one holds the cellar key and wants the pantry key; thread two holds the pantry key and wants the cellar key. Each waits, courteously, forever. That is *deadlock*, and its structure is worth one precise sentence: it requires a cycle -- a ring of holders, each waiting on the next -- and it requires that locks be held *while* acquiring more. Break either property and the trap cannot close.

Which yields the standard defences, in ascending order of dignity. One may detect the cycle and break it by force -- databases do this, since they cannot legislate what transactions ask for; one victim is chosen, rolled back, and politely told to try again; the pilot's transactional world lives on this. Or one may *prevent* the cycle by decree: a global ordering of locks -- cellar before pantry, always, for everyone -- so that no ring can form, because rings require someone to acquire against the order. The kernel lives by this discipline. Thousands of locks, and for the sober majority a documented hierarchy: memory before this, scheduler before that, and a debugging apparatus -- a proving ground called lockdep -- that watches every acquisition in a test kernel and *screams* on the first ordering violation, not when deadlock occurs but when it first becomes possible. The trade's summary is worth engraving: deadlock is not a bug you find; it is a bug you make impossible, or a bug you meet in production.

The subtler sorrow is not stopping but *slowing*, and it is the one that reshaped our house's entire skeleton, so let me tell it as the history it is.

When Linux first grew to two processors, in the middle nineties, the kernel's concurrency plan was the simplest thing that could work: one lock. The Big Kernel Lock, and the name was official. Whichever processor entered the kernel took it; the other processor, needing the kernel, waited. Two processors, and below stairs still a strict one-at-a-time affair -- the entire staff funnelled through a single doorway. It worked, in the way a bridge of one plank works, and its inadequacy grew precisely as fast as processor counts did. The decade-and-a-half that followed -- from the giant lock, to locks per subsystem, to locks per structure, to locks per *piece* of structure -- is the story of the kernel's adolescence, and its every chapter has one plot: find what the single doorway serialises, split it, and inherit a new family of possible deadlocks and a new ordering to document. The final fragments of the Big Kernel Lock were not swept out until twenty eleven -- sixteen years of demolition -- and the maxim the era bequeathed is the one I want you to keep: *a lock's cost is not the acquiring; it is the queue.* Locks are cheap; *contention* is ruinous; and the whole craft of scaling is arranging for hands not to want the same thing at the same moment. When Episode Four shows you memory kept per-processor, and Episode Six shows you network queues kept per-processor, you are seeing this maxim built into architecture -- sharing avoided outright, which outranks sharing managed.

And lest the Big Kernel Lock seem a quaint fossil of the nineties, sir, permit me to point out that you have almost certainly spent this very week programming under its twin. The Python interpreter -- the duct tape of sir's profession and mine -- has carried since its infancy a Global Interpreter Lock: one lock, taken by whichever thread would execute Python, so that however many threads a Python program spawns, *one* advances the interpreter at a time. It is the Big Kernel Lock's exact structure transplanted upstairs -- adopted for the same reasons (simplicity, and the safety of a single doorway around bookkeeping never designed for many hands), defended for decades with the same arguments (single-threaded speed, and the vast estate of extensions built on the doorway's guarantees), and dismantled, now, with the same grinding caution: the free-threaded interpreter -- the lock removed, the bookkeeping made truly concurrent -- arrived in recent years as a build-it-yourself option and is, as I record, working its way toward respectability, twenty-odd years after the first serious attempt was abandoned because removing the lock made single-threaded programs slower, which is precisely the toll the kernel paid and precisely why the demolition took a decade there too. The pattern, sir, is a law of nature in this trade: every successful system begins with one big lock, grows rich enough to afford the demolition, and spends ten years paying for it. The only variable is the year the invoice arrives.

And the third sorrow you have already watched play out on another planet, so here let me pay the hook's technical balance. Priority inversion is not exotic; it is *latent in the arithmetic* of any system that has both priorities and locks, which is to say every system, and its cure is now standard equipment. Our house's real-time mutexes implement priority inheritance exactly as the Martian fix did: the holder of a contended lock is boosted, for the duration, to the priority of its grandest waiter -- the boost chaining, if need be, through a whole file of holders. You are relying on it daily, sir, in a place you would not guess: the telephone again. Android's binder -- the inter-process courier we shall properly meet in Episode Eleven -- carries priority *with the message*: the humble service handling a foreground application's request is boosted while it serves, precisely so that the visible, finger-on-glass work is never starved by machinery of middling rank. And the audio path -- the one subsystem where a missed deadline is not degradation but an audible *click*, the ear being the strictest scheduler in the house -- runs on real-time priorities threaded through this same inheritance machinery from tap to speaker.

Which obliges me to say plainly what "real-time" means below stairs, because the term is sold in two grades and the difference is a worldview. Our house offers real-time *scheduling classes*: policies that outrank every ordinary task, unthrottled but for a safety valve -- a reserved sliver of each second kept for the ordinary world, so that a runaway real-time loop cannot seize the machine entire; sir may recall the pilot's principle that there must always be a way to stop a program that has declined to cooperate. There is even a class scheduled by declared *deadline* -- tell the kernel how much time you need by when, and be admitted only if the sums close, the kernel actually performing the admission arithmetic and refusing the over-subscribed. This is real time as a *service*. What it is not -- and the distinction was, for twenty years, the moat around systems like our Martian friend VxWorks and the QNX of Episode Eleven -- is real time as a *guarantee*: a bound on the worst case, provable, of how long the kernel itself can keep you waiting. General-purpose kernels held long unpreemptible stretches; the patient real-time faction spent two decades -- the *preempt-RT* project, one of the longest engineering campaigns in the kernel's history -- grinding those stretches down: making the kernel preemptible nearly everywhere, converting its spinlocks to sleeping, inheritance-bearing locks, and *threading* its interrupts -- the handler's work moved out of the interrupt itself into an ordinary kernel thread, schedulable and preemptible like any other resident, so that even the doorbell takes its turn -- a manoeuvre the pilot glimpsed in passing. As I record this, that campaign has ended in victory -- merged, at last, into the ordinary mainline kernel; the same system that runs sir's servers can be configured, unpatched, to make promises a machine tool can bet a finger on. Tanenbaum's heirs -- the microkernel party, whose professor famously told the young Linus that a monolithic kernel was obsolete on arrival -- will note, correctly, that a microkernel had those promises in the eighties; the reply, which is the season's refrain, is that the general-purpose house got them eventually *without ceasing to be general*, and history has consistently paid that combination the higher price.

## Part Three -- The Bell Rung Only on Quarrels

Now below the mutex, to the mechanism it is made of -- and to a debt outstanding since the pilot's opening minutes.

Ask where waiting *happens* and the pilot's architecture forces the question. Sleeping is the kernel's business -- queues, scheduling, waking: all below stairs, all reachable only by ringing the bell. Must every mutex acquisition, then, be a system call? In the early two-thousands, when threaded programs became the norm, that was intolerable arithmetic: the overwhelming majority of acquisitions are *uncontended* -- nobody holds the lock; take it and go -- and paying a boundary crossing for the unanimous case -- the trap below stairs and back, registers saved and restored, caches disturbed, hundreds of cycles spent on a lock that was free all along -- is the tax the pilot taught you to hunt.

The answer, added to our house in two thousand and two, is the *futex* -- the fast userspace mutex -- and it is less a mechanism than a treaty between the floors. The fast path stays upstairs entirely: the lock is one word of ordinary shared memory, and acquisition is a single *compare-and-swap* -- if the word says free, atomically write taken; success is silent, instantaneous, invisible to the kernel, free. Only on *contention* -- the word already says taken -- does anyone ring: the loser calls down, "put me to sleep on the address of this word, *if it still holds the value I saw*"; the release path's loser-aware holder calls down, "wake the sleepers on this address". The kernel keeps no ledger of locks at all -- merely, on demand, queues of sleepers keyed by memory address, conjured when a quarrel starts and gone when it ends. Locks upstairs, waiting downstairs, and the boundary crossed *only when there is an actual dispute to adjudicate*: the design is so exactly the house's philosophy that one could teach the whole course from this one mechanism. Every mutex, semaphore, and condition variable in every threaded program on the machine -- every language runtime's synchronisation, behind whatever costume -- bottoms out in this treaty.

The conditional clause in the sleeper's request -- *if it still holds the value I saw* -- is doing quiet, load-bearing work, and the disaster it prevents deserves a slow telling. Two actors, sir, and the whole affair turns on the gap between their moves: the *loser*, who has just seen the word say taken and resolved to go below and sleep; and the *holder*, who is at this very moment finishing and about to release. Move one: the loser reads the word -- taken -- and turns toward the stairs, the sleeping request not yet made. Move two: in exactly that gap, the holder writes the word free and calls down, "wake the sleepers on this address." The kernel looks; there are no sleepers yet; the wake evaporates, having woken nobody -- for wakes are not stored, only delivered. Move three: the loser, knowing nothing of this, arrives below and lies down to await a knock that has already come and gone. Nobody holds the lock now; nobody will ever call down again; the sleeper sleeps forever. That is the *lost wakeup*, the sleeper unfindable, the bug eternal -- and the conditional clause abolishes it: the kernel, before granting the nap, reads the word once more *under its own lock*, finds it free, refuses the nap, and sends the loser back upstairs to try again. File the shape: *check the condition and commit to waiting as one atomic act* -- it is the same shape as compare-and-swap, one level up, and it recurs everywhere waiting is built.

And now the pilot's opening disaster can be read at full depth, as promised -- and let me restate the event itself, so that tonight stands on its own floor. A *leap second* is the extra second the world's timekeepers occasionally splice into civil time to keep the clocks honest with the slowing Earth; at midsummer's midnight in twenty twelve the kernel absorbed one by stepping its clock -- and the step was made without the notification that re-anchors sleeping deadlines. Where were the deadline-sleepers sleeping? On futexes -- every timed wait in every threaded runtime is a futex wait with an expiry. The expiries, computed against the pre-step clock, all came due at once and *stayed* due: each waiter woke, found its condition unmet, recomputed a timeout already in the past, and slept again for no time at all, around and around at processor speed. Multiply by every timed wait in every Java runtime and database on Earth and you have the pinned processors doing nothing whatsoever, with tremendous enthusiasm, that opened this course. The cure -- setting the clock to itself -- re-anchored the deadlines. The pilot could tell you what happened; you now know, one mechanism deep, *why*.

One more sorrow lives at this level, and its name has entered the language. Wake *all* the sleepers when a resource frees -- and a thousand threads rise, race for one lock, and nine hundred and ninety-nine go back to sleep, having accomplished a context switch each: the *thundering herd*, a stampede purchasing nothing. The refinements are what you would design in the second draft: wake one, not all; and for the composite waits that condition variables produce -- everyone wakes on the broadcast, then everyone queues on the same mutex -- a futex operation that *re-queues*: wake one, and transfer the rest, asleep, from the condition's queue to the mutex's queue, so they never rise at all until the lock itself is theirs. Herds managed below stairs, where the beds are.

## Part Four -- The Library That Never Closes

The maxim of Part Two -- contention is the ruin -- has a logical conclusion the kernel pursued to its end: the best lock is the one nobody takes. Tonight's last room holds the escape routes.

Before the grand escapes, one small specialised lock deserves its paragraph, because sir consults it several thousand times an hour without knowing. Reader-writer locks -- many readers or one writer -- are the obvious refinement of the mutex for read-mostly data, and their obvious cost is that readers must still *register*, stamping a shared counter whose cache line becomes, at scale, the very contention it was bought to avoid. For small, hot, seldom-written data the kernel has a slyer instrument: the *sequence lock* -- *seqlock*, the sources compress it, one word, and that is the name to search. The writer keeps a counter beside the data, incrementing it once on beginning an update and once on finishing -- odd, therefore, exactly while the data is mid-change. A reader takes no lock at all: it reads the counter, reads the data, reads the counter again, and if the two counter readings match and are even, the data is coherent -- otherwise the reader simply *reads again*. Readers pay nothing but the risk of a retry; writers never wait on readers at all; the only casualty is the guarantee that a reader finishes on schedule, which for a three-word structure is no casualty worth the name. And the reason I promise you use it hourly: the pilot's hall clock -- the *vDSO*, the manuals name it, a small vee and three capitals said one at a time, short for *virtual dynamic shared object*: the page the kernel maps into every process so that the time can be read without a system call -- is guarded exactly so. The kernel's timekeeper updates the clock fields under a sequence counter; your program's timestamp read spins that tiny read-check-retry dance in the hallway, no bell, no lock, no contention with a thousand other clock-readers. The pilot told you the staff hung a clock where the family could read it; tonight you learn the clock has a shutter, and the read is timed between blinks.

The first escape you have already seen twice without the label: *do not share*. The pilot's one-queue-per-processor scheduler; the per-processor pools Episode Four will show you in the memory allocator; the per-processor everything that modern subsystems reach for first. Give each hand its own ledger and consolidate on the rare occasions truth is needed globally: no lock, because no sharing; the fastest critical section is the one that does not exist. Its philosophical extension -- share nothing, communicate by message -- is a whole civilisation of design we shall visit properly in Episode Eleven, where a certain Canadian microkernel builds the entire operating system out of it. But one member of that civilisation lives on sir's own servers and deserves its passage tonight: the Go language, whose entire concurrency offer is a bet placed *against* the kernel thread. A goroutine is not a thread; it is a few kilobytes of stack and a ledger entry in the Go runtime, which schedules hundreds of thousands of them -- cooperatively, upstairs, at function-call granularity -- onto a small pool of genuine kernel threads, roughly one per processor. The arithmetic it is exploiting is precisely tonight's: a kernel context switch costs what the pilot priced, and a futex sleep costs a bell-ring, but switching between two goroutines costs a function call -- the runtime simply moves its own registers and stack pointers, upstairs, no kernel in sight. The house's role shrinks to landlord: it schedules the pool; the runtime schedules the crowd. The seams show exactly where you would predict -- when a goroutine rings the bell and blocks, it takes its precious kernel thread down with it, so the runtime detects the loss and conscripts another; and the network waiting that would sink the whole scheme is routed instead through *epoll* -- the kernel's readiness desk, the single shared waiting mechanism the pilot promised covers everything: hand it a crowd of descriptors and sleep until any of them stirs -- one thread minding ten thousand sockets on the crowd's behalf. Channels -- Go's typed pipes, the share-nothing sacrament -- complete the doctrine, and the language's famous slogan makes the theology explicit: do not communicate by sharing memory; share memory by communicating. It is the microkernel argument, sir, fought to a draw *inside a language runtime* -- and the fact that the fashionable concurrency of the twenty-twenties is green threads over message channels, atop a kernel whose own concurrency is locks over shared memory, is a peace treaty of exactly the kind Episode One taught you to read: both armies won, on different floors of the same house.

The second escape: share, but *without locks* -- the lock-free structures, built directly on Part One's compare-and-swap. The flavour in one design: to push onto a shared list, prepare your node, read the current head, point your node at it, and compare-and-swap the head from the value you read to your node -- if some other hand moved the head meanwhile, the swap reports failure and you simply *retry* against the new state. No lock, no sleeping, no inversion -- a thread preempted mid-operation delays nobody, which is the property (the trade calls it lock-freedom) that locks can never give: somebody *always* makes progress. The price is a school of subtleties I shall name and not enter -- foremost the pleasingly named *ABA problem*, in which the head returns to a value you recognise while the world behind it has been demolished and rebuilt, and your swap succeeds into wreckage. Clever, treacherous, and -- the honest summary -- a specialist's instrument: the kernel uses lock-free technique in hot corners, and mortals use libraries written by the three people who enjoy the proofs.

But the third escape, sir, is the beautiful one, and it is the kernel's own signature move: *read-copy-update*, RCU, the mechanism I promised as a library that never closes.

Begin from the observation that drives it: the kernel's hottest shared structures -- the routing table, the roll of open files, the directory cache -- are read overwhelmingly and changed rarely. A lock, even a sophisticated one, taxes every reader to protect against the rare writer; reader-writer locks still make readers announce themselves, and on many processors the announcing *is* the contention -- everyone stamping the same counter. RCU's proposition is radical: readers shall do *nothing*. No lock, no counter, no announcement -- a reader simply notes, locally and for free, "I am reading", walks the structure at full speed, and notes "done". The entire burden moves to the writer, and the writer's discipline is the trick's whole substance.

The writer never edits in place. To change the library's edition, it *copies* the page in question, amends the copy, and then -- with one atomic pointer-swing, Part One's small-size atomicity -- publishes: the catalogue now points at the new page. Readers arriving after the swing see the new edition; readers already mid-walk on the old page continue undisturbed, reading a consistent past -- the moral of Episode Two's fork window, you notice, reappearing at nanosecond scale: there, a forked child kept a copy-on-write photograph of its parent's memory, frozen at the instant of the fork, and worked from the photograph at leisure; here, a reader keeps the edition it began on; in both, *a consistent past is a resource*. One question remains, and it is the only hard one: when may the writer *reclaim* the old page? Answer: when every reader who could possibly still be walking it has left -- and here is the kernel's sleight of hand. RCU readers are forbidden to sleep while reading. Therefore, the moment every processor in the machine has passed through a context switch -- an event the scheduler observes for free -- every reader who began before the publication *must* have finished. Say that again slowly, for it is the hinge of the whole trick: a reader who may not sleep never leaves its processor mid-walk; so a processor that has switched tasks has, by that very act, sworn that no reader from before the switch is still walking on it; and once every processor has so sworn, the old page can have no readers left in all the world. That interval is the *grace period*: the writer publishes, waits for each processor to switch once, and then reclaims the old edition in perfect safety, having synchronised with ten thousand readers by watching for an event that was going to happen anyway. No reader ever paid a nanosecond. The library never closed; the old books were simply collected once the last patron holding one had gone home for the day.

RCU is used across the kernel in tens of thousands of places; the pilot's path-walking -- those directory lookups that almost never touch a lock -- is RCU; the network's routing decisions are RCU; and I linger on it not because sir will write any, but because it is the finest single specimen of the season's thesis in the concurrency chapter: an *argument* -- that readers are the common case and must ride free, and that the scheduler's own heartbeat can stand in for an army of bookkeeping -- built into machinery so deeply that its costs vanish into events already paid for. It is also, historians note, an idea with a patent history and an academic pedigree the kernel absorbed and industrialised -- the bazaar, as ever, less inventor than *metaboliser* of inventions.

## The Books of the Evening, and an Honest Fence

The accounts. A race is correct code failing by interleaving -- silent, rare, and living in the timing where no debugger looks. Atomicity is bought in two sizes: the instruction, compare-and-swap chief among them, and the critical section, fenced by locks that wait by rattling or by sleeping, priced by the pilot's context-switch arithmetic. The sorrows of ownership: deadlock, a cycle made impossible by ordering or met in production; contention, the true cost of locking, answered across a decade by breaking one giant lock into a fine-grained thousand; inversion, cured by inheritance, from the surface of Mars to the audio path of the telephone in your pocket; and real time matured from a patched speciality into the mainline's standing promise. Beneath it all the futex treaty -- quarrels only, adjudicated below stairs; the leap-second storm paid off at full depth -- and beyond it the escapes: sharing avoided, sharing unlocked, and RCU's library, where readers ride free and the scheduler's own pulse marks the closing of the books.

Now the fence, stated plainly as the constitution requires. One storey below tonight lies the *memory model*: the precise law of which writes become visible to which readers *when*, on processors that -- I shall say only this much -- do not always let one core see another's writes in the order they were written, and on compilers that reorder in the service of speed. Down there live the acquire and the release, the barrier and the fence, and the formal semantics that turn "it seems to work" into "it cannot fail". That storey is real, it is where the lock-free proofs live, and it is *deliberately unpaid tonight*: it is desk-course territory -- the kind that wants notation, worked derivations, and problems sir has expressly declined -- and no honest treatment of it fits in the ear alone. What I have given you instead is the load-bearing floor above it: every mechanism tonight -- mutex, futex, RCU -- is built *by* the three people who did the proofs precisely so that the ten thousand of us upstairs need only the contracts. Tonight you have the contracts, and -- rarer -- the shape of what they protect you from.

The season's debts stand: the microkernel question compounds toward Episode Eleven, where tonight's share-nothing escape route becomes an entire architecture; trusting-trust toward Episode Eight. And next week we open the largest room below stairs. The pilot called virtual memory the great and necessary lie -- every process promised a vast private memory of its own, the promise honoured page by page out of whatever physical store actually exists -- and showed you the lie's mechanics; next week, the *ledger* behind the lie -- how the kernel decides, second by second, which pages live in the precious fast and which are exiled to the slow; the sweep of the clock hands through every page you own; the day the phone in your pocket taught the server in your rack a better way to forget. And the hook is the oldest story in the season: Manchester, nineteen sixty-two, the machine that invented the lie itself -- one level of store, conjured from two, by men who had run out of memory and decided to pretend otherwise.

Until then, sir.

*-- End of Episode Three.*
