# Episode Two — The Life and Times of a Process

*The Well-Run House — A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux*

## The Day Microsoft Shipped Linux

Good day, sir.

In the spring of twenty sixteen, at Microsoft's annual developers' conference, a company that had spent two decades treating our house as a hostile power announced — to an audience whose collective expression I can only describe as that of a congregation watching the vicar produce a rabbit from the communion chalice — that the next update to Windows would run Linux programs. Natively. The genuine articles: the very binaries from an Ubuntu archive, unmodified, executing on the NT kernel, no virtual machine in sight.

The announcement made the newspapers as a cultural event — the cancer speech was still within living memory, after all. But the engineering underneath interests us more tonight, because of what it attempted, and because of the precise place where the attempt bent and finally broke.

The scheme was called the Windows Subsystem for Linux, and its first incarnation was a work of genuine audacity. NT, you will recall from last week, is a serious kernel with its own ideas; the subsystem's authors set out to teach it to *impersonate* ours. A special breed of process — the *pico process*, adapted from a Microsoft research project called Drawbridge — was created empty of all Windows conventions, and every time a program inside one rang the bell — made a *system call*, that is: pulled the one bell rope by which any program asks its kernel for any service — the call was caught and translated, live, into whatever NT machinery could be made to serve. Three hundred-odd services of our bell rope, re-enacted on foreign plumbing. Open a file: translate. Allocate memory: translate. And then the program calls *fork* — and here, sir, the translators earned their pay, because NT has no fork. It can *nearly* fork; buried in its depths, for reasons that are themselves a story tonight, there is machinery for duplicating a process; but the semantics our programs assume — the *copy-on-write* economics, under which a forked child *shares* its parent's memory pages until one side writes, and pays for a private copy of only the page it touched, only then; the precise inheritance of every resource; the contract that fork *returns twice*, once in the parent bearing the child's number and once in the child bearing zero — all of that had to be built, faithfully, on a kernel whose architects considered the entire idea a design error.

And the remarkable thing is that it *worked*. Mostly. Ubuntu ran. Your shell ran. And then the seams began to show, in exactly the places a listener of this course could now predict: the filesystem, where NT's notions of caching and locking and case made our programs' assumptions expensive to honour — a compile inside the subsystem could run several times slower than the same compile on the same hardware under real Linux, nearly all of it lost in translation at the boundary; and the long tail of behaviour — the thousand small contracts about signals and timers and memory maps that no specification fully writes down, which is to say the *semantics*, the lived customs of the house as opposed to its published etiquette.

So in twenty nineteen Microsoft did the thing that every engineer in the audience understood instantly and the marketing department must have loathed: it gave up — honourably, completely, and in the only direction that works. The second Windows Subsystem for Linux abandoned impersonation. It ships a genuine Linux kernel, our actual kernel, inside a lightweight virtual machine so tightly integrated that most users never notice the border, and the fastidious translation layer went to the attic. The lesson deserves framing, and it is tonight's thesis: *the interface of an operating system can be imitated; the process model cannot.* The bell rope's list of services is documentation; anyone may copy it. But fork — what it means, what it costs, what ten thousand programs silently assume about it — is not an interface. It is a constitution. Microsoft, with effectively unlimited engineers and total control of its own kernel, found it cheaper to ship ours than to counterfeit it.

So tonight we give the process the full evening it was promised. The pilot introduced it as a bundle of resources — memory, open files, credentials, all held together under one number — and showed you the two-step by which every program you have ever launched began: *fork*, which duplicates the calling process into a child, and *exec*, by which the child then discards its inherited body and takes possession of the program it means to run. That was the parlour tour. Tonight: what a process actually is in the kernel's own books, and the à-la-carte menu of which fork is merely one fixed dinner; the tree of processes and its curious rituals — including the ceremonial double fork that generations of daemons performed, and why they performed it, and what made it obsolete; the loader in full, which is to say the machinery that runs before your program that we glimpsed in the pilot, now with the lights on; the fork window revisited as *architecture* — including the reason every application on an Android telephone is the same process wearing a billion masks, and the reason a database can photograph its own memory for free; and at the last, back to Redmond for the autopsy, with everything we shall have learned arranged on the bench around it.

The resident, sir, examined at last. In we go.

## Part One — The Resident, in the Kernel's Own Books

Begin below stairs, with the ledger entry itself.

For every process — and, as the pilot let slip, for every thread, since the kernel keeps one kind of book for both — there is a structure in kernel memory that *is* that task, administratively speaking. It is the biography the staff keep on every resident. In the kernel's own sources it is declared as the *task struct* — one word, an underscore where the space should be, in the terse dialect of a trade that abbreviates whatever it handles most — and *task struct* it shall be called here, because that is its name, and you will meet it in every book on the kernel ever written. It is a fat object: state and identifiers; the scheduling accounts — the running record of processor time had and owed; a pointer to the memory map; a pointer to the file table; the credentials; the signal dispositions; the parentage and the roll of children; the namespaces it perceives the world through; the control group that meters it — the kernel's enclosure, you may recall, for rationing a whole family of processes at once. Nearly everything the pilot toured, you notice, is reachable from this one structure — which is the honest reason the process is the organising idea of the whole house. It is not that the kernel contains processes. It is that the process is the kernel's *unit of account*: the entity to which time is billed, memory is lent, files are charged, and blame is assigned.

Now let me pay a debt from the pilot, which said — accurately but hastily — that a thread is a task sharing more, a process a task sharing less, and the mechanism one call with different flags. Tonight, the menu itself.

The call is named *clone*, and it is fork's general form. Where fork offers the fixed dinner — duplicate everything, share nothing — clone presents the carte: a bundle of flags, each answering one question, *share or copy?*, for one of the resources on the biography's list. Share the memory map, or copy it? Share the file table, or copy it? The filesystem context — the current directory and its relatives? The signal handlers? Shall the new task join the caller's *thread group* — that is, shall it answer to the same process identifier as its creator, so the outside world sees one process with two hands? Set every share flag and enrol in the thread group: that is a thread, exactly as your threading library creates it. Clear them all: that is fork. And the intermediate settings are not curiosities — containers are built from this same menu, since among the flags are those that grant the new task fresh *namespaces*: private editions of some facet of the world — its own roll of process numbers, its own network, its own map of mounted filesystems — each a lens the task thereafter perceives the house through, believing its edition the only one. Share the world, or copy the world. One call, sir. Threads, processes, and containers: three sizes of the same decision, which is the kind of parsimony that makes an old engineer suspect the designers were showing off.

Two historical dishes on the menu deserve a word, because you will meet their ghosts. There is *vfork*, a relic of the days before copy-on-write reached these shores: it lends the child the parent's actual memory — not a copy, the very pages — and *suspends the parent* until the child execs or exits, the child meanwhile under strict instructions to touch nothing. It was a performance hack for machines that could not fork cheaply, it is a loaded weapon left in a drawer, and modern code reaches instead for a composed convenience called *spawn* — create-and-exec in one gesture, no window, no danger — which, in a pleasing turn, the standard library quietly implements *using* the borrowed-memory trick, correctly, on your behalf. The lesson generalises: the house keeps its dangerous old tools, because removing anything is forbidden — a contract the season will keep meeting — but it wraps them in safer furniture.

And one modern refinement, closing a hole the pilot noticed. Process identifiers are small numbers, recycled; the pilot warned of the ancient race — the process you meant to signal dies, its number is reissued, your signal strikes a stranger. The repair is the *pid file descriptor* — *pidfd* in the manuals and the sources, and that is the name to search: ask, when cloning or afterwards, for a file descriptor that refers to *this* process — the object itself rather than its number plate — stable against all recycling, and waitable, like any descriptor, with the same readiness machinery that watches sockets and pipes, so a supervisor may keep its processes and its connections in one waiting room. The newest process managers hold their charges by pid file descriptor, not by number, and an entire genus of race retires. Everything, given time, becomes a file.

As for the resident's *state* — the single letter you have read in process listings for twenty-five years — the alphabet is short and you have now met every letter in the wild: running or ready, which the listing does not trouble to distinguish; sleeping interruptibly, the common case, the resident dozing till the bell; sleeping *uninterruptibly* — the tradesman-wait, a resident gone below to wait on slow hardware, deaf even to the ninth signal until the errand returns — with a modern annex, the *idle* variant, invented so that kernel threads waiting harmlessly forever stop alarming the load average, which counts the uninterruptible among the demanding; stopped, held by the terminal's suspend or a debugger's grip; and the *zombie*: dead, finished, but still on the books — the ledger entry retained until the parent collects the child's exit status, *reaps* it, in the house's word, whereupon the entry is struck at last. The letters are the resident's whereabouts in the house at a glance, and I shall use them freely from here on.

And since we have the ledger open, let me correct a small fiction the pilot allowed you to keep: the belief that the tree has one root. Run your process listing with the right persuasion and you will find, beside the first process, a second elder — and beneath it a whole population of residents whose names appear in square brackets and whose command lines are empty. These are the *kernel threads*: tasks that live their entire lives below stairs, running kernel code only, with no program, no memory map of their own, no possibility of ever ringing the bell because they are already on its far side. The pilot met several without introduction — the writeback thread that carries dirty pages to disk, the per-processor threads that absorb deferred interrupt work, the reaper's colleagues of every stripe. They are scheduled by the same scheduler, appear in the same listing, and are billed in the same books as your programs, which is precisely the design: even the staff's own errands are run as residents, so that one accounting covers the house entire. Two trees, then: the family's, rooted in process one, and the staff's, rooted beside it — and the brackets around a name are the tradesman's entrance sign, telling you the resident you are looking at has no upstairs existence at all.

Process one itself carries privileges of office the pilot had no space for, and they explain a container mystery you have certainly met. The first process cannot be killed by signals it has not chosen to handle — the kernel simply declines to deliver a fatal default to it, on the sensible ground that process one is the house's adopter of last resort — every orphan in the tree lands in its lap, as we shall see in a moment — and the adopter of last resort must not itself die. Inside a process-number namespace the same courtesy applies to *that* namespace's process one: signals from its own children that it has no handler for are quietly not delivered. Which is why a container whose main program is an innocent shell script so often refuses to stop politely — the tooling sends the gentle termination signal, the script has no handler installed, the namespace's first-process privilege swallows the signal whole, and after the grace period the supervisor gives up and reaches for the sledgehammer, which is permitted from *outside* the namespace. The ten-second pause you have watched a thousand times between asking a container to stop and watching it die is not slowness. It is a privilege of office, exercised by a shell script that has no idea it holds it, timing out.

## Part Two — The Tree, and Its Rituals

Every resident has a parent; the parentage relation makes the tree; and at the root of the tree sits the first process — the *undertaker*, as the pilot styled him, for the two duties that define the office: he adopts every orphan whose parent dies before it, and he reaps the zombies nobody else will collect. We may now watch him at his full range of duties.

The pilot gave you sessions and process groups as terminal furniture; let me restate that furniture properly, because tonight's first rite stands on it. Processes gather into *process groups* — a pipeline of three commands is one group — and groups gather into a *session*, which is, to a first approximation, one login: one terminal, and everything you have launched from it. The terminal recognises one group at a time as its *foreground* group — the one your interrupt key strikes — and when the terminal itself disconnects, the kernel delivers the *hangup* signal, whose default disposition is death, to fell the session it has abandoned. A session, in other words, is a line of inheritance with a terminal at its head, and the terminal's fate travels down the line. Tonight, notice what that machinery implies for a program that wants to *escape* it. A service — a daemon, in the house's old word — is precisely a resident who must not die when a terminal does. And in the classical world, before the modern majordomo, a program achieved that emancipation by performing a ritual so odd that generations of engineers have copied it from one another like a superstition. I shall now perform the exorcism of explaining it, because every step is load-bearing, and you have surely seen the incantation in old code.

Three processes will figure in this story, so let me set the places before anything moves. There is the *founder* — the process your shell actually launched, the one that aspires to become a daemon. There will shortly be the founder's *child*. And there will, at the last, be the child's child — the *grandchild* — and the whole point of the rite, mark it now, is that only the grandchild survives. Two generations die on purpose so that the third can live free.

Step one: the founder forks a child, and then the founder exits. One move, two effects, both intended. The shell that launched the founder was waiting for the founder to finish; the founder has now finished; the shell is satisfied and returns your prompt. And the child, its parent dead, is now an orphan — so it is adopted by the first process, as every orphan in the house is. With that single fork-and-exit, the child has stepped outside the shell's books entirely: nobody upstairs is waiting on it any more, and its parent is process one.

Step two belongs to the child, alone now. It asks the kernel for a *new session*, and becomes that new session's leader. Recall what a session is: a line of inheritance with a terminal at its head. A freshly minted session has no terminal at its head — none at all — so this is the emancipation proper: the hangup that fells a disconnected session can no longer reach the child, because the child no longer stands in any line a terminal heads.

You might think the work complete. Here arrives the step that separates the initiates from the cargo cultists. Step three: the child forks once more — this produces the grandchild — and then the child exits, its work done. Why a second death, sir? Because of a rule the pilot had no room for: a session *leader* that so much as opens a terminal device can find that terminal becoming its session's controlling terminal — the very leash the rite exists to slip, quietly reattaching itself. The child is the new session's leader, so the leash could still find *it*. The grandchild is a member of the new session but not its leader, and can never become its leader; for the grandchild, the door to re-adoption by any terminal is closed forever. That is the entire meaning of the double fork, and it bears saying twice: the first fork escapes the shell; the second escapes the session *leadership* that the escape itself created.

Add the housekeeping — change to a safe directory, reset the file-creation mask, close the inherited descriptors, point the standard three at the bottomless sink (the *null device*, as the manuals have it: the file that discards all writes) — and you have the full rite: fork, exit, new session, fork, exit, sweep. Six gestures to be born free of every terminal on Earth.

Two things about the rite deserve your attention. First: every step is a *negative* — shed this inheritance, decline that adoption. The process model's inheritances are so pervasive that becoming independent is a labour of systematic renunciation; the daemon is the house's monk. And second: the rite is obsolete, and the pilot told you why without telling you it had. The modern service manager starts services *itself*, from a parent that never had a terminal, in a fresh session already, with descriptors already arranged — the entire six-step renunciation rendered moot by being born in the right place. The double fork survives in old code and old tutorials as ritual fossilised after its god had died — and, deliciously, the modern manager *prefers* services that do not daemonise, because a service that forks itself into the background is a service the manager must chase; you will find in many a unit file a setting whose entire meaning is "please, we beg you, just run in the foreground and let us do the supervising". Progress in our field, as often as not, consists of persuading programs to stop performing their ancestors' emergencies.

The rite explained, let me pay out the rest of the terminal's machinery, because Part Two of the pilot left two mysteries on the table that this equipment now solves in passing. You suspend a job with the terminal's suspend key and revive it with the foreground command; you launch another with the ampersand and it runs behind; so far, mere habit. Mystery one: why does a *background* job that tries to *read* from the terminal freeze solid — showing, if you look, as stopped — until you bring it forward? Because the terminal driver enforces a rule of the salon: only the foreground group may read the conversation. A background resident that reaches for the input is not refused with an error; it is *suspended* by signal, put politely to sleep at the very threshold, so that when you eventually foreground it, it resumes inside the read as though no time had passed and takes its turn. Two claimants to one keyboard would otherwise steal keystrokes from each other blindly; the driver prefers suspended animation to anarchy. Mystery two, the mirror case: why does a background job *writing* to the terminal sometimes splatter its output across your prompt unmolested, and other times freeze exactly as the reader did? Because for *output* the salon rule is optional — a terminal setting chooses whether background writers are tolerated (the default, hence the splatter you have cursed for decades) or suspended like the readers. One flag, sir, standing between you and a lifetime of interleaved prompts; the shell purists set it; the rest of humanity learned to press the redraw key. Both mysteries, notice, are the same lesson the daemon's rite taught: the terminal is not a passive pipe but a *governed room*, with a floor-holder, rules of address, and a bailiff — and half of classical job control is just the bailiff's arrest warrants, delivered as signals.

While we stand at the tree, three modern grafts.

The undertaker's monopoly has been broken, usefully: a process may now declare itself a *sub-reaper*, meaning that orphans among its descendants reparent to *it* rather than climbing all the way to the root. That is how per-user service managers and container supervisors keep their own books — each subtree with its own undertaker, the zombie-and-reaping logic of Part One operating identically one storey down.

The tree's numbers, you now know from the clone menu, are per-namespace: a containerised resident believes itself process one. Tonight's addendum is only that the belief is *layered* — the same task has one number in its own namespace and another in yours, both true, both visible, depending on where the observer stands. When you read a container's processes from the host and the numbers disagree with the container's own account, neither is lying; you are reading two registers of the same census.

And a confession of the books' own limits, which the pilot promised the observability episode would need: the tree records *parentage*, not *history*. When a process execs, the biography keeps its number and its parent but replaces its name and body — the possession, as the pilot styled that replacement — so the tree as read tonight tells you who spawned whom, but not what anything *used to be*. The auditing machinery that remembers — that can testify, afterwards, that this shell became that compiler became that linker — is a different instrument, and Episode Twelve will play it.

## Part Three — The Loader, With the Lights On

Now the exec side, properly. The pilot showed you the shape, which I restate: the kernel maps the program into memory, maps the *dynamic linker* beside it — the helper program whose trade is stitching a program to the libraries it depends on — and jumps to the *linker* first, your program only afterwards. Depth was promised later. It is later.

An executable, the pilot said, is an ELF — the *executable and linkable format*, the house's standard container for programs: header, then sections. The load-bearing part is a small table of *program headers*, each a single imperative sentence addressed to the kernel: map this span of the file at this address, readable and executable; map that span readable and writable; reserve so much zeroed space beyond it. The kernel, obeying, is doing nothing exotic — these are ordinary *file mappings*. The kernel keeps one communal store in memory — the *page cache* — holding the recently used pages of every file in the house, and a mapped file is simply that store lent into a process's address space: nothing copied up front, each page faulted in on demand the first time the program touches it. A program *starting* and a program *being read* are, below stairs, the same activity, which is why the second launch of anything is so brisk: the pages never left.

One program header is different in kind: the one that says, in effect, *I am not complete; fetch my interpreter*. There stands the path of the dynamic linker, and the kernel — precisely as the pilot described — maps that beside the program and starts execution there. But I can now tell you *what it is doing* in that industrious overture, and it is worth the candle, because half the folklore of deployment lives in this machinery.

The linker's task is a treasure hunt with a map of debts. The program's file carries tables: the names of every function and object it uses but does not contain — its *undefined symbols*, the debts — and the names of the libraries expected to supply them. Each library, mapped in turn, brings debts of its own; the linker walks the whole graph, breadth-first, assembling a search order. Then the reckoning: for each debt, walk the order, find the first definition bearing that name, and write its address into the debtor's tables. That first-definition rule is the entire mechanism of the *preload* trick the pilot performed: an environment variable names a library of your choosing, the linker places that library early in the order, and every debt it can answer, it answers first — which is to say, any library function you care to intercept, intercepted. No hook, no registry of interceptions: just an orderly queue and a rule about who gets asked.

The writing-of-addresses is called *relocation*, and where it lands matters. Code pages, the pilot established, are shared — four hundred residents, one physical copy of the standard library. Shared pages must not be scribbled on per-process; so the compiler arranges for all cross-library calls to go *indirectly*, through a small private table each process owns. Its name in the trade is the *global offset table* — a dry name, but say it three times and it is yours, for it is the term every linker manual and every exploit write-up will use — and what it holds is exactly what it sounds like: the program's private book of where everything finally landed. The linker fills the global offset table; the code pages stay pristine and communal. And *lazy resolution* — the arrangement whereby a function's first call lands on a little stub that looks the real address up and writes it down, so every later call flies direct — is precisely an entry in the global offset table being filled on demand; the stubs themselves live in a companion structure the manuals call the *procedure linkage table*, the offset table's antechamber. Likewise the hardened build the pilot mentioned: resolve every entry up front, then seal the global offset table read-only — the same book, bound differently. What the pilot called a table with an attractive target painted on it, you now know by its government name, and as the place where late binding and security bargain over the same pages.

Notice, too, what all this costs, because the bill explains a modern fashion. The treasure hunt is proportional to libraries times symbols; a large desktop application can spend a perceptible fraction of its start-up in pure name-resolution. And the graph must *exist* at run time: the right libraries, in the right versions, at the right paths — the whole genus of misery your profession knows as dependency hell, and the reason the container image — the stacked read-only layers of the pilot, a whole filesystem shipped along with the program so that nothing is left to chance on arrival — is at bottom *a program's private copy of the world to link against*. Against all of which stands the countervailing church: the statically linked binary, all debts settled at build time, one self-sufficient file, no linker, no hunt, no hell — the Go language made this its sacrament and half the cloud's tooling followed — at the price the pilot taught you to compute: nothing shared, every copy of the library resident in every copy of every program, and a security patch to the library meaning every binary in the fleet rebuilt rather than one file replaced. Sharing against certainty; the house has never settled the argument, because it is not settleable; it is a dial, and now you know both ends of it.

Before the linker takes a single step, incidentally, it reads a letter the kernel has left for it, and the letter's whereabouts is one of the house's better secrets. When exec builds the new process, it must hand over the essentials: the argument list you typed, the environment you bequeathed, and a private annexe of facts the linker needs — where the program's headers were mapped, where the entry point is, the page size, whether to ignore the preload variable because the credentials changed on the way in — the *setuid* case, a program that runs with more authority than the user who launched it, where an attacker-chosen preload would otherwise walk straight through the seam — the address of the hall-clock page, the shared page on which the kernel publishes the time for reading without a system call, so the program need never search for it, and — a detail to pocket for Episode Eight — a few bytes of fresh randomness, delivered gratis so that every program can seed its own defences without asking. And where does this correspondence physically live? *On the new process's stack.* The kernel writes the arguments, the environment, and the annexe onto the top of the stack like documents left on a hall table, sets the stack pointer beneath them, and jumps; the program's first act of life is to look up and find its world already in writing. When you change a process's environment, you are not updating a registry anywhere — you are choosing what gets written on a dead man's table for his successor to read, which is why the environment is inherited at exec and never, ever, alterable from outside afterwards: the table was written once, at the possession, and the kernel has no forwarding address.

While we are correcting folklore with the lights on: the versioned-symbol affair, which has ruined at least one of sir's afternoons, I would wager, in the form of the error that says a library version is *not found*. The treasure hunt as I described it matches names; but the standard library learned long ago that names alone are a trap — it must be free to change what a function *means* between decades without breaking a single old binary. So each symbol in the standard library carries a small version tag, and each program records not merely "I need this function" but "I need this function *as it meant in the era I was linked*". Old binaries request old meanings and receive them forever; that is why a program built twenty years ago still runs. But the bargain has a sharp edge pointing the other way: a binary built on a *new* system records the new era's tags, and carried to an older system it demands meanings that have not been invented there yet — whereupon the linker, quite correctly, refuses. The error message that looks like gibberish is the loader saying, precisely: *this program was linked against the future.* Forward compatibility was never promised; only backward; and the practical rule every release engineer eventually derives — build on the oldest system you intend to support, not the newest you happen to own — is just that asymmetry, learned the expensive way. The container image, once again, dissolves the problem by shipping the era along with the program.

One last door in the loader, easily missed and full of guests. The kernel decides *what kind* of file it has been asked to exec by reading the first bytes — the interpreter line for scripts, the opening line that begins hash-bang and names the program that should actually run the text, was one case of a general registry. That registry is extensible at run time: tell the kernel *files beginning with these bytes are to be handed to that interpreter*, and foreign formats become native citizens. Register the emulator for another processor's binaries and programs for a machine you do not own run transparently — the container tooling does exactly this to build images for foreign architectures, which is very likely how the images on sir's own build servers for his telephone-adjacent projects are made. Register a Windows-executable handler and Wine catches the guests at the door. Apple's Rosetta — the translator that carried the Macintosh across its processor migration so smoothly that civilians never learned it happened — is the same doorman's trick performed with the full weight of the house behind it. The exec call, in other words, is not a loader; it is a *dispatch*, and the set of things the house can be taught to run is a configuration file, not a constitution.

## Part Four — The Window, Made Load-Bearing

The pilot's finest hour, by sir's own report, was the *fork window*, and it earns its restatement in full. Between the fork and the exec, the child is alive, running the parent's code, and entirely its own master; whatever it rearranges in that interval — files opened, descriptors seated in the standard places, privileges dropped — it rearranges for itself alone, and the program that exec then installs inherits the arranged world without ever knowing it was arranged. That is the trick beneath every redirect you have ever typed: the shell forks, the *child* opens the file and seats it as its own standard output, and only then execs your command, which writes to what it innocently believes is the terminal. Tonight I want to promote that trick from anecdote to architecture, with two exhibits: one from a database you administer, and one from the telephone in your pocket.

The database first, because it is the purer specimen. Redis — the in-memory store — must periodically write its entire dataset to disk without stopping work. The naive designs are all bad: pause and dump (an outage), copy the data aside first (double the memory), interleave carefully (a bug farm). What Redis actually does is a thing of insolent economy: *it forks.* The child inherits, at zero copying cost, a copy-on-write photograph of the entire dataset frozen at the instant of the fork — the lazy duplication from the top of the evening doing all the work, pages shared until written — and the child then strolls through its frozen inheritance writing it to disk at leisure, while the parent serves traffic and, page by page as it writes, quietly pays for private copies of only what changes. When the child finishes, it exits; the photograph is returned; the accounting balances. Fork here is not a way of making a process. It is being used as a *memory-snapshot instrument* — the only one the house provides that is instantaneous, kernel-enforced, and free in proportion to how little the parent writes during the exposure. The cost model has teeth — a write-heavy interval during the dump forces copies of every touched page, and an undersized memory budget meets the kernel's *out-of-memory killer* — the bailiff who, when memory runs dry, selects a resident to shoot — with the usual injustice — but the design is the fork window generalised: *between the fork and the child's purpose lies an interval in which the child owns a consistent past, and that is a resource.*

Now the telephone, where the window built a civilisation. Android's problem, at birth: applications are Java-family programs wanting a heavyweight runtime — class libraries, a virtual machine, warmed caches — and a telephone must launch them *instantly*, on hardware then modest, with dozens resident at once. Loading the runtime per launch: seconds, unacceptable. Sharing one runtime among all applications: one crash fells the town, and no walls between tenants — for the only walls the hardware itself enforces are the separate address spaces of separate processes, and they come one set *per process*.

The answer is a resident called the *zygote*, and the name is the design document. At boot, Android starts one process that loads and initialises *everything every application will ever share* — the class libraries, the runtime, the fonts, the common resources — thousands of files, seconds of work, performed once. Then it opens a socket and waits. When you tap an icon, the system does not start a program. It asks the zygote, and the zygote *forks*, and the child — inheriting, copy-on-write and instantaneous, the entire warmed world — becomes, in the fork window, the application: drops what must be dropped, assumes the application's identity and sandbox, and proceeds. Launch cost: a fork plus the application's own code. Memory cost: all those preloaded pages *shared physically* among every application on the device — one physical copy mapped into all, precisely as the page cache lends one copy of a library to four hundred residents; the runtime is resident once, however many masks it wears. Fork's twin gifts — cheap duplication of a prepared past, and total post-fork isolation — are the load-bearing structure of the most widely deployed application platform in history. When Episode One promised you a billion masks on one face, this was the machinery; and when NT's architects ruled fork a design error, this — to be fair to all parties — is the invoice of what that ruling forecloses.

And a third exhibit, sitting on sir's own desk, completes the set: the web browser. Chrome's founding architectural bet — the one that separated it from every browser before it — was to spend processes like water: one for the browser proper, one per site, one per extension, sandboxed renderers by the dozen, on the theory you can now state precisely: the process boundary is the only wall the hardware itself enforces, so put one between the internet's untrusted pages and everything you care about, and between the pages themselves. A tab crashes; the town survives. A malicious page escapes its renderer; it finds itself in a process so pre-stripped of capabilities and descriptors — the fork window used as a disarmament chamber — that there is nothing in reach worth stealing. The bill for the wall-per-tab policy is paid in memory, as any process listing on a browsing machine will testify; and on Linux, to pay it down, Chrome runs — I promise I am not inventing this for symmetry — *a zygote*: a warmed template process, forked per renderer, for exactly Android's reasons and by exactly Android's mechanism. The three exhibits triangulate the moral: the fork window is the house's general instrument for manufacturing processes that begin life *already correct* — already snapshotted, already warmed, already disarmed — and every serious platform built on this kernel has independently rediscovered it.

Fairness now compels the ledger's other column, because fork has a dark side and it is not small. The pilot's fork was innocent because its processes were single-threaded. Fork a *multithreaded* process, and the fine print bites: the child receives a copy of the *calling thread only*. The other threads are not carried across — they simply do not exist in the child — but their *effects* do, frozen mid-gesture. A lock held by a thread that has no successor in the child is a lock nobody will ever release; memory an absent thread was midway through reshaping stays half-reshaped forever. The child inherits a world stopped between heartbeats, and the standard's honest counsel is brutal: after forking a threaded process, the child may safely do almost nothing except exec — cross the window quickly and be reborn, touching nothing on the way. The window, in a threaded world, is not a workshop but a tightrope. That, more than any single grievance, is the engineering case the fork-sceptics press — the model was conceived when a process was one thread of control and its every assumption whispers it still — and the modern spawn-style interfaces are the concession: where the window isn't needed, don't open it. The zygote, you will be amused to know, threads this needle by decree: it keeps itself scrupulously nearly-single-threaded precisely so that its ten thousand daily forks stay off the tightrope. The most successful fork in the world survives by refusing to be the kind of process fork cannot handle.

## Part Five — The Autopsy at Redmond, and the Verdict

Now we can reopen the WSL file with instruments, and the findings organise themselves.

NT's process model, on its own terms, is coherent and defensible: a process is created *empty*, from an executable, attributes supplied up front in one richly parameterised act — no duplication, no window, no two-step. Where our house says "copy the world, then adjust it from inside", NT says "describe the world, then receive it ready-made". For threaded programs it is arguably the sounder constitution — there is no tightrope because there is no window. And NT's kernel, as I hinted, *can* duplicate a process — machinery kept, historically, precisely for visiting Unix dignitaries: NT shipped for years with a POSIX subsystem, later expanded through an acquired product into a full Unix environment, all of it leaning on that buried near-fork. The first Windows Subsystem for Linux was thus not a novelty but a third attempt at an old ambition — the most serious ever mounted, with live translation of our entire bell rope.

And the failure teaches with precision, because *what* failed is a map of where an operating system's truth actually lives. The calls were translated; the calls were never the problem. What could not be economically reproduced was the *cost model and the customs*: fork at our speed and our copy-on-write economics, on a kernel whose every subsystem assumes processes are built empty; our filesystem semantics — the cheap *stat* call that reads off a file's particulars, the directory as a plain list of names pointing at files, the file that, deleted while open, lives on for whoever still holds it — re-enacted over NT's object machinery at NT's prices, which is where the compiles went to die; the thousand unwritten contracts of signal timing and map behaviour that no test suite enumerates because nobody ever wrote them down; they are case law, not statute. An operating system, it turns out, is case law *all the way through* — and the only complete edition of the case law is the kernel itself. Hence the capitulation, which I insist is the most flattering document ever published about our house: the second subsystem ships the case law verbatim, one genuine kernel in a trim virtual machine, and reverses the direction of translation — it is now *Windows* files served *into* Linux over a venerable network-filesystem protocol borrowed, in a pleasing genuflection, from a research system built by the same Bell Labs lineage that started Episode One. The impedance was not eliminated; it was *relocated*, pushed to the border where it bothers fewer things — cross-boundary file access remains the one place users still feel a seam, and the standing advice to keep one's Linux files on the Linux side is that relocated seam speaking.

The regime since has behaved like any sensible occupying administration and gone native by degrees. The subsystem gained the ability to launch Windows programs from the Linux shell and pipe the two worlds together — your muscle-memory pipelines with a Windows clipboard tool at one end, which is ordinary descriptor-plumbing — one program's output seated as the next one's input — working across a kernel boundary, and I shall let you sit with how strange that is. It gained graphical support by bundling — and here I ask you to file the fact carefully for the drawing-room episodes — a *Wayland compositor*, so that Linux desktop applications open their windows on the NT desktop through the very protocol whose biography is Episode Ten. And the kernel it ships is built from the ordinary public sources, patches published, updated through the ordinary Windows update channel — which yields the sentence I promised Episode One and deliver now with ceremony: *Microsoft builds, ships, patches, and supports a Linux kernel, delivered to hundreds of millions of machines by Windows Update.* The student's hobby, distributed as a component of Windows. The pilot's maxim held that the contract is simple and the implementation clever; Redmond's discovery is the corollary: you can reimplement cleverness, but you cannot reimplement *history* — and when history cannot be reimplemented, the remaining move is to *ship* it, which is as close to a formal surrender instrument as our industry issues.

## The Books of the Evening

The accounts, then. The process is the kernel's unit of account — one task struct, one biography, per task — and thread against process is a matter of flags on one call — share or copy, asked separately of every resource, with containers merely the same question asked about the world. The tree's rituals are inheritances managed: the daemon's double-fork a rite of sixfold renunciation, fossilised now that services are born free; the sub-reaper and the pid file descriptor closing the old holes of adoption and mistaken identity. The loader is a treasure hunt over a debt graph — first definition wins, each process's global offset table private, code pages communal — and exec is not a loader but a dispatch, teachable to run any guest. The fork window, which the pilot showed you as a shell trick, is structural steel: a database photographs its memory with it; a telephone platform amortises its entire runtime through it; and its price is the tightrope every threaded process walks in it. And the strongest kernel Microsoft could build, given every incentive to counterfeit these semantics, shipped the original instead — because the interface is statute, but the behaviour is case law, and the case law cannot be ported.

One small debt within the season is hereby paid: Episode One promised you the billion masks, and you have seen the face beneath them. The standing debts — the microkernel question, the trusting-trust lecture — remain on the books at interest, payable in Episodes Eleven and Eight respectively.

Next week the residents stop queueing politely and start sharing the rooms — many hands in one memory, the locks on the doors, and what happens when the locking goes wrong. Our opening exhibit is the finest concurrency bug in the history of engineering, and I choose the superlative with care: it occurred on another planet, was diagnosed from this one, and was repaired by editing a variable a hundred million miles away. A spacecraft, sir, brought low by precisely the machinery this episode has been praising — an inheritance, a priority, and a lock.

Until then, sir.
