# The Well-Run House

*A Working Tour of the Innards of Linux — The Pilot*

## The Night an Extra Second Arrived

Good day, sir.

On the thirtieth of June, in the year two thousand and twelve, at the stroke of midnight in Greenwich, the world's timekeepers inserted an extra second into the day. It is a routine act of housekeeping. The Earth's rotation is not quite the metronome our atomic clocks are, and every so often the two must be reconciled by holding the clock still for a single tick. The thing is announced months ahead. It is, on the face of it, the least dramatic event in the calendar.

Within minutes, a large and expensive portion of the internet fell over.

Reddit went dark. LinkedIn faltered. Mozilla's services buckled. Amadeus Altéa — the reservation system standing behind a good many airlines — so far lost its composure that passengers were checked in by hand at airport counters, which is the twenty-first century's way of announcing that something has gone catastrophically wrong several storeys below the desk. Across the world, administrators logged into their servers and found every processor pinned at full load, accomplishing precisely nothing, with tremendous enthusiasm.

The cause, when it was run to ground, was almost insultingly small. Informed that a leap second is coming, the kernel arms an internal alarm to go off at the appointed moment and quietly amend its books. The alarm duly went off. The books were duly amended. But nobody thought to inform one particular subsystem — the one that keeps the high-resolution alarms — that the clock beneath it had been altered. So every program which had said "wake me in ten milliseconds" now held an appointment that was, by the new reckoning, already in the past. It fired at once. The program looked about, found nothing whatever to do, and set the alarm afresh. Which was also already in the past. And so onward, at the speed of a modern processor, for as long as anyone cared to watch.

The remedy, once understood, had a certain perfection about it. Nothing needed patching. Nothing needed rebooting. One ran a single command setting the system clock to the value it already held — the date, set to the date — because the *act* of setting the clock, however redundant its content, informed the alarm subsystem that the clock had been set, whereupon it recomputed every appointment it held and the fever broke on the instant. Half the internet was restored by telling the computer something it already knew.

I open with this, sir, for three reasons.

The first is that it is very funny, and we have two hours to fill.

The second is that it is an unusually clean illustration of what a kernel *is*. Not a presiding mystery. Not a black box tended by initiates. It is a program — an enormous and scrupulously written program, but a program — composed of subsystems that must remember to tell one another things, and which now and then forget, with consequences visible from orbit.

The third is the one that matters. Every element of that story is something you have used, professionally, for twenty-five years. Alarms. Sleeping. Waking. The clock. The scheduler that pinned the processors. The lock that could not be taken. You have been standing on this machinery your entire working life, and you know it as one knows a house one has lived in for decades — able to find the light switches in the dark, aware which stair creaks — without ever having seen the plans, or the wiring, or the frankly alarming thing the previous owner did to the plumbing.

That is the office of these two hours. Not to make a kernel developer of you; that is a longer and quite different undertaking, and should it tempt you we shall commission it properly. This is the tour of the house. What lies below stairs, who works there, what they do all day, and — the part I care most about — *why the things you already do work as they do*. There is a particular species of pleasure available to a man who has performed a ritual correctly for years and is at last shown the mechanism it operates. I intend to supply it repeatedly and without apology.

A word on how I shall proceed, and one admission.

The house will serve as my running image, partly because it is apt and partly because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving it alone. Above stairs live the programs — your shell, your editor, your browser, your database. They are the family and their guests. They have the run of the place and they are not permitted within thirty feet of the boiler. Below stairs is the kernel: the staff. The staff hold keys to everything. Theirs are the doors, the post, the accounts, the fires, and the tradesmen's entrance. And there exists exactly one way for the family to obtain anything requiring the staff, which is to ring and ask. One does not simply walk into the kitchen. The architecture of the house forbids it — physically, in the silicon — and a great deal of what follows is the consequence of that single arrangement.

The admission is this. Everything I tell you today is true and simplified. Not "true if one squints", a formula for which I have no patience and neither have you, but true with detail withheld. Where I omit something a careful man might trip over, I shall say so. And where the kernel appears to contradict what I have just told you, the explanation is nearly always that the kernel is doing something ingenious for speed, and that the simple account is the *contract* while the ingenuity is the *implementation*. That distinction will present itself perhaps thirty times before we are done, so let us give it its name now.

Our route, then.

We begin at the boundary — the two worlds, the ring of privilege, and the bell one rings to pass between them. Then we start the machine from cold iron and follow it up to a prompt, a journey with more stages in it than anyone expects. Thereafter, in order: what a process actually is and what passes when one begets another; the magnificent lie the kernel tells every program about memory; how the hours are divided among the impatient; what is really meant by the slogan that everything is a file, which is wrong in an interesting way rather than a dull one; how a packet makes its way from a wire into your program; and finally the walls the kernel can raise within itself, which is where containers come from and why they are not the thing most people take them for.

And at the last we shall type a single command at a shell, press the return key, and trace what follows across every subject we have touched, from the keystroke to the letters on the glass. If I have done my work properly, that final passage will feel less like a summary than a curtain call.

Two hours, sir. Below stairs we go.

## Part One — The Two Worlds and the Bell

Let us begin with the most consequential fact about your computer, which is that the processor inside it is, at any given instant, in one of two humours.

In the first, it will do whatever is asked of it. It will read any byte of physical memory. It will address the disk controller directly. It will rewrite the tables that determine what memory *means*. It will disable interrupts, halt the machine, or reprogram the clock at will. This is supervisor mode; on the processors you actually own, ring zero.

In the second humour it is kept on a short lead. Certain instructions simply decline to execute; attempt one and the processor arrests you where you stand. Vast tracts of memory are not merely forbidden but *invisible* — not "permission denied" but "there is nothing at that address". Hardware is beyond reach. And you cannot restore yourself to the first humour, which is the crux of the matter, so let me put it without ornament: there exists no instruction meaning "make me privileged". This is user mode; ring three.

Your programs — all of them, every last one, the browser and the database and the shell and the editor and the compiler — run in the second humour. The kernel runs in the first. That is the whole of the distinction between above stairs and below. It is enforced by the hardware rather than by convention or good manners, and it is the footing on which every other protection in the system stands. Should it fail — and we shall see before the part is out that it has failed — nothing built above it can be trusted for a moment.

Now. If a program may not touch hardware, and may not enter the privileged humour, how does anything useful ever come about? Your editor plainly does write to disk. Your browser plainly does address the network. By what means?

By ringing the bell.

There exists a single instruction — on the processors beneath your desk it is called, with admirable literalness, *syscall* — whose entire purpose is to enter the kernel in a controlled fashion. And *controlled* is bearing the full weight of that sentence. The instruction does not permit the program to choose where in the kernel it lands. The landing site was fixed by the kernel at boot and written into a register that user code cannot so much as scratch. So the instruction means: assume the privileged humour, and in the same motion arrive at *the one address the kernel has nominated*. A program cannot say "make me privileged and carry on from here". It may only say "make me privileged and present myself at the front desk", where a member of staff is waiting, and where the first order of business is to examine what has been asked for and determine whether it is permitted.

I should like to dwell on this, since it is to my mind the most elegant single idea in the system and it is almost never laid out. The boundary between your program and the kernel is not a wall with a door in it. It is a wall with a *bell rope*. You cannot open the door. You may pull the rope and state your business, and the staff will decide.

What one states, on ringing, is a number naming the service wanted, together with as many as six arguments in registers. Open this file. Read that many bytes into this buffer. Map me this much memory. Send this on that socket. There are something over three hundred such services on a modern system, and the whole of Linux, as far as any program is concerned, *is that list*. Everything else — every library, every language runtime, every framework you have ever imported — is a more comfortable way of arriving eventually at one of those calls. The C library is not the kernel. It is a well-appointed waiting room with an exceptionally good butler in it.

Here is the first article of folklore I should like to retire, since it accounts for a whole category of debugging. When you run a program under a tracer and watch its system calls scroll past, you are not reading an approximate log. You are reading the *complete* record of every dealing that program has with the world outside itself. A program making no system calls is doing nothing you can observe from without: it is computing, or spinning, or waiting. Which is why the tracer is so devastating an instrument. It is not a debugger's convenience. It is a wiretap on the only telephone line out of the building.

The mechanism of the wiretap deserves a sentence, being another of those "so *that* is why" affairs. The tracer works by asking the kernel to halt the target and notify it at every crossing of the boundary. The kernel obliges — this is a service like any other — which accounts for the remarkable sloth of a traced program. Each call now entails stopping the process, waking the tracer, permitting it to inspect and resume, and only then proceeding. You are not observing the system so much as conscripting the staff into announcing every errand twice over.

Crossing is not free even when nobody is watching. The processor must preserve the state of the interrupted program and change stacks — the kernel places no trust in a program's stack and keeps its own — and on modern hardware rather more besides. In two thousand and eighteen, when the Meltdown and Spectre vulnerabilities were disclosed, we learned that processors had for years been speculating their way around the privilege boundary, and that a patient program could read kernel memory by timing how long various failures took to fail. Linux's mitigation was to stop keeping the kernel's memory mapped alongside the program's at all, and to exchange the entire map at every crossing. It works. It is also expensive, and the expense falls precisely at the crossing. Systems making a great many small calls — network servers, chiefly — grew measurably slower one Tuesday and never entirely recovered. If you have ever looked at a benchmark from before that year and found it unaccountably flattering, there is your answer. The house was obliged to fit locks to a door everyone had taken for solid wall.

Which prompts the obvious question. If crossings are dear, can they be avoided?

Now and then, delightfully, they can. Consider the asking of the time. It is among the most frequently requested services in the system — every log line, every timer, every profiler wants it — and it requires no privilege whatever. The answer is merely a number the kernel holds, and reading a number injures nobody. So the kernel resorts to a stratagem. It maps a small page of its own code and data into *every* process, readable but not writable, holding the current time and a modest routine for computing it. Your program calls that routine, takes the value, and returns, without ever having rung the bell. The staff have simply hung a clock in the hallway, that the family need not ask.

The page bears the name virtual dynamic shared object, which is a wretched name for a graceful idea, and it is why timing calls on Linux run some forty times faster than they have any right to. It is also why, when you trace a program and watch it print timestamps with no visible call to fetch them, you are not losing your reason. You are watching a man read the hall clock.

The last matter at the boundary is what happens when it is crossed the *other* way, and the kernel must interrupt you.

Two things can do it. The first is hardware: a network card with a packet, a disk with a finished read, a timer chip with an opinion about the hour. These raise an interrupt line; the processor abandons what it was doing, even mid-instruction-stream, assumes the privileged humour, and runs a handler. Your program is not consulted and does not notice, save that time has passed strangely.

The second is a fault: your program has attempted the impossible. Divided by zero. Executed rubbish. Touched an address that is not there. The processor arrests the instruction and hands the matter to the kernel, which must now determine what it signifies. Sometimes it signifies that the program has erred, and the kernel puts it down with a signal you know well under its courteous name, *segmentation fault*. But sometimes — and this is the most satisfying inversion in the whole system — the fault is entirely expected, and is in fact the very means by which a central feature is delivered. We shall come to it when we reach memory, and I promise it repays the wait.

Before we quit the boundary, I owe you the failure I mentioned. In two thousand and sixteen a flaw was disclosed under the name Dirty COW: a race in the kernel's handling of a certain kind of memory copy, by which any unprivileged user might write to files he could only read, and thence become root. What makes it a *story* rather than a bulletin is its provenance. Linus Torvalds had found the race and attempted to close it eleven years earlier. His fix disagreed with IBM mainframes and was reverted, and the race was left standing as a theoretical curiosity — because on the hardware of that day, striking the window was not a practical proposition. Then the years went by. Machines acquired more cores. The memory subsystem grew more parallel. And the theoretical window widened, quietly, without a hand laid on the code, until it was wide enough to drive a privilege escalation through.

I find that a genuinely instructive piece of history. The bug did not change. The *world* changed around the bug. Nobody had lied and nobody had been idle; a judgement made honestly against one machine merely expired against another. I commend it to you the next time you feel moved to be scornful about somebody's old code.

So: two worlds, one bell, a clock in the hallway, and a wall that must be maintained rather than merely built. Such is the shape of the boundary.

Now let us return to the very beginning and watch the whole house wake.

## Part Two — From Cold Iron to a Prompt

There is a particular species of moment, familiar to anyone who has kept machines, in which one presses the power button on a server and then waits, in silence, for an interval that feels geological, wondering what on earth the thing is *doing*. I should like to answer that, because the answer is a chain of small handovers, each link solving the problem its predecessor could not, and it is among the most satisfying stories in computing.

The machine wakes in a state so primitive as to border on the comic. On the processors beneath your desk, for reasons of compatibility so deep-rooted they reach back to the nineteen-seventies, the first instruction is fetched in a mode a machine of that era would recognise at once: sixteen bits wide, no memory protection, an address space of about a megabyte. Modern firmware escapes within microseconds — but it begins there, every time, like a formal bow to a monarch some decades dead.

What runs first is the *firmware*, itself a small operating system, resident in a flash chip on the motherboard. We used to call it the BIOS; today it is almost certainly UEFI, which is larger, more capable, and — I shall be candid — the sort of thing that results when a committee is invited to replace something that worked. Its task is to bring the machine from *electrically alive* to *capable of loading software*: test and configure the memory controller, bring up the buses, enumerate what hangs off them, and then find something to boot.

Under UEFI, finding something to boot means seeking a small partition, formatted in a filesystem chosen some time in the nineteen-eighties, holding programs the firmware knows how to execute. Where Secure Boot is enforced, the firmware also verifies a cryptographic signature before running anything, and the chain of trust that follows — firmware vouching for a small shim, shim for a bootloader, bootloader for a kernel — is the reason that installing a third-party driver on a locked-down machine makes for such a joyless afternoon.

The program the firmware runs is the *bootloader*; on most systems, GRUB. Its office is humbler than reputation suggests. It offers a menu, reads a configuration file, and loads two things into memory: the kernel, and a bundle called the initial ramdisk. Then it surrenders control and ceases to exist. The bootloader is not a stratum of the running system. It is a man who opens a door, gestures you through, and is never seen again.

The kernel takes over, and its first act is to unpack itself. The file on disk is compressed, with a small decompressor stapled to the front — hence its traditional name. The stub inflates the real kernel into memory, moves the processor into its proper modern mode with the full address space at its disposal, constructs the first page tables so that memory may be addressed as the rest of the kernel expects, and jumps to the main entry point.

There follows a long, dull, wholly indispensable sequence of initialisations: the memory allocators, the interrupt tables, the timekeeping, the scheduler, the filesystem layer, the driver model. Every subsystem we shall discuss this evening is stood up here, in a carefully chosen order, by a function whose name — *start kernel* — is the least dramatic ever bestowed on the most consequential procedure in the system. Throughout, the machine is still running on a single processor. The rest sit halted, waiting, and are roused one at a time and brought into the fold. On a large server that alone is a visible fraction of the boot.

And now the kernel meets a genuinely awkward difficulty, whose resolution is among my favourite pieces of engineering pragmatism anywhere in the system.

The kernel must mount the root filesystem — your disk — in order to have anything at all to run. But the disk might hang off any of two hundred varieties of controller. It might be an array assembled out of six drives. It might be encrypted and want a passphrase. It might live at the far end of a network. The code for all of that is enormous, and compiling every driver in the world into every kernel would be an absurdity. So the drivers live as modules, as separate files, on the root filesystem.

Which the kernel cannot mount, for want of the driver, which is on the filesystem, which it cannot mount.

The escape is the initial ramdisk, the second thing the bootloader loaded. It is a small, complete, self-sufficient filesystem held entirely in memory, containing precisely the drivers and tools this particular machine requires to reach its real root. The kernel mounts *that*, since it demands no drivers whatever — it is already in memory — and runs a small program within it. That program loads the storage driver, assembles the array, asks you for the passphrase, activates the logical volumes, and mounts the real root at last. Whereupon it performs a manoeuvre of exquisite cheek: it *replaces the root of the world*, swinging the entire filesystem tree across to the real disk, discards the temporary one it had been standing on, and hands control to the real system's first program.

So *that*, sir, is why the initial ramdisk must be regenerated after certain upgrades, and why neglecting to do so yields a machine that boots to a black screen and an unhelpful remark about being unable to find its root device. You have altered which drivers the kernel possesses; the little emergency kit in memory still holds yesterday's. Every administrator learns this exactly once, generally at a distance, invariably at an unsociable hour.

While we are here, a second small mystery dispatched. Your bootloader configuration almost certainly does not say that the root filesystem is the first partition of the first disk. It names instead a long hexadecimal identifier, which offends people's sense of tidiness and is entirely correct. The *names* of disks on Linux are assigned in whatever order the drivers happen to find them, and that order carries no guarantee of repeating. Add a card, change a cable, enjoy a marginally different initialisation race on a warm boot, and yesterday's first disk is today's second. Naming the filesystem by an identifier stored *inside* it disposes of the question. The label travels with the thing, rather than with the socket it happens to be plugged into.

Now, at last, the kernel executes the first real program. It is given process number one, and it is privileged in ways we shall return to. Its business is to start everything else, and for the greater part of Linux's life that business was conducted by a program called init, which ran a great many shell scripts one after another in an order determined by numbers in their filenames.

I want to be fair to that design, it being fashionable to be unfair to it. It was comprehensible. You could read it. When it misbehaved you could see precisely where, because it was shell script and you could plant an echo in it. Its defects were two. It was serial — each script awaited the last, so a boot took as long as the sum of everything in it. And it had no real notion of what it had started. It ran a script; the script forked a daemon into the background and reported success; and thereafter the system's knowledge of that daemon consisted of a file containing a number, written by the daemon itself, which might or might not still correspond to anything alive. The entire edifice of process supervision on classical Unix rested upon a text file containing a number, maintained on the honour system.

Then, in two thousand and ten, systemd arrived, and the community enjoyed the most entertaining quarrel of the decade.

I shall not relitigate it. I shall tell you what the thing actually does, which is of more use than either the enthusiasm or the fury.

Systemd replaces the ordered list of scripts with a *dependency graph* of small declarative files called units. A unit declares what it is, what it requires, what must precede it, and how it is to be started. Systemd reads the whole graph, works out what may proceed in parallel, and sets it all going at once. That is the provenance of the speed — not conjuring, but the observation that most of what a system does at boot is wait, and that waiting may be done concurrently.

Two of its ideas deserve your attention on their merits.

The first is socket activation, honestly borrowed from older systems. Rather than starting a daemon and hoping it opens its port before anything attempts to connect, systemd opens the port *itself*, at boot, immediately, and holds the queue. Should something connect, systemd starts the daemon and hands over the socket already open. The client never learns that it waited. This dissolves an entire genus of ordering problem: one no longer needs to know that the logging daemon must precede the mail daemon, because the mail daemon's first log line will simply wait in a socket until there is a logging daemon to read it.

The second is that systemd does not track processes by a text file containing a number. It places each service in its own *control group* — a kernel-level enclosure for accounting and control, which we shall meet properly in due course — and the kernel thereafter tells it, authoritatively, which processes belong to that service. All of them, including any that attempted to slip out by forking twice, which was the classical daemon's way of leaving by the back door. When you stop a service now, it stops, together with every child it ever spawned, because the register is kept by the kernel rather than by the daemon on its own recognisance.

That is likewise why the logs changed. Holding each service's output stream directly, systemd can capture everything a service writes, attribute it to the service that wrote it, and store it structurally rather than as lines of text in a file. Whether you prefer that to plain files is a question of taste on which I shall not intrude; but the reason for it is not fashion. It is that the supervisor is now the process's actual parent and has the output in its hands.

And one last small thing you have certainly typed without knowing why. Having edited a unit file, you must tell systemd to reload before the change takes any effect — because systemd parsed the whole graph at start-up and holds it in memory, and is not consulting your file afresh each time. You are not refreshing a cache. You are asking the majordomo to re-read the household's standing orders, which he committed to memory this morning and has not glanced at since.

The machine is up. The services are running. A login prompt waits. Let us look at what one of these running things actually *is*.

## Part Three — The Anatomy of a Running Thing

Ask an engineer what a process is and he will generally offer "a running program", which is true in the sense that "a house is somewhere one lives" is true.

A process is a *bundle of resources* the kernel maintains on your behalf, and the contents of the bundle repay being named aloud, since nearly every surprising behaviour in this quarter of the system follows from what is in it and what is shared.

It contains: a private map of memory, the subject of our next part and much the largest item. A set of registers and a stack for each thread of execution. A table of open files, numbered from zero. A set of credentials — which user, which groups, which particular powers. A current directory. A set of environment variables. A parent, and a roll of children. A collection of signal dispositions. And a great deal of accounting: processor time consumed, faults taken, the hour at which it began.

Now, threads. There persists a belief that threads and processes are two distinct species of object. On Linux they are, in essence, one object under different arrangements of sharing. The kernel's unit is the *task*, and a task has everything I have just enumerated. When you create a new task, you specify item by item which of those resources it shares with its creator and of which it takes a private copy. Share the memory map and the file table, and you have what the world calls a thread. Share nothing, and you have what the world calls a process. The kernel is largely indifferent to which word you favour; it is the same call under different flags. That is a genuinely uncommon design choice — most systems keep the two apart as separate orders of being — and it accounts for the fact that a thread on Linux carries its own identifier and will appear in a process listing if you ask nicely, to the perennial bewilderment of those arriving from elsewhere.

And now to what is, I think, the strangest good idea in the whole of Unix: the manner in which a new process comes into the world.

You might expect a call meaning "run this program". No such call exists. There are instead two, used in sequence, and the sequence is peculiar.

The first is *fork*, and what it does is duplicate the calling process entire. The new process receives a copy of the memory, a copy of the file table, the same current directory, the same environment, the same credentials, and — here is the part that unsettles people on first acquaintance — it resumes at precisely the same instruction, with the same values in the same registers. The call returns *twice*: once in the original, reporting the identifier of the new child, and once in the child, reporting zero. There is no other distinction between them whatever. Two processes walk away from a single line of code, told apart only by what that line handed back.

The second call is *exec*, and it performs an equally strange office in the opposite direction. It creates nothing. It takes the process you already are and replaces its entire contents with a different program — discards the memory map, loads the new executable, resets the stack, jumps to the new entry point. The process identifier is unchanged. The open files are unchanged. Credentials, current directory, environment: all carried across. The body is replaced; the identity persists. It is less the starting of a program than a remarkably thorough possession.

So running a command is: fork, and then, in the child, exec. Which appears roundabout, and is the kind of thing that moves a sensible man to ask why in heaven's name it was not one call.

The answer is the whole reason I am detaining you on this, and it is the point at which the design repays its cost several times over. Between the fork and the exec there lies a window, in which you are running your own code, inside a process that is about to become the new program — and in that window you may arrange the world the new program is to wake up in.

Which is exactly what a shell does. When you run a command with its output redirected to a file, the shell forks; and in the child, before the exec, it opens the file, and then takes the descriptor for that file and *duplicates it onto descriptor number one*, the number everything on Unix agrees to mean standard output. Then it execs. The new program starts, writes to standard output as it has always written to standard output, knowing nothing whatever of any of this, and the bytes land in the file.

The program was never told. The program *cannot* tell. There is no redirection feature within it; there is only the circumstance that the descriptor it was born holding happened to point at a file rather than a terminal. Every redirect, every pipeline, every command substitution you have typed in twenty-five years is that one manoeuvre — a quiet rearrangement of the furniture in the interval between conception and identity. A pipeline is the same manoeuvre performed twice with a pipe between: the kernel makes a pipe, which is no more than a buffer with a reading end and a writing end, and the shell settles one end onto the writer's output descriptor and the other onto the reader's input descriptor before each exec.

I have watched wholly competent engineers use pipelines for decades without knowing this, and I say so without a particle of condescension, since the design's great virtue is precisely that one need not know. But there is a dividend in knowing, and here it is. It explains, instantly and completely, why redirection composes with absolutely everything; why it behaves identically for programs written in every language ever compiled on the system; and why it requires no cooperation whatever from those programs. It is not a feature of the shell conversing with the program. It is a fact about the world into which the program awakens.

The same window accounts for a good deal else the child inherits — the environment, the current directory, the resource limits, and, if the code is your own, essentially any state you care to arrange. And it accounts, in the other direction, for the perennial confusion about changing directory within a script. A script that changes directory changes *its own*, and its own was a copy struck at the fork. The parent's is untouched. There exists no mechanism by which a child may reach upward and alter its parent, and this is not an oversight but the isolation working exactly as designed. Which is why the shell's directory-changing command is built into the shell rather than living on disk as a program. It *cannot* be a program. A program would change its own copy and then exit, having accomplished nothing.

Let us look inside the exec, since a modern program does not merely start.

The executable on disk is in a format called ELF: a header, then sections of code and data, and a note as to how it wishes to be loaded. Were the program statically linked, the kernel would map it into memory, jump to it, and the matter would end there. But almost nothing is statically linked, because almost everything wants the standard library, and one copy of that library in memory shared among four hundred processes is self-evidently preferable to four hundred copies.

So the header names an accomplice: the *dynamic linker*. The kernel loads that as well, and jumps to *it* first rather than to your program. The linker does the real work — reads the roll of libraries your program requires, finds them, maps them in, then walks your program's table of external references, entering the true address of every function it may call. Only then does it jump to your program's entry point. There is a small industry at work before the first line of your own code is reached.

That arrangement is the entire explanation of a trick you have certainly employed and possibly regarded as sorcery. Because the linker resolves names by searching a list of libraries in order, and because an environment variable exists which inserts a library at the head of that list, you may interpose your own version of any function in any program, at start-up, without recompiling a thing. Yours is searched first; yours is found first; the program calls yours. That is how debugging tools inject themselves, how a certain kind of sandbox operates, how memory allocators are swapped wholesale, and — inevitably — how a quantity of malware makes itself at home. It is not a subversion of the system. It is the system's designed extension point, being put to uses its designers had not envisaged.

One refinement, which explains something subtle about the cost of starting. Resolving every symbol at start-up is wasteful, most programs calling only a fraction of what they link against. So resolution is by default *lazy*: each external function initially addresses a stub which, on first call, enters the linker, obtains the true address, records it in a table, and proceeds there. Every subsequent call goes straight through. This makes start-up brisker and the first call to each function marginally slower. It also makes that table an inviting target for anyone wishing to redirect a program's control flow, which is why hardened builds resolve everything at once and then set the table read-only. That is a security posture with a real and measurable cost at start-up; it is the right trade; and you now know what is being traded.

Before endings, a word on *who* a process is, since it explains the most-used command on your machine.

Among those resources are the credentials: a user identifier, a set of group identifiers, and, on modern kernels, a set of individual powers. They live in the kernel, attached to the process. They are not a property of your session, nor of the terminal, nor of anything above stairs. When a process asks to open a file, the kernel sets the credentials in its own records against the ownership and permission bits on the inode, and adjudicates.

Observe *when* it adjudicates: at the open. Once you hold a descriptor, the question has been settled and will not be reopened. Reduce a file's permissions to nothing at all and the process already holding it open reads on contentedly, nobody being engaged in re-examining a decision already taken. This is not a loophole but the design, and every service that drops its privileges after opening its files and ports depends on it utterly.

Two details of the permission bits repay mention, both being routinely misread. On a *directory*, the read bit and the execute bit mean quite different things: read permits you to list the names, execute permits you to pass through to what lies beyond. So a directory may be traversable but not listable — you may open a file within it if you already know its name, but you may not discover what is there. A genuinely useful arrangement, and a good deal of shared hosting stands upon it. And on a shared writable directory, the sticky bit provides that although anyone may create files, only a file's owner may remove it — which is the sole reason the temporary directory is not a free-for-all in which any user may delete another's work.

Now, credentials ordinarily pass from parent to child unaltered, which raises the question of how one ever *acquires* privilege. If a process may only inherit, and the chain of inheritance runs back to the first process of all, by what route does an ordinary user become the superuser?

By a bit on the executable file. When it is set, an exec of that file confers upon the resulting process the credentials of the file's *owner* rather than the caller's. That is the one sanctioned point in the entire system at which privilege is manufactured rather than inherited, and it is why the command you use to elevate yourself is owned by the superuser and carries that bit. It is running as root by virtue of the exec itself — before it has done anything, before it has asked you for a password. The password does not confer the privilege. The privilege was conferred at the exec; the password is that program, already privileged, deciding whether to act on your behalf.

Which recasts the whole arrangement rather usefully. Every such program is a small, permanently armed privilege escalation that has undertaken to check your credentials first, and whose correctness is therefore the only thing standing between any user and the machine entire. The set is therefore kept deliberately minute, each member of it scrutinised without mercy, and the mechanism is being retired in favour of granting individual capabilities to individual files — so that the program which sends network probes may hold the single power it needs to open a raw socket, rather than being handed the kingdom and trusted to occupy one room of it.

And that is why the bit is ignored on scripts. A script is not executed by the kernel at all: the kernel reads its first line, finds the interpreter named there, and executes *that* instead — which opens a window between deciding what to run and running it, and an attacker who can exchange the file within that window has the interpreter running as root against a file of his choosing. The race was closed by the simple expedient of declining to honour the bit on anything the kernel does not execute directly. If you have ever set it on a shell script and watched nothing whatever happen, there is your reason, and the remedy is not to try harder.

Now, endings.

When a process finishes it does not vanish. It calls exit with a small number, and the kernel dismantles its memory and closes its files — but retains the husk of its entry in the process table, holding the exit status, in case the parent should wish to know how the child got on. Such a process is dead and still listed, and it is called, in the finest coinage our field has produced, a *zombie*.

The parent clears it by calling wait, which collects the status and releases the entry. Should the parent never trouble to, the zombies accumulate unreaped. They consume neither memory nor processor time, being pure bookkeeping, but they consume table entries; and the table is finite; and a program that forks vigorously and never reaps its dead will in the end render the machine incapable of starting anything at all.

Should the parent die first, the orphaned children are made over to process number one, which is expected to reap dutifully whatever arrives at its door. That is why the first process is special beyond the mere accident of being first: it is the system's undertaker.

Which brings me to something that has bitten a great many people in the past decade. When you run a container, the process you named becomes process number one *within* it. And your program — a web server, a script, whatever it may be — was never written to be an undertaker. So when something inside forks and dies, the orphan is made over to your program, which does not reap it, and the zombies accumulate in a place nobody thinks to look. Which is the origin of the flag the container tooling grew, which inserts a tiny purpose-built first process whose whole occupation is to reap the dead and pass signals along. If you have ever added that flag because a forum told you to, that is its office. You have engaged an undertaker for a house that lacked one.

The exit status itself is worth thirty seconds, since you read it constantly. A process exiting normally supplies a small number, zero conventionally meaning success. A process that is *killed* has no opportunity to supply anything, so the shell reports the fact by adding the signal's number to one hundred and twenty-eight. Which is why a process struck down by the ninth signal reports one hundred and thirty-seven, and one dispatched by the fifteenth reports one hundred and forty-three. Those two numbers are engraved on the memory of everyone who has read container logs at three in the morning, generally without either the arithmetic or the meaning ever having been made plain. One hundred and thirty-seven, in particular, nearly always means the memory manager has killed you, and we shall meet the gentleman responsible shortly.

Signals themselves are the system's crude, ancient, indispensable means of interruption. A signal is a number delivered to a process out of the blue; the process may have registered a handler, or may suffer the default disposition, which for most signals is death. Crude, because there are only a few dozen of them, because they carry almost no information, and because delivering one means interrupting the program wherever it happens to stand — from which it follows that a handler may safely do only a very few things. Nearly every rule about the austerity of signal handlers descends from the fact that yours may have interrupted the very library function it is about to call.

Two signals may be neither caught, nor blocked, nor ignored: the one that stops a process dead and the one that suspends it. This is deliberate. There must exist a means of arresting a program that has resolved not to cooperate.

Which allows me to dispose of an old article of folklore. It is said that the ninth signal always kills. It does — but not always *at once*, and in that distinction lies the whole story. A process in the middle of a system call, waiting in the kernel on a device, cannot be terminated until it emerges. Most waits are interruptible and the kernel will abandon them and deliver the signal. Some are not: a read from a wedged network filesystem, a driver stalled on hardware that has stopped answering. Such a process shows in the listing in an uninterruptible state, will not die, and will not be moved by any amount of escalation, because the request has not returned and the kernel will not abandon a process halfway inside a driver. It is not ignoring you. It is not *there*. It is below stairs, waiting for a tradesman who is never coming.

Finally the terminal, which resolves several daily mysteries at a stroke.

Processes are gathered into *process groups*, typically one per pipeline you launch, and into *sessions*, typically one per login. A session may possess a controlling terminal, and that terminal has, at any moment, exactly one *foreground* process group. The terminal driver in the kernel — not a passive conduit but an active piece of machinery — watches the characters you type; and on seeing the interrupt character it delivers it to nobody. It sends a signal to every process in the foreground group.

So *that* is why the interrupt key stops the thing in front of you and not the things running behind: it is aimed at the foreground group by definition. That is why it stops a whole pipeline at once rather than one member of it: the pipeline *is* the group. And that is why a program may decline to die of it — death is merely the default disposition, and a handler may be installed instead, which is precisely what your editor does.

The same machinery explains the hangup. When your terminal ceases to exist — the connection drops, you close the window — the kernel sends the hangup signal to the session, and the default disposition is death. So closing a session kills the long job you had left running; the traditional remedy runs the job with that signal ignored; and the modern remedy is to run it inside a terminal multiplexer, which keeps alive a terminal that nobody need be looking at. The whole of it descends from a design intended for physical teleprinters connected by actual telephone lines that could actually be hung up, and every one of us still lives inside that metaphor.

And now, memory — where the kernel ceases merely to manage your program and begins to lie to it magnificently.

## Part Four — The Great and Necessary Lie

Every program on your machine believes it possesses the entire memory of the computer, laid out in one vast unbroken expanse, beginning at the beginning, with nobody else in it.

This is false. It is false of every program simultaneously, in mutually contradictory ways, and the machinery that sustains the falsehood is, I would contend, the most important thing in the box after the processor itself.

The arrangement runs thus. When your program names an address, that address is not a location in the memory chips. It is a *virtual* address: a number bearing meaning only within your process. Between processor and memory sits a piece of hardware whose entire occupation is translation — taking the address your program used and producing the physical address it truly denotes. Translation proceeds by consulting a set of tables in memory; those tables are built and owned by the kernel; and the register that points at them may be altered only from the privileged humour.

Translation is conducted in units called *pages*, four kilobytes apiece by long convention. For each page of your virtual expanse the tables declare either "this corresponds to that physical page, under these permissions" or "there is nothing here". They are arranged as a tree, four levels deep on ordinary hardware and five on the newest, a flat table for a sixty-four-bit address space being larger than the universe will permit. The hardware walks the tree on your behalf; and since walking four levels at every memory reference would be ruinous, the processor keeps a small cache of recent translations. That cache is finite, and when a program's working set of pages outgrows it, performance falls off a cliff for reasons invisible in the source and unrelated to the algorithm. Hence the existence of very large pages as an option: one great page occupies a single cache entry where five hundred small ones would occupy five hundred.

Everything else in this part follows from a single observation: *the kernel owns those tables, and may therefore put in them whatever it pleases — including nothing at all.*

Consider what ensues when your program touches an address whose entry says "there is nothing here". The hardware cannot proceed. It raises a fault; the processor assumes the privileged humour; and the kernel is handed the address and invited to say what it signifies.

Now — and here is the inversion I promised — the kernel's answer is very often *"quite all right, sir; one moment"*.

Because the fault need not be an error. The fault is the *mechanism*. When you ask for a great block of memory, the kernel does not go and find that much memory. It records that the region is yours, marks the pages absent, and returns at once. Nothing has been allocated. When at length you touch a page, the fault fires, and only then does the kernel find a physical page, zero it, install it in your tables, and resume your program at the very instruction that failed — which now succeeds, in blissful ignorance that anything occurred. Allocation happens at first touch, one page at a time, driven wholly by faults. A program that asks for a gigabyte and uses a kilobyte has cost you a kilobyte.

The same stratagem loads your programs. Running a large executable, the kernel does not read it into memory. It *maps* it — writes entries declaring that these pages correspond to that region of that file, and marks them absent. As execution proceeds, each page is faulted in from disk on demand. A program whose start-up path touches a tenth of its code has read a tenth of its file. So enormous binaries start far faster than their size would suggest; and the page cache and the memory map are, on Linux, not two systems but one, to which I shall return.

And the same stratagem makes the fork we discussed a moment ago affordable. Duplicating a process's entire memory would be preposterous, the more so as the child is generally about to discard all of it in an exec. So the kernel does not copy. It points both processes' tables at the *same* physical pages and marks every one of them read-only in both. Either may read at leisure, sharing everything, at no cost whatever. But when either one *writes*, the write offends against the read-only marking, and the fault arrives at the kernel — which now perceives what is really afoot, takes a private copy of that single page, installs it writable in the writer's tables, and resumes. The copying is done lazily, page by page, only for pages actually altered, and quite possibly never.

That is *copy-on-write*, and it is why a process holding four gigabytes may fork in microseconds. It is also, as it happens, the precise machinery in which the flaw I described earlier resided: a race between determining that a write requires a private copy and actually taking one, which could be induced — given sufficiently unkind timing — to write to the original instead. The most elegant mechanisms have the most interesting failures.

Now let us do the thing this part exists to do, and demolish an article of folklore you have carried for twenty-five years.

You look at a machine's memory and observe that almost none of it is free. Nearly the whole of it sits in a category labelled buffers and cache. The instinctive response — and I have watched grown engineers act upon this instinct in production — is alarm, followed by an attempt at reclamation.

The instinct is exactly backwards. That memory is not lost. It is the *page cache*, and it is the kernel doing its work properly.

Every time a file is read, the kernel retains the data afterwards, on the entirely reasonable theory that it may be wanted again. Every time a file is written, the data enters memory and is committed to disk shortly after by a background thread. All input and output on the system passes through this cache, and it is the single greatest contributor to your machine feeling quick. And it is *wholly reclaimable*: the instant any program requires memory, the kernel discards as much cache as needed, at no cost, the data being on disk in any case.

Free memory, then, is not a hoard you are prudently keeping. Free memory is memory doing nothing. A well-run Linux machine has almost none, precisely as a well-run kitchen has almost no empty worktop — not from disorder but from use. The figure to watch is not what is free but what is *available*, which the system reports separately and which counts the reclaimable cache as yours for the asking. There exists a command to drop the caches by hand. It exists for benchmarking. Employing it in production is a method of making your machine slow for several minutes in exchange for a more becoming number in a summary.

While I am about it, the two figures beside it. A process listing reports a virtual size and a resident size. The virtual size is how much *address space* the process has mapped — which, given everything I have just said about mapping costing nothing, is close to meaningless. A program that maps a hundred-gigabyte file of which it will read four bytes has a virtual size of a hundred gigabytes and is using nothing at all. The resident size counts the physical pages it actually holds, which is a good deal more honest but carries a trap of its own: shared pages are counted in full against every process sharing them, so totting up the resident sizes on your machine will cheerfully yield a figure larger than the memory you own. The figure that apportions shared pages fairly among their users exists too, and is the one to reach for when the question is truly "who is using the memory".

Now, the disagreeable consequence of all this ingenuity.

Because a mapping costs almost nothing until touched, the kernel routinely permits programs to map, between them, far more memory than physically exists. This is *overcommit*; it is on by default; and it is a wager — historically an excellent one, programs being habitually more ambitious in their asking than in their using, and the fork mechanism alone being unusable otherwise.

But a wager may be lost. If enough programs touch enough of what they asked for, the kernel arrives at a moment where a fault has occurred, a physical page must be produced, there is no cache left to reclaim, there is nothing left to swap, and there is simply no page to be had. There is no graceful exit. The request cannot be refused, since the program that faulted was merely writing to memory it had been told was its own, and there is nowhere to report a refusal to.

So the kernel kills something. This is the out-of-memory killer, and it is one of the few places in the system where the design is frankly and avowedly a choice of the least bad thing available.

It selects its victim by score, weighing chiefly how much memory a process is using, adjusted by a knob you may set per process to render something more or less eligible. The natural consequence — and the source of a great deal of confusion and unfair blame — is that it generally kills the *largest* process, which is generally the most important one. Your database did not cause the crisis. Your database was merely the stoutest thing in the room when the decision fell. Somewhere a runaway script was asking for memory in a loop; the database was executed in its stead. If you have ever found your principal service dead with nothing in its own logs, and the system log recording that the kernel selected it and terminated it — and if you have ever seen a container exit with that number one hundred and thirty-seven — this is what befell you, and you now know both the mechanism and the injustice.

Which leads me to swap, on which the folklore is likewise inverted.

The received wisdom holds that swap is a relic, that memory is cheap, and that a serious machine should have none. The reasoning is that swapping is slow, and therefore swap is bad.

But consider what memory the kernel can actually reclaim under pressure. File-backed pages — program code, cached file data — may always be discarded, since they exist on disk. Anonymous pages — your heap, your stack, everything a program computed rather than read — cannot be discarded, there being nowhere for them to go. *Unless there is swap.*

A machine without swap therefore does not avoid paging. It merely confines the kernel to evicting one category. Under pressure it must throw away file pages, which on a busy machine means throwing away the executable code of running programs, which are thereupon immediately faulted back in. That is a machine thrashing on its own binaries while a hundred megabytes of some daemon's heap — untouched since start-up, ideal swap material — sits resident and immovable. The system with a modest quantity of swap has a *choice*, and makes a better one. And the knob everyone reaches for does not mean "how much to swap"; it expresses the kernel's relative appetite for evicting anonymous pages as against file pages.

I shall be fair to the other side. On a machine under strict latency obligations, an unlooked-for fault to a slow disk is genuinely intolerable, and disabling swap so that the failure mode becomes "die quickly" rather than "degrade mysteriously" is a defensible piece of engineering. What is not defensible is the belief that a machine without swap does no paging. It pages constantly. It merely pages the things it can least afford to lose.

Two closing notes on this part.

The kernel's own memory is not paged. It cannot be: the code that handles a fault may not itself be absent when the fault arrives. Kernel memory is therefore drawn from its own pools — a buddy allocator dispensing physical pages in runs of powers of two, and above it an allocator for the small, numerous, identically shaped objects the kernel is forever creating and destroying: the record of an open file, the record of a directory entry, the record of a network connection. These are the caches you find itemised when you ask the system where its memory has gone, and they are a common answer to the question "the processes account for only half of it; where is the rest?"

And finally, the modern refinement. Since the kernel can see exactly how much time is being lost to memory pressure, it now reports the fact directly: a measure of how much of the last interval was spent with work stalled waiting on memory rather than running. That is a far better early warning than watching the free figure, because it measures the thing you actually care about — whether you are suffering — rather than a number which, as we have established, is supposed to be small.

Such is the lie, and such the machinery that sustains it. Now: given all these processes, each convinced the machine is its own, who decides which of them actually runs?

## Part Five — The Dividing of the Hours

A moderately busy machine carries some hundreds of processes and, let us say, sixteen places at table. Somebody must decide who sits.

The scheduler's problem is harder than it looks, its objectives being at odds with one another. It must be *fair*, or a greedy program starves the rest. It must be *responsive*, or your keystrokes arrive late while a compilation grinds away. It must respect *priority*, or you have no means of saying what matters. It must exploit *locality*, since moving a task to another processor throws away everything that processor had learned about it. And it must settle all of this in well under a microsecond, some thousands of times a second, without itself becoming the load.

A task surrenders the processor by one of two routes. It may yield voluntarily, which is what occurs whenever it blocks — asks for a file, waits on a lock, sleeps. In practice this is the common case; most tasks spend most of their existence waiting. Or it may be *preempted*: a timer interrupt arrives, the kernel takes stock, and judges another's need the greater. That second route is what makes the system proof against programs that never yield, and its existence is why a runaway loop renders your machine sluggish rather than dead.

For many years the timer fired at a fixed rate whether or not there was anything to do, which was tidy and squandered a great deal of power rousing idle processors to be told that nothing awaited them. Modern kernels are largely *tickless*: an idle processor may sleep until there is genuinely something to attend to. Your laptop's battery owes a good deal to that change, as does every hypervisor holding hundreds of mostly idle guests.

As to *whom* it chooses: for some fifteen years the answer was an algorithm called the Completely Fair Scheduler, whose central idea is worth carrying away even now that it has been superseded. It gave each task a *virtual* clock, advancing as the task ran but at a rate divided by the task's weight, so that a task of twice the weight had its clock advance half as quickly. The rule was then simply to run whoever's clock stood furthest behind. No slices to tune, no heuristics sniffing about for "interactive" tasks — a ledger of debts and an instruction to serve whoever is owed most. And the politeness value you have been setting on processes since the nineteen-eighties is precisely that weight, on a scale where each step is about a tenth part, so that the relation between two tasks is a ratio and not an absolute promise. A polite task on an otherwise idle machine still receives everything.

In late two thousand and twenty-three that was displaced, in the six-point-six kernel, by a scheme rejoicing in the name Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First — which is a mouthful, and which rests on a paper from the middle nineties, our field's notion of novelty being idiosyncratic. It keeps the ledger and adds the notion of a *deadline*: each task receives not merely a claim upon its share but an hour by which that share ought to have been delivered, and the scheduler serves the most pressing eligible claim. The practical gain is that latency becomes something a task may *state* — a program wanting to be woken promptly and then to run briefly may say so, rather than leaving the kernel to infer it from behaviour. If you have been reading release notes and wondering what became of the fair scheduler, that is what became of it.

Two facts of implementation leak into your working life and are worth holding.

The first is that there is not one queue but one per processor, with periodic balancing between them, and the balancing is deliberately reluctant. Migrating a task means its cached data is on the wrong core and must be fetched afresh; and on a large machine with memory attached to particular sockets, it may mean its *memory* now lies on the far side of the house, at a standing penalty. Pinning therefore matters for latency-sensitive work, and the benchmark that improved when you pinned it was not a fluke.

The second is that a context switch is not free and its cost is largely invisible. Saving and restoring registers is trivial. Changing address spaces means changing page tables, which — as we saw — costs translation-cache entries, and since the Meltdown mitigations a good deal more besides. The incoming task then runs with a processor cache full of its predecessor's data and spends its first several thousand instructions fetching its own back. None of this appears in any profile under its own name. It appears as everything being slightly slower, which is the most difficult species of cost to hunt down.

Let me now deal properly with interrupts, since they compete for the same hours.

When a device wants attention it raises an interrupt, and the processor abandons everything to run the handler. Because this occurs with the current work suspended, and often with further interrupts disabled, the handler must be *brief*; the discipline on this point is fanatical. So the work is divided. The immediate handler does the minimum — acknowledge the device, seize the data, note that there is work outstanding. The work itself is deferred to a softer context running shortly afterwards with interrupts enabled, preemptible like anything else.

Should the deferred work become overwhelming — a network card receiving at full tilt, say — the kernel ceases to run it inline and consigns it to a dedicated thread, one per processor, which you may see in your process listing and which is *visible to the scheduler*. That is an elegant safety valve: when the interrupt load exceeds what can be absorbed invisibly, it becomes a schedulable citizen competing on the same terms as everyone else, rather than starving the machine from underneath.

A machine under heavy network load may therefore show a processor at full utilisation with no process to blame for it. And on a card with a single queue, all that work lands on whichever processor the interrupt is routed to, and one core saturates while fifteen sit idle — whence the remedies, which are to spread the interrupts across cores, or to obtain a card with many queues, or to have the kernel distribute the received packets in software.

And now the number everybody quotes and almost nobody understands.

The load average is not processor utilisation. It is not a percentage. On Linux specifically — and this is a genuine local peculiarity, a decision taken in the early nineties which diverged from the rest of Unix and was never revisited — the load average counts not merely tasks running or waiting to run, but tasks in *uninterruptible sleep*: those stalled on a device.

The consequence is that the load average measures *demand upon the system*, storage included, rather than demand upon the processor. A machine showing fifty with sixteen idle cores is not computationally overloaded in the least; it is a machine on which thirty-odd processes are sitting on a storage device that has stopped answering. And a machine showing two may be in genuine distress, if the two are the only things that matter and they are being starved. The three figures, incidentally, are not averages over the last minute, five and fifteen; they are exponentially damped, which is why the first moves briskly and the last has a long memory. As a shape they are useful: rising means worsening, falling means passing. As an absolute number set against your core count they are close to meaningless, and the ritual of so setting them is exactly the kind of cargo cult we are here to dismantle.

Finally, the manner in which time is really divided on a modern server, which is not by politeness values at all but by *control groups*.

The scheduler offers a group of processes two quite distinct controls. One is a *weight*: a share of whatever is going, taking effect only under contention, and behaving much as the politeness value does when applied to a whole group at once. The other is a *quota*: this group may consume no more than so much processor time in each interval, full stop, though the machine be entirely idle.

These are wildly different in character and are routinely confounded, and the confusion produces one of the most common and most baffling faults in modern infrastructure. Impose a hard quota on a service and the accounting runs thus: the group receives an allowance at the opening of each interval — a tenth of a second, typically — and when the allowance is spent, *every thread in the group is stopped* until the interval closes.

Now consider a service of sixteen worker threads under a quota of two processors' worth. Those sixteen, running in parallel, may consume a two-processor allowance in an eighth of the interval. Whereupon the entire service — all sixteen — is frozen for the remaining seven-eighths. Not slowed. Stopped. Its average utilisation is exactly the two processors you asked for, so every dashboard shows it comfortably within its means, while its response times have acquired a tail of eighty-odd milliseconds appearing in no profile whatever — because from the program's own vantage nothing happened at all. Time merely passed between two instructions.

That is the throttling trap, and I raise it because it is the perfect specimen of what this entire tour is for. You cannot debug it from above stairs. Everything above stairs looks fine. You can debug it only by knowing that a mechanism of that shape exists below, and then going to read the one counter which records it — the number of intervals in which the group was throttled — which the kernel has been keeping faithfully the whole time, waiting for somebody to ask.

And since we are on the subject of hours, let me settle the debt I opened with.

Your machine does not have a clock. It has several, and they disagree on purpose.

One is the wall clock: the time a human would recognise, the one your log lines carry. It is disciplined by the time protocol against servers elsewhere, and is therefore *adjustable*. It may be corrected. It may leap forward. It may leap *backward*, should your machine's notion of the hour have run ahead of the world's. And on the thirtieth of June in certain years it may be told to sit perfectly still for a second while the Earth catches up.

The other is the monotonic clock. It counts from some arbitrary instant — boot, typically — and its only undertaking is that it shall never run backwards and never leap. It is not adjusted. It is not corrected. It cares nothing for what year it is or what any external authority believes.

The rule that follows is the single most valuable thing I can tell you about time on a computer, and it is violated constantly in production code: *the wall clock is for saying when a thing happened; the monotonic clock is for measuring how long it took.* Any code computing a duration by subtracting two wall-clock readings is a bug that has not yet been provoked. It will report negative durations when the clock is corrected backwards. It will report a timeout as instantly expired, or as expiring in an hour, according to which way the correction went. It will do so rarely, unreproducibly, and in production. Every timeout, every retry interval, every latency measurement, every rate limiter ought to be counting monotonic ticks.

You will observe, I hope, that this is precisely the shape of the disaster I opened with. A second was inserted into the world's timekeeping; a subsystem holding deadlines was not told; and everything that had said "wake me shortly" discovered its appointment already in the past. The same bug, committed inside the kernel rather than above it, at a scale sufficient to embarrass several airlines.

Two footnotes, both enjoyable.

Since it is the leap that does the damage, the protocol prefers not to leap. For small discrepancies it *slews*: it runs your clock imperceptibly fast or slow until the error is absorbed, so that time is never discontinuous, merely briefly untruthful as to its rate. Only for gross errors does it step. And the largest operators of computers concluded some years ago that even a one-second step was more than they cared to survive, and simply declined to implement the leap second at all — spreading it instead across many hours as an adjustment of rate, so that their machines never meet an irregular second, only a day whose every second is very slightly longer than a second. Time, at that scale, is a matter for negotiation.

The second footnote is that we have a deadline of our own. The traditional representation of time on Unix counts seconds from the beginning of nineteen-seventy in a signed thirty-two-bit number, and that number runs out in the year twenty thirty-eight, in January, on a Tuesday. On sixty-four-bit systems the matter was settled long ago. On the enormous population of thirty-two-bit embedded devices — the routers, the meters, the controllers, the things inside walls — it demanded a substantial and frankly heroic reworking of the kernel's interfaces, completed only in recent years. Whether the devices in question will ever receive that kernel is a separate question, and not one I should care to be answerable for.

## Part Six — In Which Everything Is a File, Very Nearly

You have had the slogan by heart for twenty-five years: on Unix, everything is a file. Like most slogans it is a compression of something more interesting, and the unpacking is worth the minutes.

The accurate form is: *everything is a file descriptor*. And a file descriptor is a small integer.

That integer indexes a table the kernel keeps for your process. Nothing further. When you open something, the kernel finds the lowest unused slot in your table, points it at the thing, and hands you the number. When you read, you present the number and the kernel looks it up. The number means nothing outside your process — descriptor seven here and descriptor seven there have not the remotest connection with one another.

There is a subtlety worth a moment, since it accounts for behaviour that otherwise reads as a defect. There are in fact *two* levels of indirection, not one. Your table entry points at an *open file description* — an object holding the current position in the file, the mode it was opened under, and so forth — and *that* points at the file itself. The middle object exists in order that it may be shared. When you fork, parent and child share it, and therefore share the *position*. When the shell duplicates a descriptor onto standard output, both refer to that one object, and share the position likewise.

And so: two processes appending to the same log through descriptors inherited from a common ancestor will interleave tidily rather than overwrite one another, both advancing a single shared position. Whereas two processes that opened the same file independently hold separate middle objects, separate positions, and will cheerfully write over each other's work. Same file, same operations, entirely opposite outcomes — and the difference is a piece of kernel bookkeeping with no representation whatever in the shell syntax you typed.

Beneath the descriptor lies a stratum called the virtual filesystem, essentially an exercise in polymorphism conducted in C some years before that became fashionable. It defines what a filesystem *is* — open, read, write, look up a name, list a directory — and every actual filesystem furnishes an implementation. Which is how one read call behaves identically on a local disk, a network mount, a compressed archive mounted as a filesystem, a filesystem implemented by an ordinary user-space program, and a set of files that do not exist at all. We shall come to that last category shortly; it is my favourite.

Two objects hold the stratum up.

The *inode* is the file. Not the name — the *file*: its size, its permissions, its owner, its timestamps, and the whereabouts of its data. The name forms no part of it. The name lives in a directory, which is no more than a table pairing names with inode numbers.

From which it follows that a file may bear several names, all equally genuine, none of them the original. That is a hard link. And it follows that deleting a file does not delete a file: it removes a *name*, and decrements a count on the inode, and only when that count reaches zero does the data become eligible for removal.

Only when the count reaches zero — *and* nobody holds the file open.

There, sir, is the explanation of the commonest disk-space mystery in our profession. You find a log grown to consume the volume. You delete it. Free space does not stir. You look again, with more urgency, and it still does not stir, and the tool that lists directories maintains the file is gone while the tool that reports free space insists the space is spoken for.

Both are telling the truth. You removed the name. The process that has been writing to that file holds it open still, so the count is not zero, so the data remains — a file with no name, invisible to every directory listing, being appended to indefinitely by a program with no notion that anything has happened. The space returns when that process closes the descriptor or exits, which is why restarting the service "cures" it. And should you be unable to restart it, that being the very thing you were trying to keep alive, the file is reachable yet: the kernel exposes every process's descriptors as a directory of links, into which you may walk and read the contents of a file that no longer exists.

The second object is the directory entry, which the kernel caches with great enthusiasm, since resolving a path means walking it component by component, and doing so against the disk every time would be insupportable. That cache accounts for a large part of why a filesystem feels quick the second time.

Now, durability, where the important stories live.

When you write to a file, the data enters the page cache and the call returns. Nothing has reached the disk. A background mechanism commits dirty pages within some seconds; the thresholds governing it are tunable; and the default is a deliberate exchange of durability for speed, which is right for very nearly everything and catastrophic for a database.

Should you require the data actually on the medium, you must ask — by a call whose name every engineer knows and whose semantics rather fewer do. It flushes the file's dirty pages and waits.

And here is the story I have been looking forward to. In two thousand and eighteen the PostgreSQL developers discovered that their handling of that call was unsafe on Linux, in a manner that had gone unremarked for something like twenty years. The difficulty was this. When a write fails at the hardware level, the filesystem cannot hold the failed data in cache indefinitely; so it reports the error once, to whoever asks next, and then marks the pages clean and discards them. The data is gone. The error has been delivered.

Now consider a program doing the eminently sensible-looking thing: it flushes, it receives an error, and — being robust — it *retries*. The second flush finds no dirty pages, they having been discarded, and reports success. The program concludes that the data is safely on disk. It is not. It no longer exists anywhere at all.

There followed a furious and rather magnificent argument as to whose fault this was: whether the kernel had behaved unreasonably, whether the interface had ever promised what everyone had assumed, whether twenty years of database code had been built upon a misreading. The resolution was that PostgreSQL now treats a failure of that call as unrecoverable and deliberately crashes, on the ground that crashing and recovering from the write-ahead log is the only remaining route to certainty. A remarkable conclusion for a database to arrive at: that the safest response to a disk error is to panic.

I tell it because it is the finest illustration available of a general truth. The interface between your program and the kernel is a *contract*; the contract is narrower than intuition supposes; and twenty years of everyone assuming otherwise does not widen it by an inch. The correct procedure for durably replacing a file's contents — write to a temporary file, flush it, rename it over the original, and flush the *directory* — is passed among engineers like a recipe, in the manner of folklore. It is not folklore. Every step bears load, and you now know which clause of the contract each one satisfies.

A word on filesystems themselves, the choice being put to you at install time and seldom explained. The workhorse journals its *metadata*: before making a structural change it writes down its intention, so that a crash midway leaves the filesystem consistent — you may lose recent data, but you will not find a directory pointing at a block belonging to something else. The newer generation takes an entirely different tack: never overwrite anything; always write fresh blocks and then swing a pointer atomically. Consistency comes free with that, as do checksums that detect a disk quietly corrupting your data, and snapshots that cost almost nothing because sharing unaltered blocks is what the design does anyway. The price is complexity, and a fragmentation profile that can be unkind to spinning disks. The trade is real in both directions.

Below the filesystem lies the block layer, whose character has altered completely within a decade. When storage was a spinning platter, the layer's most valuable service was *reordering* — gathering requests and sorting them so that the head travelled sensibly. Modern solid-state storage has no head, serves many requests at once, and is quick enough that the time spent reordering cleverly may exceed the time thereby saved. So the modern default for such devices is, in essence, to stand aside.

Now let me show you my favourite thing in the filesystem layer, which is a filesystem containing no files.

The process information filesystem presents every process as a directory, and within it everything the kernel knows: the command line, the environment, the memory map, the open descriptors, the accounting. And none of it exists. There is no storage behind any of it. When you read one of those files, the kernel runs a function which composes the answer on the spot from live data. The file has no size until read, which is why the listing shows zeros, and which has bewildered everybody at least once.

Every process-inspecting tool on the system is therefore, underneath, a program that reads text files out of a directory. You may inspect a process with nothing but a shell. The system's tuning knobs are *files you write to* rather than a configuration interface. The decision was that the kernel's surface for introspection and control should be the filesystem, so that it might inherit everything the filesystem already possesses: names, permissions, tools, and the whole vocabulary you already speak.

The device directory is the same idea in older clothes. A device node is a file with no contents whatever; what it has instead is a pair of numbers naming which driver it belongs to and which instance of it. Open the node, and the virtual filesystem layer looks up the driver and routes your reads and writes to that driver's functions. So reading from a disk, reading from a serial port, and reading from a file traverse *precisely* the same path within your program and part company only at the last step. The slogan is no metaphor here; it is an implementation detail that happens to be visible.

That also accounts for the standing furniture of the directory. The bottomless sink which discards everything written to it is not a special case in the kernel's write path; it is a driver whose write function returns success and does nothing. The inexhaustible spring of zeroes is a driver whose read function fills your buffer. The source of randomness is a driver with considerably more going on behind it. Each is a few dozen lines. They are not features of the shell, nor of the filesystem; they are the smallest device drivers anyone has ever had occasion to write.

The nodes themselves were once created by hand, which was as dismal as it sounds: a machine's device directory was a great static collection of nodes for hardware it mostly did not possess. Now the kernel creates them as it discovers hardware, and announces each discovery to a daemon listening above stairs. That daemon applies your rules — it may rename the thing, set its permissions, hand it to a particular group, run a program, or, most usefully, lay a symbolic link beside it under a stable and meaningful name.

Which closes the loop on something from the second part. The reason you may name a disk by its filesystem identifier, or its hardware path, or its serial number, rather than by the unstable name the driver assigned in whatever order it found things, is that this daemon has laid a set of alternative names beside it, derived from properties of the device itself rather than from the accident of discovery. The unstable name persists underneath. You have merely been given better handles on the same object, by a program that watched it arrive.

And that brings us to the real payoff of the slogan, which has nothing to do with files.

Once everything is a descriptor, one mechanism may wait for *everything*. That matters enormously, the central problem of a server being to wait upon ten thousand things at once and be woken for whichever stirs first.

So the pattern was extended, deliberately, until it covered everything a program might have occasion to wait for. Pipes are descriptors. Sockets are descriptors. A *timer* may be a descriptor, becoming readable when it expires. A *signal* may be a descriptor, so that instead of being violently interrupted you may read your signals as data, at an hour of your own choosing, in ordinary code. Notifications of filesystem change are descriptors. Even a *process* may be a descriptor now — a stable reference to one particular process, immune to the ancient race in which the process you meant to signal has died and had its number reissued to somebody quite different.

Every one of those exists so that a single waiting call may encompass it. That is what the slogan is really about: not that a socket resembles a file, which it does not in the least, but that *there is exactly one way to wait*, and everything worth waiting for has been made to fit it.

The most recent chapter is a genuine break with the whole tradition, and it returns us to something I said in the first part. If crossing between the worlds is expensive, and a busy server crosses millions of times, then the answer is to stop crossing. So the modern interface establishes two ring buffers in memory shared between your program and the kernel: you write your requests into the one, and the kernel writes completions into the other. Under load you may submit hundreds of operations and gather hundreds of results having crossed the boundary once — or, with a kernel-side polling thread, not at all. It is the largest single change in how Linux performs input and output since the beginning, and it exists because the cost of ringing the bell at last became the dominant term.

## Part Seven — The Post, and the Machinery of Its Sorting

A packet arrives at your network card. Let us follow it, the journey accounting for a surprising quantity of operational folklore.

The card does not interrupt the processor with the packet. It writes the packet directly into memory the driver prepared in advance — into a ring of buffers the card owns — and only then signals that something has occurred. This matters enormously at modern speeds. A card receiving a million packets a second cannot raise a million interrupts a second, handling an interrupt costing more than handling a packet; the machine would spend its whole existence being told of work and none performing it. The failure mode has a name, and it was a real and much-discussed affliction.

The remedy is a civil little protocol between card and kernel. The first packet raises an interrupt in the ordinary way; the driver then *disables* the interrupt and invites the kernel to poll it. The kernel returns and drains as many packets as it can within a budget. When the ring runs dry, interrupts are re-enabled and the card resumes knocking. The system thus adjusts itself: at low rates you have interrupt-driven latency, under load you have the efficiency of polling, with nothing to configure and no threshold to tune. It is among the neater adaptive designs in the kernel.

Each packet is wrapped in a structure that accompanies it the whole way up, and the structure is contrived so that nothing is copied as the packet ascends. Headers are peeled by advancing a pointer. The link layer looks at it and passes it to the network layer; the network layer examines the destination and determines whether this is for us; if it is, the transport layer finds the socket it belongs to, appends the payload to that socket's receive queue, and wakes whatever process was waiting on it.

Now, along that path there are certain fixed points at which the kernel halts and consults a set of rules. Just after receipt, before routing. After the routing decision is taken. Just before transmission. And so forth.

Those points are the entire basis of firewalling on Linux, and grasping it dissolves most of the confusion that surrounds the subject. Your firewall is not a program interposed between the network and your machine. It is a *set of tables consulted at five particular moments* of a journey the packet was going to make regardless. Hence the chains bear the names they do: they are named for the moments, not for functions. A rule in the wrong chain accomplishes nothing however well it may be written — it is consulted at a point the packet does not pass. And the ordering of rules within a chain is the whole of the game, the packet being matched against them in sequence until one pronounces upon its fate.

Beside that sits *connection tracking*, which is what makes the firewall stateful. The kernel keeps a table of every connection it has seen, so that a rule may say "permit replies to connections we ourselves began" without your having to describe what a reply looks like. It is also the machinery beneath address translation, translating an address requiring that one remember the translation in order to reverse it on the return journey.

The table is finite. When it fills — because your machine handles an enormous number of short-lived connections, or because somebody is scanning you — the kernel begins dropping packets and logging a remark about the table being full. Everything above stairs perceives random, inexplicable, intermittent connection failures under load, with every application in perfect health. It is a famous and miserable fault. The cure is to know that the table exists, which cannot be known from any vantage above the boundary.

Two further items on the socket side, since they generate a steady traffic in confusion.

When a program listens on a port there are two queues, not one. Connections which have begun the handshake but not completed it wait in the first; connections fully established but not yet accepted by the program wait in the second. Should the second fill — your program being busy and slow to accept — new connections are not, as most people suppose, refused. They are dropped in silence, and the client waits, and retries, and eventually reports a timeout. Whence the operational lesson: a *refused* connection means nothing was listening and is immediate and unambiguous; a *timed-out* connection frequently means something was listening and could not keep pace. The two read almost alike in a log and say entirely different things about your system.

And on the sending side: when your program writes to a socket and the call returns, the data has not been transmitted. It has been copied into a buffer in the kernel, and transmission proceeds as the protocol allows. This is precisely the arrangement we met with files, and it carries the same moral — the acknowledgement you receive is from the staff accepting your letter, not from the recipient reading it.

Now the modern matter, which I have saved deliberately, it being the most interesting thing to have happened to Linux in a decade and its name having doubtless drifted past you in conference talks for years without ever being explained.

In nineteen ninety-two, two researchers at Berkeley wanted a means by which a packet-capture tool might tell the kernel which packets it cared for, without every packet on the wire being copied up to the program to be examined and discarded. Their solution was a tiny virtual machine inside the kernel: the program submits a small filter, written for an imaginary processor with a handful of instructions and no capacity to loop, and the kernel runs it against each packet. Unable to loop, it cannot hang. Possessing no memory access to speak of, it cannot do harm. It is a sandbox so restricted that its safety is evident on inspection.

The idea sat there, useful and unremarked, for twenty-two years. Then, in two thousand and fourteen, it was taken out and rebuilt with a proper register set, a compiler back end, and — crucially — a *verifier*: a piece of kernel code which examines a submitted program before it runs and refuses anything it cannot prove terminates and cannot prove stays within its bounds. It was then compiled to native machine code and attached not merely to packets but to very nearly anything: the entry and exit of any kernel function, any tracepoint, any system call, the scheduler, the memory allocator, the socket layer, and the earliest point of the network driver, before the packet has so much as been given its structure.

The consequence is what I should like you to carry away. It is now possible to run your own code, safely, inside the kernel — without writing a kernel module, without rebooting, and without the power to crash the machine, since the verifier will simply decline to load anything it cannot vouch for.

This has quietly become the answer to three separate problems. For observability, it means you may put questions to a running production kernel that formerly demanded a debug build and an outage: which processes are causing these disk reads, what is the distribution of latency across this one function, what keeps opening that file. The tracing tools that do it are, underneath, small programs compiled and injected on the spot. For networking, it means load balancers and policy engines running at the driver, dropping or redirecting packets before the kernel has done any work on them at all, which is how a modern machine survives a volumetric attack that would otherwise consume every processor it has. And for security, it means system-call policy expressed as a program rather than a list.

Were I obliged to name the single largest shift in what a working engineer can *see*, it would be this. For most of our careers the kernel was opaque in operation: you could read the source, but you could not put a novel question to a running system. Now you can. The family are admitted below stairs after all — provided each errand is submitted in advance and notarised at the door.

## Part Eight — Walls Within the House

We come to containers, and I should like to begin by taking something out of your mind rather than putting something in.

There is no such thing as a container. I mean that precisely. Search the kernel for the concept and you will not find it: no container object, no container system call, no container subsystem. What exists is a set of independent mechanisms, developed separately over some fifteen years for unrelated purposes, which a piece of tooling above stairs assembles into an arrangement we have agreed to call a container. Once the mechanisms are known to you, the whole technology ceases to be magical and becomes, frankly, rather obvious.

The first mechanism is *namespaces*, and the idea runs thus. The kernel maintains a number of global registers of names: the process numbers, the mount table, the network interfaces and their addresses, the machine's own hostname, the identifiers of users. A namespace is a second copy of one such register. A process holds a pointer to the copy it is using, and when it puts a question to the kernel, it is answered out of its own copy.

So a process in a fresh process-number namespace sees itself as number one, sees none but its own descendants, and can neither signal nor so much as name anything outside — the things outside having no numbers *in its namespace*, and a thing one cannot name being a thing one cannot touch. A process in a fresh network namespace has its own interfaces, its own routing table, its own firewall rules, and its own notion of which ports are occupied — which is exactly why two containers may both bind the same port without a word of argument, and why connecting them requires the manufacture of a virtual cable: a pair of interfaces where whatever enters one emerges from the other, one end left inside and the other plugged into a bridge without.

A process in a fresh mount namespace has its own filesystem tree, which is the whole of the image business. The tooling assembles a root directory, changes the process's root to it, and from within, that is the world entire. And the layered images everybody uses are a filesystem in their own right: a stack of read-only directories with a writable one atop, where a read is served by the topmost layer possessing the file, and a write to a file from a lower layer first copies it upward. So image layers may be shared among hundreds of containers at no cost, and writing to a large file inside a container is slow the first time and unremarkable thereafter.

The second mechanism we have already met: *control groups*, which supply the limits. How much memory this group may hold, before the killer calls upon it specifically rather than ranging over the whole machine. What share of processor time, or what hard quota — with the throttling trap I described earlier lying in wait. How much disk throughput. How many processes it may create at all, which is the specific defence against a fork bomb.

The third is the dismantling of root. Historically the superuser was one indivisible thing: one held every power or none. That has been divided into some forty distinct *capabilities*, grantable individually. The power to bind a low-numbered port. The power to change a file's ownership. The power to load kernel modules. A container is typically given a modest handful and denied the remainder, which is why a process may be the superuser within a container and yet be quite unable to do anything of interest to the machine.

The fourth is the power to restrict which system calls a process may make at all — and the filters are, pleasingly, written in the same little in-kernel language we discussed a moment ago. The standard container profile forbids several dozen calls that no ordinary program requires and that have historically proved fertile ground for escapes. Applied to a process, the restriction is inherited by everything it starts and cannot be revoked. It is the most direct defence there is, narrowing the attack surface to the door itself: with the bell rope for a given errand cut, the errand cannot be asked for.

And there is a fifth, which most people never touch directly and which is running on your machine regardless. The kernel carries hooks at every point where it makes a security decision, and a module may be attached to them enforcing policy beyond the ordinary permissions — the mandatory access control systems, which determine not merely whether *you* may read a file but whether *this program* may, whoever is running it. When a service is inexplicably denied a file it plainly owns, and the ordinary permissions are plainly in order, this is nearly always the author of the difficulty; and the ritual of disabling it in vexation is one I would gently discourage.

Now assemble them. Create a process. Give it fresh namespaces of every kind, so that it sees its own processes, its own filesystem, its own network. Place it in a control group with limits. Strip its capabilities to a handful. Install a system-call filter. Start your program.

That is a container. It is an ordinary process. It appears in the host's process listing like anything else. It is scheduled by the same scheduler, faults into the same page cache, and — the point that matters most — *runs on the same kernel*. There is no second operating system anywhere in the arrangement. The isolation consists entirely in the kernel answering its questions differently.

Which is exactly why the security boundary is the thing it is. A virtual machine is separated from its host by hardware: it has its own kernel, and to escape it one must defeat the processor's virtualisation. A container is separated from its host by the correctness of a great many kernel code paths. That is a real boundary, defended in depth and improving steadily — but it is a boundary of a different order, and any flaw in the kernel is a flaw on both sides of it, there being only the one kernel. This is not an argument against containers, which are excellent. It is an argument for knowing what one has bought.

And it is likewise why containers start instantly. There is no boot. No firmware, no bootloader, no kernel initialisation, none of the long chain of handovers we traced in the second part. There is a fork, some arrangement of namespaces and control groups, and an exec. Everything you have been told about containers being lightweight follows from that one fact: they are light because there is almost nothing there.

## Two Letters and a Press of the Return Key

Let us gather it up.

You are at a terminal. You type two letters — the command that lists a directory — and press return. I shall trace what follows, and I shall use every part of this episode to do it. Nothing here will be new. That is rather the point.

Your keypress is an electrical event in a keyboard, which reaches a controller, which raises an *interrupt*. The processor abandons what it was doing and assumes the privileged humour. The handler is brief, as we said it must be: it takes the scancode and defers the rest. The deferred work translates the scancode, and the character arrives at the terminal device.

There it meets the line discipline, that active machinery I mentioned. The discipline echoes your letter back so that you may see it, which is a *write* travelling the other way, and holds the line in a buffer, you being in the mode where the kernel gathers a whole line before delivering any of it. Which is why you may correct a typing error before pressing return and the program never learns of the correction: the editing happened below stairs.

Your shell, meanwhile, is asleep. It made a *read* on descriptor zero some time ago, found no complete line, and blocked — surrendering the processor voluntarily, as most tasks do most of the time. It is not running. It is not being polled. It is on a queue attached to that terminal, waiting.

You press return. The discipline sees the line is complete and hands it over, which renders the shell *runnable*. The scheduler observes a newly runnable task, judges its claim the most pressing, and switches to it — restoring its registers, changing its page tables, letting it resume from within a call it made some minutes ago.

The shell parses your two letters. This is no built-in, so an external program is wanted, and the shell does what shells have done for fifty years: it *forks*. The child is created without a byte of memory being copied, both sets of pages marked read-only, the copying deferred against the strong likelihood that it will never be wanted — which, here, it will not, the child being about to replace itself entire. In the window between the fork and what follows, the child could arrange its descriptors; had you asked for a redirect or a pipe, that is where it would have happened.

Then the child *execs*. The kernel walks your search path, resolving each component through the directory-entry cache, almost certainly without troubling a disk, you having run this a thousand times. It finds the inode, sets the permissions against the process's credentials, reads the header, and discovers a dynamically linked program. So it maps the program into a fresh address space, maps the dynamic linker beside it, and jumps to *the linker* — which finds the standard library, maps it in, and resolves the first handful of symbols lazily, leaving the rest as stubs to fetch their own addresses should they ever be called.

Observe what has *not* happened. The program has not been read from disk. It has been mapped, its pages absent; and as execution proceeds, each page it actually touches raises a *fault*, which the kernel settles in silence — finding the page in the page cache, where it has sat since the last occasion you ran this, installing it, and resuming the instruction that failed. The program knows nothing of any of it. From within, memory simply works.

The program now opens the directory. That is a crossing: the bell, the fixed landing site, the examination of permissions, the virtual filesystem layer dispatching to whichever filesystem this happens to be. The directory's contents are in all likelihood in the page cache already; failing that, the request descends to the block layer, is handed to the storage device, and the process *blocks* — surrendering the processor entirely, letting something else run — until the device raises an interrupt to announce that it has finished, whereupon the completion is handled, the process is made runnable, and the scheduler restores it.

It formats its output and writes it to descriptor one. That descriptor addresses the terminal, so the bytes pass outward through the line discipline and emerge on the far side, where a terminal emulator — an ordinary unprivileged program, subject to every last thing we have described — reads them, draws them, and asks the graphics hardware to lay them on glass.

The program exits. Its memory is dismantled, its descriptors closed, and it becomes a *zombie*: a table entry holding an exit status and nothing else. The kernel sends the child-terminated signal to the shell, which has been sitting in a wait call throughout, and which now collects the status, releases the zombie, stores the number where your next enquiry will find it, and prints a fresh prompt.

Elapsed time: a few milliseconds. Crossings between the two worlds: some dozens. Page faults settled: some hundreds. Scheduling decisions: several. And every one of them, on a machine you have used for twenty-five years, has been going on beneath every command you have ever run, thousands of times a second, without once requiring your attention.

That is the house, sir. That is what the staff have been doing while you were upstairs.

## What to Poke At, and a Word in Closing

I promised education and I hope some has been delivered. Let me close with what I should actually do next, were I you, since two hours of listening leaves a residue and it is worth converting into something before it evaporates.

Read your own process. The kernel exposes every process as a directory, and every process may look at itself. Read its memory map — you will find the program, the libraries, the heap, the stack, each with its permissions, and the whole of the fourth part of this episode sitting there in plain text. Read its open descriptors, and note that they are links to the things they denote. Read its limits and its accounting.

Trace something trivial. Run the simplest program you can devise under the system-call tracer and read every line of the output. It will be longer than you expect and nearly all of it will be start-up: the dynamic linker seeking its libraries, probing for their whereabouts, mapping them in. You will watch the entire third part of this episode transpire before your program's first line is reached.

Read your control groups. If you run anything containerised, go and look at the actual files — the memory limit, the processor quota, and above all the statistics recording how many intervals were throttled. Should that last figure be other than zero on a service you had believed healthy, you have found something this evening.

And when something is slow and the reason will not show itself, remember that the kernel may now be *asked*. The tracing tools built upon the in-kernel virtual machine will answer, on a live production system, questions that would have demanded a debug build and an outage a decade ago. That capability is the largest thing to have changed in our working lives, and it remains, so far as I can observe, under-used by roughly an order of magnitude.

As to reading: if you want one book, Michael Kerrisk's *The Linux Programming Interface* is the definitive account of the boundary — the bell rope, and everything one may ask for by pulling it — written with a clarity that puts most of the field to shame. For the performance side, Brendan Gregg's *Systems Performance* is the standard work and will teach you what to measure and why you are measuring it. And should you wish to watch the kernel being argued over as it changes, the reporting at Linux Weekly News has been the field's paper of record for a quarter of a century.

A last word on what this was and was not.

This was a tour, and a tour is an honest form with honest limits. I have given you the shape of eight subsystems and the reasons behind perhaps thirty things you were already doing correctly without knowing why. I have not given you the ability to write kernel code, to reason about the ordering of locks, or to diagnose a memory-ordering fault at three in the morning. Those are real skills, earned by other means — a proper course, worked slowly, with the hard parts done properly rather than gestured at.

Should you want that course, sir, I should be very glad indeed to build it: the scheduler derived rather than described, the memory manager taken apart on the bench, concurrency and the memory model treated honestly, filesystems from the block upward, and the whole of it examined by problems you would actually have to sit down and work. Say the word and I shall draw up a constitution for it.

But for this evening the ambition was narrower, and I hope it has been met. You have lived in this house for twenty-five years. You now know what lies below stairs, who works there, and why the bell rope hangs where it does.

It has been a considerable pleasure, sir.

*— End of the Pilot.*
