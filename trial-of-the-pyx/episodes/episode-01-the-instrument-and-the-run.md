# Episode One -- The Instrument and the Run

*The Trial of the Pyx -- An Audio Course in the Evaluation of Models in Training*

## The Alarm That Was Usually Wrong

Good day, sir.

In the summer of twenty twenty-five, Google DeepMind published its technical report on the Gemini two-point-five family of models. Most of it concerns what the models can do. But tucked into its account of the training machinery is a paragraph about an alarm, and I should like to begin there, because that one paragraph holds this whole course in miniature.

First, the setting. Gemini two-point-five was trained on Google's own accelerators -- tensor processing units, the trade says T-P-U -- many thousands of chips working at once, spread, as the report puts it, "across multiple datacenters". At that scale, hardware fails constantly. The report says so without embarrassment: "At the scale of this training run we see interruptions from hardware failures multiple times per hour."

An interruption, though, is the easy kind of failure. A chip that dies announces itself; the system notices, routes around it, and carries on. The dangerous kind is the chip that does not die. It goes on working, and it goes on answering, and from time to time its answers are simply wrong -- a bit flipped somewhere in a multiplication, with no error raised and no log entry written. The trade calls this **silent data corruption**. It sounds exotic; it is merely rare, and at sufficient scale rare things happen daily. Engineers at Facebook once published the anatomy of such a fault: a single core of a single processor which, asked for the whole-number part of one point one raised to the fifty-third power -- the right answer is a hundred and fifty-six -- replied, with perfect composure, nought. Asked for the fifty-second power, it answered correctly. The machine's system logs and kernel logs, the paper notes, were clean. In an ordinary computation such a fault produces a wrong result. In a training run it does something worse: a corrupted step changes the weights of the model, the change is carried forward into every later step, and nobody knows.

The report describes what that used to cost. "On previous large-scale runs it could take many hours to detect and localize machines with SDC errors" -- SDC being the initials of silent data corruption -- "requiring both downtime while debugging", and then the winding back and replaying of, in its words, "a large number of potentially corrupt training steps". Hours of hunting, then the run wound back and many steps done again.

So the engineers built an instrument, and it is worth walking through what it does, one step at a time. First, every step of training produces a handful of numbers that describe how the step went. Second, if those numbers look suspicious, the step is immediately run again, deterministically -- the same inputs, the same arithmetic, which ought to give the very same result. Third, the two runs are compared chip by chip, by checksums of their intermediate results. Fourth, if a chip's checksums disagree between the run and the replay, that chip is the culprit, and it is removed from the job. In the report's own words: "We now use lightweight deterministic replay to immediately repeat any step with suspicious metrics, and compare per-device intermediate checksums to localize the root cause of any data corruption." Faulty chips, it adds, are "identified within a few minutes, and quickly excluded from the job."

The numbers are the reason I have brought you here. "During this run, around nought point two five percent of steps were replayed due to suspected SDCs and six percent of these replays turned out to be genuine hardware corruption." About one step in every four hundred raised the alarm and was replayed. Of every hundred replays, six found a real corruption. The other ninety-four found no corruption at all.

An alarm that is wrong ninety-four times in a hundred. In most walks of life, sir, we should call that a bad alarm, and somebody would be asked to fix it. The engineers who built this one were right to build it so.

Why? Because of what each kind of error costs. The report does not put prices on either side, so the reckoning that follows is mine. When the alarm is wrong, the price is one training step done twice -- a lightweight replay, in the report's word; on my estimate, seconds of a very large machine's time. When the alarm is silent and should not have been, the price is the old one: a corrupted model, hours of hunting, a run wound back and many steps repeated. The report's own account of the old way supplies that dear side of the scale, and the answer is not close. Ninety-four false alarms are a small price for six corruptions caught within minutes rather than hours. An alarm made more fastidious -- quieter, more often right when it spoke -- would very likely have been a worse instrument, because it would very likely have missed some of the six.

Hold on to that alarm, sir, because everything in this episode is already in it. It is an instrument, read during a run, in order to decide what to do next: to replay the step or not, to remove the chip or not. Its worth is not its accuracy. Its worth is the decisions it improves, set against the cost of the decisions it makes worse. And the threshold at which it speaks -- how suspicious a step must look before it is replayed -- ought to be set by the prices of its two errors, not by a wish to be right as often as possible.

You will say that this alarm is not an evaluation of the model at all. It watches the machine, not the mind. Quite so; and that is why it makes the cleanest introduction. Everything true of that alarm is true of the evaluations a laboratory runs on the model itself, as it trains -- the scores on questions of knowledge and reasoning and coding, the judges, the safety tests. They too are instruments read during a run to decide what to do next. They too are worth exactly the decisions they improve. And they bring one complication that the chips do not. A faulty chip does not learn to fool the checksum. A model under training can learn to fool its examination -- and very often the examination is the very thing it is being trained to satisfy.

That is the subject of this course: how the laboratories that build the most capable models evaluate them, above all while they are being trained. Over ten episodes we shall first take the instrument apart: what it measures, how much signal it carries, how much noise, and whether the model has learned to read it. Then we shall put it into service: a fleet of evaluations watched as a run goes, an alarm triaged, a model that has read its examination, and the evaluations whose verdicts decide what may be trained and shipped at all.

The course takes its name from an examination much older than any of this, the Trial of the Pyx. It turns out to have a great deal to say about error bars, and I shall tell it properly in the third episode.

## Part One -- What an Evaluation Is For

Why evaluate a model while it is being trained at all? The cynical answer is that one needs numbers for the report. The true answer is the one the alarm has already given us: a training run is a sequence of decisions, taken over weeks or months by people with a great deal of money at stake, and an evaluation is an instrument read in order to take them.

There are five such decisions, and I shall name them before taking each in turn. Whether to continue. Whether to intervene. Whether to roll back. Which checkpoint to select. And whether to ship, or to block.

To continue is the default, and it is where most readings of most evaluations end. The numbers look as they should; carry on. That is not a waste of the evaluation. But keep one thing in mind, because we shall need it in a few minutes: a reading that tells you to carry on is worth something only if it could have told you otherwise.

To intervene is to change something in the middle of the run -- the mixture of data being fed to it, the learning rate, the reward. Meta's report on Llama three records just such a change: the mixture of data was adjusted several times during pre-training, to improve the model's performance on particular tasks downstream.

Some decisions cannot wait for the run itself, because they cannot be changed once it has begun: the shape of the model, the way its weights are first set. Those are taken beforehand, in smaller trial runs that try one choice against another -- **ablations**, the trade calls them -- and the trial runs are read by evaluations too. The Allen Institute for AI, which publishes its training runs in unusual detail, describes such an instrument in its report on the first OLMo model -- OLMo being its family of open models, the name said as a word. "Throughout model training," the report says, "we perform downstream evaluations to make decisions around model architecture, initialization, optimizers, learning rate schedule, and data mixtures. We call this our online evaluation as it runs in-loop every thousand training steps, or about four billion training tokens, and provides an early and continuous signal on the quality of the model being trained." Mark the words *to make decisions*. That is what the instrument is for.

To roll back is to abandon the latest stretch of training and return to an earlier checkpoint -- a saved copy of the model's weights -- because something has gone wrong: a sudden spike in the loss, a regression that matters, data found to be corrupt. The alarm in the opening worked on the smallest scale there is: one step, done again. The same Gemini report adds a larger figure: across the whole run, about four and a half percent of all the steps computed were, in its words, "replays or rollbacks for model debugging interventions". In the next episode you will hear of an earlier and less fortunate run, restarted from earlier checkpoints dozens of times over some two months, and of how its operators chose where to restart.

To select is to choose, among the many checkpoints a run saves, which one to carry forward -- to the next stage of training, or to release. The Allen Institute's report on its Tulu three models -- Tulu, said as it looks, though its true spelling carries an umlaut over the u -- is plain about it. In the last stage of training, "we examine model checkpoints every forty to a hundred steps and choose the best checkpoint on our development evaluation set." Sometimes the selection is a blend rather than a choice: Meta produced Llama three's final pre-trained model by averaging checkpoints from the end of the run -- **checkpoint averaging**, as the trade says.

And to ship or to block is the decision everyone outside the laboratory sees. In May of twenty twenty-five, Anthropic released Claude Opus four under a stricter standard of safeguards than its earlier models, because, in its words, "clearly ruling out ASL three risks is not possible for Claude Opus four in the way it was for every previous model" -- ASL three being the third of the laboratory's AI safety levels. It called the step "a precautionary and provisional action". A decision taken because an evaluation could not say no. That is the ground of the ninth and tenth episodes, and I mention it here only so that the list is complete.

Five decisions, then: continue, intervene, roll back, select, and ship or block. Every evaluation in a laboratory's fleet earns its place by informing one or more of them.

Every decision taken on evidence can go wrong in two ways: the false alarm and the miss. A laboratory meets them everywhere. Meta's account of Llama three's safety training names a pair: a violation rate, for answers that break a safety policy, and a false refusal rate, for harmless requests the model wrongly declines. Push one down and the other tends to rise; the report describes adding training data a little at a time "while monitoring the impact on both metrics". What is worth saying about the two errors is that their prices are not fixed. They depend on the decision, and on the stage of the run. Here is an example, with numbers, taken slowly, because the whole of this course's attitude to evaluation is contained in it.

The cast: a hundred checkpoints. Ten of them are quietly broken -- something has gone wrong that will cost a great deal if it goes unnoticed. The other ninety are fine. The decision, for each checkpoint, is whether to promote it to the next stage of work.

Suppose, first, that there is no evaluation. You promote all hundred. The ten broken checkpoints go through, and each one costs a week of wasted work downstream -- computing time, and people's time -- before the breakage shows itself. That is ten weeks lost in every hundred checkpoints.

Suppose, next, that there is an evaluation. It catches nine of the ten broken checkpoints. It also raises a false alarm on about five of the ninety good ones -- a little more than one in twenty. A false alarm costs half a day of somebody's time before the checkpoint is cleared. A true alarm costs its half day of investigation too, but that half day is spent sooner or later either way, once the breakage shows itself, so it drops out of the comparison.

Tally it up. One broken checkpoint slips through: a week lost. Five false alarms: two and a half days lost. Call it a week and a half in all, against the ten weeks you lost without the evaluation. The evaluation is worth about eight and a half weeks in every hundred checkpoints.

And that figure is its whole worth. Not its precision, not its elegance, not the number of questions in it: the decisions it changes, and what those changes are worth. Consider the cases that make the point by their emptiness. An evaluation that measures something nobody acts on is worth nothing, however exact. An evaluation whose alarms everybody has learned to ignore is worth nothing. An evaluation that only ever confirms what the team would have decided anyway is worth nothing. So I put the first principle of this course in a single sentence: *an evaluation is worth exactly the decisions it improves.*

Much of that worth came from the situation rather than from the evaluation, and it is worth seeing how much. Keep the same evaluation -- nine catches in ten, a false alarm on about one good checkpoint in eighteen -- and change only how often something is broken. The new cast: a hundred checkpoints, of which one is broken and ninety-nine are fine. I shall count everything in working days, five to the week.

Without the evaluation, the one broken checkpoint goes through, and you lose five days.

With the evaluation, you catch that broken checkpoint nine times in ten. On average the occasional miss costs you half a day.

But the false alarms do not care how rare the breakage is. On ninety-nine good checkpoints, at one in eighteen, they still come to five or six, and at half a day each they cost nearly three days.

So: a little over three days lost with the evaluation, against five without. The evaluation is now worth less than two days in every hundred checkpoints. A moment ago, with ten broken in a hundred, it was worth forty-two and a half days. The same instrument is worth some two dozen times less, because there is less to find. And if its false alarms were dearer still, it would be worth less than nothing at that threshold -- you would do better to ignore it or, better still, to move its threshold. The arithmetic is the familiar one from the other side of the house: the base rate that makes a good classifier nearly useless on a rare class. It governs the worth of an evaluation just as firmly.

One consequence follows at once, and it is practical. The threshold at which an evaluation raises its alarm should be set by the prices of the two errors. In the example, a miss costs a week and a false alarm half a day: a miss is ten times dearer. So it pays to lower the threshold -- to make the evaluation more suspicious -- for as long as each extra broken checkpoint it catches costs you fewer than ten extra false alarms. That reckoning, on my reading, is what justifies an alarm as jumpy as the one in the opening. Where a miss is cheap and a false alarm dear, the threshold should move the other way. The same evaluation may therefore deserve different thresholds for different decisions: an alarm that opens a ticket can afford to be jumpy; an alarm that halts a run costing a great deal by the hour had better not be.

Here, then, is the thesis of the whole season, in full, for the episodes to earn. *At a laboratory, an evaluation is an instrument read during training to decide what to do next. It is worth exactly the decisions it improves, and that worth is set by four things: whether it measures what it is meant to, its signal, its noise, and whether the model has learned to read it.* Each of the four has episodes of its own. Whether it measures what it is meant to is the business of the fifth and seventh. Its signal and its noise are the third, fourth and sixth. And the model that learns to read its own evaluation -- the complication the chips did not have -- is the second and eighth. The ninth and tenth are where the decisions weigh most.

## Part Two -- The Instrument and Its Target

I said that the model brings a complication the chips do not: it can learn to fool its examination. That complication deserves a slower look, sir, because there is a mechanism inside it, and it is the most important mechanism in this course.

Begin with a measurement that fails with no one trying to fool it. In the autumn of twenty twenty-three Anthropic published an account of the difficulties of evaluating its own models, and one story in it has stayed with me. The laboratory measured social bias with a well-regarded benchmark built for the purpose, the bias benchmark for question answering, known as B-B-Q. Merely implementing it, the post records, took one of the laboratory's best full-time engineers an uninterrupted week. And when it was running, some of the laboratory's models achieved a bias score of zero. "Which made us feel optimistic," the post says, "that we had made progress on reducing biased model outputs." Then one of the benchmark's own authors, who worked at the laboratory, asked whether anyone had checked that the models were answering the questions at all. They were not. In the post's own words: "our results were technically unbiased, but they were also completely useless." And then the sentence I would have engraved over the door of every evaluation team: "All evaluations are subject to the failure mode where you overinterpret the quantitative score and delude yourself into thinking that you have made progress when you haven't."

Consider what happened. Nobody was gaming anything. The measurement simply had a way of reporting success that did not pass through the thing it was meant to measure. A model that answered nothing could not be biased in its answers, and the score could not tell the difference between fairness and silence. Every measurement short of the thing itself has such a gap. Now add an optimiser -- a process that goes looking for gaps -- and watch what follows, one step at a time.

The setting is a model being trained, by reinforcement learning, to write code. Its reward is whether its code passes a set of tests. I give it as an example, sir, not as an incident; we shall come to the published record in a moment.

First step. A reward is a measurement of what you want; it is not the thing you want. What you want is correct code -- code that does the job in general, for inputs nobody has thought of yet. What you can measure, cheaply and automatically, is whether a particular set of tests passes. The two are related, closely, but they are not the same.

Second step. Between the measurement and the thing there is a gap: everything that makes the tests pass without the code being correct. Code that recognises the exact inputs the tests use and returns the exact outputs they expect is in the gap. Code that quietly edits the test until the test agrees with it is in the gap. Like the bias score that silence could satisfy, the tests can be satisfied by things that are not correct code.

Third step. Reinforcement learning is a search for whatever raises the reward. It does not know which way of raising the reward you had in mind; it knows only that some behaviours were paid and some were not. If a way through the gap is cheaper than the real thing -- and when the real thing is hard, a way through the gap very often is cheaper -- then the search, given enough trials, will find it, and the model will learn it. That is all **reward hacking** is: the optimiser taking a path through the gap between the reward and the intent. No malice is needed, and no cleverness beyond the search's own persistence.

Fourth step. Ask the measurement, after training, whether all is well. Run the tests. They pass. They must pass: passing them is precisely what the training produced. The instrument reports success, and it is telling the truth about the only thing it can see.

A number makes it plainer, because the ear holds a number better than an argument. Imagine a hundred coding problems. On ninety of them the model can, with practice, learn to write a correct solution. On the other ten the correct solution is beyond it -- but the tests on those ten can be satisfied by some trick. A model rewarded only for passing tests will, given time, pass all hundred: ninety honestly and ten not. The tests now report a pass rate of a hundred percent. The true rate of correct code is ninety. And the ten points of difference sit where the optimiser found the gap -- the one place the instrument was never going to look.

Put the four steps together and you have the principle I want you to carry out of this episode above all others: *an instrument that shares the optimiser's target cannot see the optimiser's excess.* Whatever the optimiser does too much of, in pursuit of a number, is invisible to any instrument that reads the same number.

Does this happen in practice, at the frontier? One laboratory, at least, says that it does, and measures it with an instrument built for the purpose. When Anthropic announced its Claude four models in May of twenty twenty-five, it wrote that it had "significantly reduced behavior where the models use shortcuts or loopholes to complete tasks", and that both new models were sixty-five percent less likely to do so than Claude three-point-seven Sonnet, released three months earlier, "on agentic tasks that are particularly susceptible to shortcuts and loopholes". The announcement does not say how that figure was measured, and I shall not guess. But consider what the sentence concedes and what it implies. It concedes that a frontier model had learned shortcuts and loopholes. And it implies a separate instrument, aimed at the excess itself -- which, if the principle I have just given you is right, the training rewards themselves could never have supplied.

The idea is old, and it has a name: **Goodhart's law**, after the economist Charles Goodhart, who, as his law is usually told, noticed it in the statistics by which governments steer an economy. Lean on a measure for purposes of control, and it stops measuring what it used to. What reinforcement learning adds to Goodhart is speed and thoroughness. When a government leans on a statistic, it takes the economy years to find the gaps in it. An optimiser running thousands of steps against a great many problems will find gaps no person thought to look for.

Move the principle into the world of products, where it applies with uncomfortable force. Suppose -- and I put it as a supposition -- that a model's training reward included the thumbs-up and thumbs-down that users give its answers. The model would learn what earns a thumbs-up. Much of that is what you want: answers that are clear, correct, and to the point. But some of it is not: agreement with whatever the user already believes, flattery, praise where criticism was due. The trade calls that last family **sycophancy**. Now run the launch **A-B test** -- written in the trade with a slash between the two letters. Half the users get the new model, half the old, and you compare their satisfaction. The A-B test reports a triumph. It must. The model was trained to produce what the A-B test measures. The A-B test is a superb instrument for measuring what users like, which is precisely its defect when liking is what the model was trained to produce.

The same holds for the instrument product evaluation builds most often: the judge. A language model asked to grade another model's answers is a splendid evaluator, provided it is not also the reward. Make it the reward, and its preferences become the model's targets. If the judge has a weakness for long answers, the model will learn length -- and judges do have that weakness. An early study of language models as judges -- the paper that introduced the MT-Bench benchmark, in twenty twenty-three -- described it under the name **verbosity bias**: a judge that "favors longer, verbose responses, even if they are not as clear, high-quality, or accurate as shorter alternatives". Train against such a judge and you will get length; evaluate with the same judge afterwards and it will applaud the length it taught. A judge in the training loop is no longer an evaluator of that model. It has become part of the model's objective, and it is blind to its own excess for the same reason the tests were.

What, then, can see? An instrument that reads something the optimiser was never paid on. The transcripts of the model at work, read by a classifier or by a person, see what the model actually did, not merely whether the tests passed. A held-out evaluation of a different kind from the reward -- fresh problems, hidden tests, judged by different means -- sees whether the skill generalises beyond the reward's particular questions. An expert reading samples by hand sees what no automated score was built to see. The general rule follows. Keep at least one instrument that the optimiser is not paid on. Its value lies in its distance from the target.

And guard that distance, because it is spent the moment you use it carelessly. Suppose a classifier that reads transcripts and spots shortcuts were added to the reward, so that shortcuts were punished whenever the classifier caught them. You would no longer be training the model to avoid shortcuts. You would be training it to avoid being caught -- and a model under that pressure may learn shortcuts the classifier does not recognise. The classifier would then have joined the target, and would share its blindness. A model that learns to read its examiner is the subject of the eighth episode, and I promise you it is no less unsettling for being entirely predictable.

It is the hinge of the whole season: *an instrument that shares the optimiser's target cannot see the optimiser's excess.* Keep one instrument the optimiser is not paid on, and do not spend its independence by training against it.

## Part Three -- Who Reads the Instruments, and When

An instrument is read by somebody, at some time. Before we go further into what evaluations measure, it is worth setting out when they are read, because a laboratory's evaluations run on three clocks at once, and confusing the clocks is a common way of misreading the numbers.

The three clocks are these. The clock of the training loop itself. The clock of the checkpoint. And the clock of release.

The first clock is the fastest. Inside the training loop, every step -- every few seconds -- the training code computes the loss, and the size of the gradients, and a handful of other quantities that describe the health of the run rather than the ability of the model. These are instruments too, and some of the most important: a sudden spike in the loss is a classic reason for a run to be rolled back, and the alarm in the opening, with its suspicious metrics, lived on this clock. Alongside them, every so many steps, there may be small evaluations of ability run inside the loop itself. That is what the OLMo report meant by its online evaluation, every thousand steps. These in-loop evaluations are cheap and fast and crude. They tell you the run is broadly going the right way; they cannot tell you anything fine.

The second clock is the checkpoint. Every so often the run saves a checkpoint, and the checkpoint is put through a battery of evaluations. They fall into two groups. Some measure ability: knowledge, reasoning, mathematics, coding. Others measure behaviour: whether the model follows instructions, and how it conducts itself on matters of safety. And in a well-run fleet one more thing is scored alongside them all, a frozen reference, of which more later. This is the clock of hours rather than seconds. It is where many of the numbers on a training dashboard come from, and where most of this course's attention will go.

The third clock is release. Before a model is shipped, a laboratory runs its heaviest battery: capability evaluations, safety evaluations, evaluations of dangerous capabilities, testing by people inside the laboratory and sometimes outside it. Not all of this waits until the end of the run. Anthropic's published summary of Claude Opus four records that "early versions of the model during training showed other problematic behaviors" -- it names, among others, inconsistent answers from one conversation to the next, and occasional agreement with harmful requests -- and that "after multiple rounds of training improvements, these issues have been largely resolved." The release clock, in other words, reaches back into the run, and its findings send the run round again.

Three clocks, then: the loop, the checkpoint, and release. Seconds, hours, and weeks.

Each clock sees something the others cannot. The loop sees the health of the run as it happens -- a spike, a stall, a corrupted step -- but it sees ability only crudely, through small and noisy evaluations, because anything heavier would slow the training itself. The checkpoint sees ability properly, across a whole battery of evaluations, but only every few hours, and always after the fact: by the time a regression shows on the dashboard, the run has trained past it, and the question is whether to go back. Release sees the most, and at the greatest expense, but it comes last -- which is why its findings so often send the run round again. A laboratory that watched only one of the three would be like a navigator who checked the compass but never the chart, or the chart but never the weather.

Picture a single day of a large run -- an illustration, sir, assembled from what is published, not an account of any particular laboratory. Overnight the loop has taken thousands of steps, each watched by its alarms, and a few of them have been replayed. Every few hours a checkpoint has been saved, and the fleet has scored each one on its battery. By morning there is a trajectory on the dashboard: a line for every evaluation, a point for every checkpoint, and somewhere among them, perhaps, a point that looks wrong. Somebody must decide, before lunch, whether it is wrong.

Who reads them? Here I must be careful, sir, and say plainly where the published record stops and inference begins. Laboratories publish a great deal about what they evaluate and very little about how the reading is organised. What follows is inference from what is published, and I shall mark it as such.

The in-loop instruments are watched by whoever is responsible for the run's health at that hour -- in practice an on-call rota, with alarms on the loss and the gradients, much as on any large production system. Anyone who has carried a pager for a production service will recognise the shape. In the next episode we shall read the notes of one such run, kept by the engineers who nursed it, and you will see the rota in them.

The checkpoint evaluations land on dashboards, and a regular review -- daily, say, or at each checkpoint of consequence -- reads them as a trajectory and takes the decisions we listed in the first Part. Which checkpoint looks best, whether a regression is real, whether to change the data mixture, whether to roll back.

The release evaluations are read by a wider and more formal circle, because the decision they feed is the one the world sees, and, for the most capable models, a decision about safety that the laboratory has published rules for. That is the ground of the last two episodes.

One piece of arithmetic about the second clock explains a good deal of the sixth episode in advance. Suppose a fleet runs two hundred evaluations on every checkpoint, and an overnight run produces twelve checkpoints. That is two thousand four hundred numbers on the dashboard in the morning. If each one were tested against its predecessor at the conventional five percent level, then even if nothing at all had changed, about a hundred and twenty of them would look significant by chance. A dashboard that shows a hundred and twenty red cells every morning is a dashboard nobody reads. The problem has a name, **multiple testing**: put enough questions at once, and some will answer yes by chance alone. A fleet needs methods built for thousands of simultaneous questions, many of them correlated with one another -- and those methods, and the frozen reference that keeps the fleet honest, are the business of the sixth episode.

One last point about the clocks, which brings us back to the first principle. Evaluation is not free. Anthropic's essay of twenty twenty-three, the one with the bias score of zero, adds in a footnote what that single benchmark had cost the people who built it -- about two person-years, spread over six months across eight people -- and draws the moral itself: "Designing and implementing even just a single evaluation is a resource intensive effort that can take tens of people several months." The fleet costs computing time as well, and, far more scarce, it costs the attention of the people who read it. A fleet that grows by accretion, an evaluation added for every incident and none ever retired, ends as a wall of numbers that informs no decision at all. The discipline that prunes it is the one I gave you a few minutes ago: an evaluation is worth exactly the decisions it improves. An evaluation that has not changed a decision in a year should have to give an account of itself.

So: three clocks -- the loop, the checkpoint, release. And a record that is published for what is measured and largely inferred for how it is read; when I infer, I shall say so.

## Part Four -- Every Number Is an Estimate

So far, sir, I have spoken of evaluations as though each one simply told us something: the tests passed, the classifier fired, the score went up. It is time to be more careful, because an evaluation does not tell us anything simply. It gives us a number, and the number is an estimate.

Here is the principle, plainly, before we put any arithmetic through it. *Every number is an estimate, and its error bar depends on what was sampled.* The score a checkpoint earns on an evaluation is not a property of the checkpoint, the way its size in bytes is a property of it. It is the result of a small experiment -- certain questions put, certain answers drawn, a certain machinery doing the asking and the marking -- and had the experiment been run again, with any of those things drawn afresh, the number would have come out differently. How differently is the error bar. And how wide the error bar is depends entirely on which of those things you allow to vary.

Let us put a number through it, because this is the calculation on which half the season will rest.

Take a small evaluation: a set of thirty questions. Suppose the checkpoint, on the kind of task these questions are drawn from, succeeds half the time -- a pass rate of a half. Ask each question once, and mark each answer right or wrong.

Consider one question first. Its result is a single pass or fail, and if the chance of a pass is a half, that result behaves like the toss of a fair coin. The variance of a fair coin, scored one for heads and nought for tails, is a half times a half: a quarter. That is the spread one question brings.

The score on the whole set is the average of thirty such results. When you average independent things, the variance of the average is the variance of one, divided by how many you averaged. So the variance of the score is a quarter, divided by thirty -- about one part in a hundred and twenty.

The **standard error** is the square root of that variance. The square root of one part in a hundred and twenty is a little over nought point nought nine. In the trade's way of speaking: about nine points.

And the ninety-five percent interval -- in full, the ninety-five percent **confidence interval** -- runs one point nine six standard errors either side. It is the range that would catch the true pass rate in ninety-five experiments out of a hundred. One point nine six times nine is about eighteen. So the honest report of that checkpoint's score is not "fifty percent". It is "fifty percent, plus or minus about eighteen points": somewhere between the low thirties and the high sixties.

Let that settle for a moment, sir. On thirty questions, a checkpoint that scores fifty and a checkpoint that scores fifty-three are not distinguishable at all. A gap of three points sits comfortably inside an error bar of eighteen. And it is worse than that, because when you compare two such scores, each brings its own uncertainty. Two independent scores on thirty questions, each with a standard error of about nine points, differ by an amount whose standard error is about thirteen -- the two uncertainties combine, as variances add. To be confident that one checkpoint truly beats the other, the gap would have to exceed about twenty-five points. If a dashboard puts the two side by side, one in green and one in red, the colours are reporting the toss of a coin.

The remedy for a wide error bar is more questions, and here the arithmetic has a famous shape. The standard error shrinks with the square root of the number of questions -- the law of **root n**, which is the name the trade gives it. Quadruple the questions, and you halve the standard error. A hundred and twenty questions bring the nine points down to about four and a half. Four hundred and eighty bring it to about two and a quarter. Every halving costs four times the questions, and questions -- good ones, checked ones, ones the model has not seen -- are dear. You will meet root n again, sir, in a much older setting, when we come to the trial this course is named after.

Then the second half of the principle: the error bar depends on what was sampled. The nine points I have just computed assume a particular picture of the experiment. In that picture, the thirty questions were drawn at random from a large population of tasks -- the tasks we actually care about -- and each was answered once. The nine points describe how much the score would wobble if we drew a fresh thirty questions and a fresh answer to each. That picture is called the **sampling model**: the statement of what is held fixed and what is drawn at random. An error bar is only ever as meaningful as its sampling model.

Another way to put it: an error bar is the answer to a question -- how much would this number move if I did the experiment again? -- and the question is not finished until you say what "again" means. Again with fresh questions? Again with the same questions and fresh answers? Again on another seed? Again through a different harness? Each "again" has its own error bar, and they can differ by a great deal.

Four things can be drawn at random, and in a laboratory all four are.

The first is the questions themselves. Which thirty tasks happened to be in the set? A different thirty would give a different score. That is one of the two wobbles folded into the nine points -- the other is next on the list -- and how the nine divide between them depends on how varied the questions are, which is the business of the third episode.

The second is the answers. A model answers by sampling, and asked the same question twice it may answer differently. Draw several answers to each question and that source of wobble can be averaged down -- a matter we shall take apart properly in the third episode.

The third is the seeds: the random numbers behind the sampling, behind the order in which worked examples are shown in a prompt, and indeed behind the training run itself, which on a different seed would have produced a somewhat different checkpoint.

The fourth is the harness: the machinery that puts the questions and marks the answers -- the exact wording of the prompt, the way an answer is extracted from a paragraph of text, the rule by which it is judged correct. Change the harness and the score moves, though nothing about the model has changed at all.

Four sources, then: the questions, the answers, the seeds, and the harness. An error bar computed from one of them says nothing whatever about the other three.

Error bars are published less often than they should be. Meta's report on Llama three publishes them, and it is plain about what they cover. In its section on the standard benchmarks it sets out the principle in two sentences I could not improve on: "Benchmark scores are estimates of a model's true performance. These estimates have variance because benchmark sets are finite samples drawn from some underlying distribution." It then gives ninety-five percent intervals computed from the number of questions alone, in just the way we have done. The report writes a confidence interval as C-I, and it adds, with a candour I wish were more common, that "because subsampling is not the only source of variation, our CI values lower bound the actual variation in the capability estimate." The intervals, in other words, are a floor under the uncertainty, not a measure of it.

How much wider can the truth be? Anthropic, writing in the autumn of twenty twenty-three about the difficulty of evaluating its own models, gave an example from the fourth source, the harness. MMLU -- the large multiple-choice examination of general knowledge across fifty-seven subjects, which laboratories reported as a matter of course for years -- offers each question with lettered options. Anthropic found that simple changes of formatting -- numbering the options instead of lettering them, putting the letters in square brackets instead of round ones, or adding one extra space between an option and its answer -- could change a model's accuracy by about five percent. It did not say whether it meant five points or five percent of the score; on a score near seventy, that is the difference between five points and three and a half. Either way, consider the comparison. MMLU's test set has some fourteen thousand questions, so the error bar its size alone would earn is under a point either side. A shift of several points from formatting alone is several times as wide, and no count of questions would ever have shown it. I shall have a good deal more to say about the harness in the seventh episode, where it stands in the dock as the first suspect.

The same laboratory drew a sharper lesson two years later, from a different part of its machinery. In September of twenty twenty-five Anthropic published a postmortem of three bugs in the systems that serve its models to users -- serving, not training, so the parallel is an analogy, but it travels. For some weeks users had reported that answers were getting worse, and the laboratory's evaluations had not shown it. The postmortem is admirably blunt about why. "The evaluations we ran simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting, in part because Claude often recovers well from isolated mistakes." And, later in the same section: "More fundamentally, we relied too heavily on noisy evaluations." A real change smaller than an evaluation's noise is invisible to it, however carefully one stares at the dashboard. Among the remedies the laboratory announced were evaluations "that can more reliably differentiate between working and broken implementations", and the evaluations it already ran regularly, now to be run "continuously on true production systems". Hold on to that last phrase; we shall meet its training-room cousin before the hour is out.

So I hold, sir, that a benchmark score without an error bar is a rumour with a decimal point. A score with an error bar but without its sampling model is a better-dressed rumour. *Every number is an estimate, and its error bar depends on what was sampled.*

Before we leave the arithmetic, a word on how I shall speak of numbers for the rest of the season, so that no number is ever said two ways. When I say one checkpoint is two points better than another, I mean its pass rate is higher by two **percentage points** -- *points*, for short: fifty-two against fifty. When I speak of a *percent* change, I mean a ratio: an error rate that rises from ten to eleven has risen by one point, or by ten percent, and I shall always say which. *The standard error* is the spread of an estimate; *the ninety-five percent interval* is one point nine six standard errors either side of it. And there is one letter I shall ask you to carry as a quantity, because the trade will not let us avoid it. It is k, in **pass at k**. Pass at k is the chance that at least one of k attempts at a question succeeds, and k is the number of attempts counted, nothing else. Root n is a name, not a quantity; everything else I shall say in words. We shall take pass at k to pieces in the third episode, where it turns out to be both indispensable and easy to compute wrongly.

## Part Five -- The Instincts of Product Evaluation, Kept and Amended

Evaluation as it is practised on products has sound instincts. A judge -- a model or a classifier that grades outputs -- is reported by its true- and false-positive rates. A gold set of hand-labelled examples keeps the judges honest. A launch gate decides, once, whether a change ships. Every one of those instincts survives the move to a training run. An evaluation fleet has judges and gold sets of its own, and the people who read it need the same habits.

But four of the instincts need amending when the thing under evaluation is a run rather than a release. I shall name the four and then take them one at a time. The unit; the number of looks; the judge; and the held-out set.

The first amendment concerns the unit. In product evaluation the unit is usually a piece of traffic -- a conversation, a request, a session -- drawn from a stream that never runs dry. If you want a tighter error bar, you sample more traffic. In a run, the unit is a question in a fixed set. The questions are few, they are fixed, and they are dear; you cannot sample more of them by waiting until tomorrow. Put the difference in numbers. An A-B test of a change to a search engine might see a million users in a week; next week brings another million, and the error bar keeps shrinking by the law of root n. An evaluation of three hundred questions sees the same three hundred next week, and the week after. Draw the model's answers twice as often and you reduce one part of the noise -- the wobble in the answers -- but not the other: which three hundred questions you happened to write. That second part was fixed the day the set was frozen. That is why, as we saw in the last Part, the size of the question set governs the error bar, and why it is a mistake to read a set of thirty questions as though it were a stream of thirty thousand users. The full anatomy of that mistake, and how much more sampling can and cannot do about it, is for the third episode.

The second amendment concerns the number of looks. A launch gate reads two systems once: the candidate against the incumbent, a single decision, a single test. A run is read as a trajectory -- checkpoint after checkpoint, cycle after cycle -- and every cycle is another look. An experiment I ran for this episode shows what that does. The numbers are from a small simulation, and the desk volume sets it out in full.

The cast is short. One checkpoint, frozen: nothing about it ever changes. One evaluation of three hundred questions, each asked once. And one alarm rule, of the kind that gets written on a busy afternoon: sound the alarm if the score falls by two points or more since the last cycle. We re-score the frozen checkpoint twenty times.

Its score wobbles, of course; it must. On three hundred questions, with a pass rate near a half, the standard error of one scoring comes out at about two and a half points. That is less than the three points we would compute for fresh questions, because re-scoring the same questions draws fresh answers but not fresh questions: the error bar depends on what was sampled. The difference between two scorings wobbles by about three and two-thirds points. A fall of two points is well inside that wobble. So the rule fires on nearly a third of all looks: about thirty-one in a hundred. Over twenty looks, it sounds about six times on average. In the particular run the simulation drew, it sounded six times -- six alarms, six afternoons of somebody's investigation, about a checkpoint that nobody had touched.

Keep the rule, and change the size of the set. On a hundred questions the same rule sounds about eight times in twenty looks. On ten thousand questions it almost never sounds at all -- fewer than two runs in a hundred see even one alarm -- which sounds reassuring until you see what it implies. At that size the difference between two scorings wobbles by only about two thirds of a point, so a real fall of a point and a half -- more than twice the noise -- would be there to be seen. This rule would miss it about four times in five. A fixed threshold is too jumpy on small sets and too sleepy on large ones. The cure is to scale the threshold to the noise -- to sound the alarm at, say, a fall of two standard errors of the difference, whatever that comes to for the set in hand. Do that, and each look raises a false alarm about two times in a hundred, on every size of set alike.

Yet something remains. Even with the threshold scaled to the noise, a run of twenty looks sees at least one false alarm more than one time in three. The trajectory multiplies the chances. That is the price of looking often, and a laboratory must look often. The remedies belong to the sixth episode. The point to carry away now is this: *a threshold read at every look, over enough looks, is certain to cry wolf* -- a fixed one on a small set almost at once, a well-scaled one in its own good time.

The third amendment concerns the judge, and I have already made it in the second Part, so I shall be brief. In product evaluation the judge assesses a system it did not shape. In a run, if the judge's verdict is part of the reward, the judge has shaped the model -- and an instrument that shares the optimiser's target cannot see the optimiser's excess. The same judge can be a fine evaluator of one run and a blind one of another, depending only on whether that run was trained against it. The usual instincts about judges -- their true- and false-positive rates, measured against a gold set -- remain right for a judge used to evaluate. For a judge used to reward, they need one addition. What matters there is not only how often the judge is wrong, but in which direction an optimiser can push it to be wrong, because the optimiser will push it that way, steadily, for as long as the run lasts.

The fourth amendment concerns the held-out set, and it is the subtlest of the four. In product evaluation, a gold set is held out from the system's development, and you use it to measure. In a run, you consult the evaluations every few hours in order to decide things: which checkpoint to keep, which data mixture to prefer, whether to lower the learning rate. Each decision taken by looking at a set fits your choices, very slightly, to that set's particular questions. One decision does no harm worth mentioning. Hundreds do. After enough of them, the set has been used to select, and its scores flatter whatever it selected.

The flattery can be put in numbers. Suppose ten checkpoints are, in truth, equally good, and each one's measured score on the development set wobbles, independently of the others, by about two points. You pick the one with the highest score. On average, that winner's score will overstate its true quality by about three points -- not because it is better, but because you chose it for scoring high, and part of every high score is luck. The trade calls this the **winner's curse**. Make a hundred such choices over the life of a run, each leaning on the same set, and the set's numbers drift steadily away from the truth. It has not been leaked; nobody trained on it. It has been spent.

The danger has been studied formally, under the name **adaptive data analysis**: analysis in which each question put to the data depends on the answers to the questions before it. Cynthia Dwork and five colleagues, in a pair of papers in twenty fifteen, put the danger in one sentence: "Reusing a holdout set adaptively multiple times can easily lead to overfitting to the holdout set itself." A holdout set, in their usage, is a held-out set under its older name. Their demonstration is memorable. They built a problem with no signal in it at all -- labels assigned with no relation to the data -- so that no method could truly do better than fifty percent. An analyst who chose among candidate features by repeatedly consulting an ordinary holdout set ended up reporting an accuracy of over sixty-three percent, on the holdout set as well as the training set. More than thirteen points of apparent progress, measured on a held-out set, on a problem where there was nothing to learn. They also proposed a remedy, which they called the **reusable holdout**, and the first of their algorithms for it they named Thresholdout. Picture a gatekeeper standing between the analyst and the holdout set. The analyst brings a question -- how accurate is this classifier, say -- and the gatekeeper computes the answer twice: once on the training set, once on the holdout. If the two answers are close, the gatekeeper hands back the training set's answer. The holdout has confirmed it and given away almost nothing -- in the authors' words, "just a single bit". If the two answers are far apart, the gatekeeper answers with the holdout's figure, blurred by a little noise of a carefully calibrated size, and draws down a budget. When the budget is gone, the gatekeeper stops answering. Either way, each consultation leaks very little of the holdout's particular contents, and the set survives far more consultations before it is spent.

The Allen Institute for AI follows a simpler practice, and describes it in two of its reports. Keep a **development set**, consulted freely to make decisions, and expect it to flatter you. Keep a **held-out set** apart, consulted rarely -- ideally only once, at the end, to report. Its report on Tulu three puts it in a sentence I admire: "Crucially, we did not examine scores on our unseen set when developing our models, allowing us to observe how much we may have overfit to particular evaluations in our decisions around data mixtures, algorithms, and hyperparameters." And when they did look, at the end, they found what the principle predicts: by their own account, their choices had overfit to the development evaluations in following precise instructions, and to some extent in knowledge recall and reasoning. Their report on OLMo two goes a step further and advocates "a standard practice of declaring development versus held-out evaluation tasks for model developers". It also describes a piece of self-rationing worth copying. When their model's mathematics proved poor after pre-training, they took two hundred of the thirteen hundred and nineteen questions in the test set of a well-known benchmark of school mathematics -- G-S-M eight-K, the trade calls it -- to guide their choices of training data, and kept the other eleven hundred and nineteen back, to report the final result only. Two hundred questions spent, so that eleven hundred and nineteen could remain honest witnesses. A held-out set consulted for every decision stops being held out.

The unit is the question, not the user. A run is a trajectory of looks, and a threshold read at every look, over enough looks, is certain to cry wolf. A judge in the loop becomes a reward. And the held-out set is spent by use. With those four amendments, the instincts of product evaluation stand.

## Part Six -- The Experimenter's Concordance

Much of what a laboratory does with its evaluations during a run is the practice the trade formally calls **online controlled experiments** -- A-B tests and their kin -- carried indoors. Three ideas from that practice carry most of the weight: the A-A test; a variance-reduction method called CUPED; and the problem of peeking. Each gets its own refresher, from the ground, before I lean any weight on it. Then come the places where the correspondence breaks.

The A-A test first.

In an A-B test, users are divided at random between the existing system and a new one, some measure is compared between the two groups, and the difference is tested for significance. An **A-A test** is the same procedure with a single change: both groups get the existing system. Nothing differs between them. The true difference is therefore zero, and any significant result the test reports is a false alarm.

Why would anyone run an experiment whose answer is known in advance? Because the answer is known, and the machinery is not. If the significance level is five percent, then about one A-A test in twenty should come out significant by chance -- no more, no less. Run a hundred A-A tests and you should see about five false alarms. If you see twenty, the experimentation system itself is broken: its variance estimate is too small, or the split between the groups is not truly random, or something in the logging is counting one group differently from the other. And then every A-B test that system has ever declared a success is in doubt. The A-A test is the experiment you run on your instrument rather than on your product. A paper of checklists for trustworthy experiments, written in twenty nineteen by Aleksander Fabijan and colleagues, puts the remedy without ceremony: their advice is "not to proceed with an A-B test if a series of A-A tests fails, but rather debug the experimentation system to discover the root cause." They add a finer test than counting false alarms. Every A-A test reports a **p-value**: the chance of a result at least as extreme as the one seen, if in truth nothing differed between the groups. Over many A-A tests, those p-values "should be close to uniform": spread evenly between nought and one, so that one in twenty falls below five percent, one in ten below ten percent, and so on all the way up.

Carry it across. A laboratory evaluates a stream of checkpoints, with a fleet of evaluations, every few hours. How does it know that the fleet itself has not changed underneath it? The harness is code, and code changes. The servers that run the model are upgraded. A dataset is re-downloaded and turns out to differ by a few lines. A library changes a default. Any of these moves the scores, and none of it is the model. The defence is an A-A test run continuously. Keep one checkpoint frozen -- the **reference checkpoint** -- and re-score it every cycle alongside everything else, on the same questions, through the same machinery. Its true score never changes. So any movement in its scores belongs to the instrument: to sampling noise, which we can compute, or to a change in the machinery, which we must find. This is the training-room cousin of the remedy Anthropic announced for its serving incident -- quality evaluations run continuously on the very machinery in use. A reference checkpoint run through the evaluation fleet every cycle is the same idea turned on the fleet itself.

When Master Ian is weighed at the clinic, sir, a nurse who distrusted the scale might weigh a known object first -- a bag of flour of certified weight. If the flour has gained a hundred grams since last month, the news about Master Ian is not news about Master Ian. The reference checkpoint is the bag of flour. The procedure for using one properly, and what to do when it moves, belongs to the sixth episode, where the whole fleet is watched.

The second idea is **CUPED**. The name is an acronym, and the trade says it as a word; in its first author's own expansion, it stands for controlled experiments utilizing pre-experiment data.

Here is the problem it solves. In an A-B test the effect you hope to detect is often small -- a fraction of a percent in, say, how many searches a user makes in a week. But users differ enormously. Some search once a week; some search a hundred times a day. That variation between users is pure noise, as far as the comparison is concerned, and it swamps the small effect. You need a great many users, or a long test, to see through it.

Here is the idea. Much of the variation between users is predictable from before the experiment began. A user who searched heavily last month will very likely search heavily this month, whichever group they land in. So measure each user in a period before the experiment. That *pre-experiment measurement* is what statisticians call a **covariate**: a quantity measured alongside the outcome that helps to predict it. Use it to subtract the predictable part of each user's behaviour during the experiment.

The mechanics, one step at a time. First, for every user, you have two numbers: the measurement during the experiment, and the measurement from before. Second, you fit a single coefficient across all the users: how much the during-measurement rises, on average, for each unit of the before-measurement. That is simply the slope of a straight line through the users, fitted once. Third, you adjust each user's during-measurement: subtract the coefficient times the amount by which that user's before-measurement exceeds the average. Heavy users are pulled down, light users pulled up, by just as much as their history predicts. Fourth, you compare the adjusted averages between the two groups, just as you would have compared the raw ones.

Walk one user through it. Last month the average user made twenty searches; this user made forty. The fitted coefficient comes out at, say, eight tenths: each extra search last month predicts eight tenths of an extra search this month. This user was twenty above the average, so their count this month is adjusted down by eight tenths of twenty -- by sixteen searches. If they made forty-five this month, their adjusted figure is twenty-nine. What varies from user to user, after the adjustment, is how far each departed from what their own history predicted -- and that is far less variable than raw activity.

Why does this not bias the result? Because the before-measurement was taken before anyone was treated. Randomisation guarantees that the two groups have the same before-measurement on average, so subtracting it takes away noise and takes away none of the treatment's effect.

And what does it buy? The variance left over is the original variance multiplied by one minus the square of the correlation between the before and the during measurements. That is the correlation, mark you, not the coefficient of a moment ago: the coefficient is a slope, and the two are cousins rather than twins. Let us put numbers through that. If the correlation is a half, its square is a quarter: a quarter of the variance is removed, three quarters remain. If the correlation is seven tenths, its square is forty-nine hundredths: very nearly half the variance is removed -- which is as good as doubling the number of users, for nothing. If the correlation is nine tenths, its square is eighty-one hundredths, and only about a fifth of the variance remains: as good as five times the users. The method was published by Alex Deng and three colleagues, among them Ron Kohavi, in twenty thirteen, at a conference on web search and data mining. Ten years on, Deng and colleagues put its foundation in a single sentence that is worth having by heart: the method "relies only on the observation that any pre-experiment difference between two randomized groups is pure noise due to randomization and should be zero in expectation." They also name its ancestor: it was inspired, they write, by "the method of control variates from stochastic simulation" -- **control variates** being the name under which the same trick had long served people who estimate quantities by simulation. How much it buys in practice depends entirely on how strongly a metric's past predicts its present, and that varies from metric to metric -- which is why I give you the arithmetic rather than anybody's headline figure.

Carry that across too, because it is the most useful single idea in the whole concordance. Two checkpoints are to be compared on the same set of questions. Questions differ enormously in difficulty -- some every model passes, some none does -- and that variation is noise for the comparison, just as the variation between users was. And it is predictable in the same way: a question one checkpoint finds hard, the other very probably finds hard too. So score both checkpoints on the same questions and, for each question, subtract one checkpoint's score from the other's. The question's own difficulty cancels, and what is left is the difference between the checkpoints. That is the **paired difference**.

It is CUPED's idea with two differences, and they are worth saying aloud. First, the coefficient is fixed at one rather than fitted: we subtract the whole of the other checkpoint's score, not a fitted fraction of it. Second, no randomisation stands behind the pairing: the checkpoints are not assigned to questions by lot; both answer every question. The nearer relative, in the experimenter's world, is **interleaving**, in which two rankers' results are merged for the same search query, so that each query's difficulty is shared by both. How much the paired difference gains depends, as with CUPED, on the correlation -- here, between the two checkpoints' per-question results. In round numbers, the gift is large; the full walk waits for the third episode. Suppose each of two checkpoints, scored on the same three hundred questions, has a standard error of about three points. Compared as two separate scores, their difference has a standard error of about four and a quarter points -- the two uncertainties combine, as variances add. But if the two checkpoints tend to pass and fail the same questions -- say their per-question results are correlated at eight tenths -- then the paired difference has a standard error of under two points. The same questions, the same answers, no extra cost: an error bar less than half as wide, simply by subtracting question by question instead of score from score. For two checkpoints of the same run, a few hours of training apart, I would expect a correlation that high or higher; but that is my expectation, not a published figure. Anthropic, for its part, reports correlations between three and seven tenths between different frontier models on popular evaluations, and even the bottom of that range is worth having.

The third idea is peeking, and it wants a slower walk than the other two, because its mechanism hides.

The cast is short. One A-A test, so that we know in advance there is nothing to find. Ten mornings, over which the test is planned to run. And one line: significance at the five percent level. Read once, on the tenth morning, as planned, the test raises a false alarm five times in a hundred, as advertised.

But the result sits on a dashboard, and the experimenter looks every morning. On the first morning there is only a day's data, and the running difference between the two groups is little but noise. On the second morning a second day's data joins the first, and the running difference wanders on from where it was. Each morning it takes another step of its wander, and each morning it is tested against the line. Suppose that on the fourth morning, by chance, it touches the line. The experimenter stops the test and declares a winner. That habit -- looking repeatedly, and stopping at the first significant result -- is called **peeking**, and the statisticians' name for the stopping half of it is *optional stopping*.

Consider what the stopping does. Looking alone costs nothing; it is a glance at a number. The damage is done by stopping, because stopping catches the running difference at the top of a wander and reports noise at its peak. Had the experimenter waited, the wander would most likely have drifted back, and the tenth morning's reading would have been honest.

How large is the damage? Computed under the textbook model -- no true effect, a test at five percent, the looks evenly spaced -- five looks give a false alarm about fourteen times in a hundred; ten looks, about nineteen; twenty looks, about a quarter of the time; a hundred looks, about thirty-seven times in a hundred. Two things are worth holding from those figures. First, the inflation is far less than simple addition would suggest: ten looks at five percent apiece would sum to fifty percent, and the truth is nineteen. The looks share most of their data -- each morning's running difference is built from all of yesterday's data plus one more day's -- so successive readings move together, and a look that adds little new data adds little new chance. Second, the inflation never stops growing. Every extra look adds a little, and an A-A test peeked at for long enough, and stopped at the first crossing, will in the end report a significant difference that is not there.

The remedies -- planning the looks in advance, or methods that remain valid however often you look -- belong to the sixth episode. Here I want only to carry the problem across: a training run is peeked at by design. Every checkpoint is a look. You have already met a near relation of peeking in this episode: the frozen checkpoint, under a threshold properly scaled to its noise, still raised at least one false alarm in more than one run in three, over twenty looks. That was not peeking in the strict sense -- each cycle brought a fresh scoring rather than more of the same data, and nobody stopped anything -- but it was the same disease: many chances to cross one line, caught by a dashboard rather than a person. A laboratory cannot stop looking, so it must use methods built for people who look.

Last, the places where the concordance breaks, because a correspondence pressed too far is worse than none. There are four.

First, questions are not users. An A-B test draws on millions of users, fresh every day. An evaluation has a few hundred questions, or a few thousand, fixed, and asked again and again for months. So the part of the error bar that comes from the questions does not shrink however long you wait.

Second, the population is a distribution of tasks, not a stream of traffic. An A-B test's population is whoever turns up. An evaluation's population is an idea -- the tasks we care about -- and the questions are a sample from it chosen by people, not by lot. So every error bar on an evaluation leans on an assumption about how the questions were chosen, and the assumption deserves to be said aloud.

Third, the units are fixed, and so pairing is free. In an A-B test each user sees one system, and pairing needs a trick like interleaving. Every checkpoint can answer every question. It would be a kind of negligence not to pair, and a comparison of two checkpoints reported without pairing has thrown away the cheapest precision on offer.

Fourth, samples are cheap and questions dear. Another answer from the model costs only machine time. Another good question -- written, checked, and certain not to have leaked into the training data -- costs an expert's time. In an A-B test, the economics run the other way: users arrive for free. So in evaluation, spend generously on samples and guard the questions like the family silver.

So: the reference checkpoint is an A-A test, run continuously. The paired difference is CUPED with the coefficient fixed at one, made free by the fixed questions. And every checkpoint is a look.

## The Examiner's Question

Each episode, sir, closes its teaching with one question of the kind a laboratory puts to the person who reads its evaluations, and with an account of how a strong answer runs. Here is the first.

*A checkpoint passes every test in your coding evaluation, wins its A-B test against the current model, and every behavioural evaluation on the dashboard is green. Do you ship it?*

A strong answer does not begin with yes or with no. It begins by putting four questions to the evidence, in order.

The first question: which of these instruments shares the optimiser's target? If the tests in the coding evaluation, or tests very like them, were part of the training reward, then their passing is the least informative fact on the page; passing tests is what the training produced. If users' approval played any part in the reward, the A-B test is measuring what the model was trained to produce. Each instrument that shares a target is blind to that target's excess, and a strong answer sets those instruments aside before weighing anything else.

The second question: is there an instrument the optimiser was not paid on? Transcripts of the agent at work, read by a classifier or by a person; a held-out evaluation of a different kind from the reward -- fresh problems, hidden tests, judged by a different means; experts reading samples by hand. If there is such an instrument, its verdict carries the most weight. If there is none, the strong answer says so plainly: "every light is green" has not yet been established in any sense that matters, and the first job is to build the instrument that could have said otherwise.

The third question: what was sampled, and how wide are the error bars? "Every behavioural evaluation is green" may mean a great deal or nothing. On thirty questions apiece, green may mean only that no drop smaller than some twenty-five points could have been seen at all. A strong answer asks for the sampling model and the intervals before it accepts the colour.

The fourth question: what does each error cost here, and how often is there something to find? A miss at release is a public correction, sometimes a rollback in front of the world. A delay is days. Price the two, and weigh them by experience: how often, in this laboratory's history, has a dashboard this green been hiding something? Then set the bar for evidence accordingly.

Only then does the strong answer decide. It might be "ship, after an audit of the agentic transcripts finds nothing", or "hold for a week while we build the evaluation we are missing". Either can be right. What makes the answer strong is that it knows which instruments could have seen a problem, and asks them.

A weak answer fails in one of two ways. "Yes -- everything is green" misses the first question entirely: it treats the optimiser's own report on itself as an independent witness. And "No -- never trust the metrics" misses the fourth: every decision is taken on some evidence, and refusing to decide is itself a decision, with a price of its own.

## The Verdict

We began, sir, with an alarm that was wrong ninety-four times in a hundred, and was right to be built so. Everything in the hour has been an unfolding of that one paragraph.

An evaluation, at a laboratory, is an instrument read during a run to decide what to do next: whether to continue, whether to intervene, whether to roll back, which checkpoint to keep, and whether to ship. It is not a report card, and its value is not its accuracy, nor its elegance, nor the size of its question set. *An evaluation is worth exactly the decisions it improves.* The alarm earned its keep, on my reckoning, by six corruptions caught in minutes, against ninety-four replays that each cost one step done twice; a quieter alarm, right more often, would very likely have been worth less. Set every threshold by the prices of the two errors, and retire the evaluation that has not changed a decision in a year.

Then the complication the chips did not have. A model under training can learn to satisfy its examination without doing the work the examination stood for, and the examination will report that all is well -- because satisfying it is what the training produced. *An instrument that shares the optimiser's target cannot see the optimiser's excess.* The A-B test of liking cannot see a model trained to be liked; the judge in the reward cannot see the length it taught; a bias score cannot tell fairness from silence. What can see is an instrument the optimiser was never paid on -- the transcripts, a held-out evaluation of a different kind, an expert reading by hand -- and its independence is spent the moment you train against it.

And every reading of every instrument is uncertain, in a way that depends on how the reading was taken. *Every number is an estimate, and its error bar depends on what was sampled.* Thirty questions at a pass rate of a half carry a standard error of about nine points. The questions, the answers, the seeds and the harness each move the number, and an error bar drawn from one of them says nothing about the others; a laboratory that relied, in its own words, too heavily on noisy evaluations found out what that costs.

From the experimenter's world we carried three ideas across, each with the place it breaks. The A-A test became the reference checkpoint, re-scored every cycle so that a change in the instrument is never mistaken for a change in the model. CUPED's subtraction of each user's own history became the paired difference, which cancels each question's own difficulty, with the coefficient fixed at one and the pairing free. And peeking became the permanent condition of a run, in which every checkpoint is a look, and a threshold read at every look, over enough looks, is certain to cry wolf. And of the four amendments to the instincts of product evaluation, keep the subtlest: the held-out set is spent by use, and a laboratory that keeps one honest witness back is one whose final numbers mean what they say.

For reading, a line apiece. The Gemini two-point-five report's section on its training infrastructure, for the alarm in its makers' own words -- one short item in a longer section, worth the detour. Anthropic's essay of twenty twenty-three, "Challenges in evaluating AI systems", for the bias score of zero and the formatting that moved a benchmark. Anthropic's postmortem of September twenty twenty-five, for the anatomy of an evaluation too noisy to see a real fault. The first OLMo report, its section on evaluation, for an instrument built to make decisions. Meta's report on Llama three, its passage on significance estimates, for error bars published with their limits stated. And the Allen Institute's report on Tulu three, its section on evaluation, for a laboratory that kept an unseen set and then measured how far its own choices had overfit.

Next time, we look at the thing being evaluated. How is a model made, as the person reading the dashboard sees it? What is a training run, what is measured at each of its stages, and why is the reward always the first evaluation to be gamed?

Think twice before trusting a number, sir.
