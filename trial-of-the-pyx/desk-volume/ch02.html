Chapter 2The Remedy

Companion to Episode Two. The statistics of evaluations, not statistics: two numbers for one examination; the trial of the pyx and the Master’s Remedy, multiplied by $n$ where it should have grown by $\sqrt n$; the two parts of an evaluation’s error bar, and why more samples cannot rescue a small set; clustered questions; the paired difference; the third draw, the training run itself; power, and what a set can see; small sets and the interval that collapses; and pass at $k$, the majority vote and pass to the $k$, with their estimators and the two directions in which a careless one errs.

A score is not a measurement until its procedure is stated: the number of questions, the samples drawn per question and their temperature, the rule that combines them, and the interval. Two scores are compared only when the procedures match.

2.2 Part One — The Master’s Remedy

Garcia’s thesis on P. A. MacMahon, Deputy Warden of the Standards at the Board of Trade from 1906, describes the trial of the pyx in his day: sample coins set aside through the year in boxes, a jury of the Goldsmiths’ Company summoned by the Remembrancer, standards of diameter, weight and composition tested by the London Assay Office, and a verdict usually in May. Of the tolerance for a consignment it records: “It was customary to calculate an overall allowable error in the pyx trials by simply summing the products of the number $n$ of each coin by the allowance $r$ for that coin, $\sum nr$; this was known as the ‘Master’s Remedy’. MacMahon pointed out that the square root of the sum of the products of the number of coins and the square of the allowance, $\sqrt{\sum nr^2}$, would make more sense, since any errors would be distributed normally. It is not clear from the surviving documents whether this suggestion was adopted.”

The thesis does not say how the allowance was meant. This volume reads it as a tolerance on the single coin, set at $z$ standard deviations of the coin’s error; under that reading MacMahon’s form holds the consignment to the same confidence as the coin, which fits his reason (“since any errors would be distributed normally”).

Proposition 2.1 (The batch’s tolerance).

Let the errors of $n$ coins of one kind be independent and normal, with mean zero and standard deviation $\sigma$, and let the allowance be the tolerance $r = z\sigma$, which an honest coin exceeds with probability $\alpha = 2(1-\Phi(z))$. The total has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt n$, and the tolerance at the same $z$ is $z\sigma\sqrt n = r\sqrt n$; across kinds $j$, with $n_j$ coins of allowance $r_j = z\sigma_j$, it is $\sqrt{\sum_j n_j r_j^2}$, MacMahon’s form. The Master’s Remedy $\sum_j n_j r_j$ exceeds it by a factor that, for a single kind, is $\sqrt n$; and an honest consignment of one kind uses more than the fraction $1/\sqrt n$ of the Master’s room with probability $\alpha$ only.

Proof.

Variances of independent errors add: the total has variance $n\sigma^2$, normal, and exceeds $z\sigma\sqrt n = r\sqrt n$ in absolute value with probability $\alpha$. Across kinds the variance is $\sum_j n_j\sigma_j^2$, and $z$ times its root is $\sqrt{\sum_j n_j r_j^2}$. For one kind, $nr/(r\sqrt n) = \sqrt n$, and using more than $1/\sqrt n$ of the room $nr$ is the event that the total exceeds $r\sqrt n$.

□
Proposition 2.2 (What the Master’s Remedy hides).

Suppose every coin carries a common bias $b$ besides its independent error, and write $z_{1-\beta}$ for the normal quantile. (a) Under the Master’s Remedy, for any fixed $|b| < r$ the consignment passes with probability tending to one as $n$ grows. (b) Under MacMahon’s remedy $r\sqrt n$, the expected total reaches the tolerance at $|b| = r/\sqrt n$, where the consignment is flagged about half the time; it is flagged with probability at least $1-\beta$ once $|b| \ge (z + z_{1-\beta})\,\sigma/\sqrt n$. At $z = 1.96$ and power $0.8$ that is $2.80\,\sigma/\sqrt n$: $0.280\,\sigma$ for a hundred coins, $0.028\,\sigma$ for ten thousand (lab 2d).

Proof.

The total is $nb + e$, with $e$ normal of standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt n$. (a) The consignment fails only if $|e| > n(r - |b|)$; the right side grows as $n$, the scale of $e$ as $\sqrt n$, so the probability of failing tends to nought. (b) Take $b > 0$; the consignment is flagged when $|nb + e| > z\sigma\sqrt n$. At $nb = z\sigma\sqrt n$, that is $b = r/\sqrt n$, the event $e > 0$ has probability one half, and the other tail adds a negligible amount. At $nb = (z + z_{1-\beta})\sigma\sqrt n$ the event $e > -z_{1-\beta}\,\sigma\sqrt n$ already has probability $1-\beta$.

□

That a Master could have exploited the gap is this volume’s gloss, not the thesis’s. The lesson for an evaluation is the remedy of Chapter 1’s Proposition 1.9: a tolerance must scale with the standard error, not with the size of the batch.

2.3 Part Two — Questions and Answers

Model — Questions from a population, answers from a model

A set of $N$ questions is drawn independently from a population of tasks; question $i$ has pass probability $p_i$, distributed as $F$ with mean $\bar p$, variance $\Var(p)$, and mean within-question variance $\E[p(1-p)]$. Each question is answered $n$ times independently, and the score is $\hat\mu = \frac1N \sum_i \frac1n \sum_{j} y_{ij}$. Governs Proposition 2.3 and Exhibit 2.2; Proposition 2.4 relaxes the independence to clusters.

Proposition 2.3 (The two parts of the error bar).

$\Var(\hat\mu) = \Var(p)/N + \E[p(1-p)]/(Nn)$. The first term, from which questions were asked, does not depend on $n$; as $n \to \infty$ the standard error falls to $\sqrt{\Var(p)/N}$ and no further.

Proof.

By the law of total variance over the draw of the questions,

\[ \Var(\hat\mu) = \Var\bigl(\E[\hat\mu \mid p_1,\dots,p_N]\bigr) + \E\bigl[\Var(\hat\mu \mid p_1,\dots,p_N)\bigr]. \]

The conditional mean is $\frac1N\sum p_i$, of variance $\Var(p)/N$; the conditional variance is $\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)/n$, of expectation $\E[p(1-p)]/(Nn)$.

□

Miller (2024, §3) states the same decomposition — “the variance associated with choosing questions from the super-population, and the mean conditional variance” — and draws the same conclusion: the first part “is a property of the super-population and therefore immutable”, and “Once $\E[\sigma_i^2]/K \ll \Var(x)$, increasing $K$ further will have little effect on the standard error.” He adds two warnings: “computing a pooled standard error across all KN answers will be inconsistent” (Problem 2.7), and “In neither case should the sampling temperature be adjusted for the sake of reducing variance in the scores.” DeepSeek-R1’s report gives the practice and its reason: greedy decoding “results in higher repetition rates and significant variability across different checkpoints”, so it reports pass@1 averaged over $n$ samples, “typically between 4 and 64”, at temperature $0.6$. (Miller writes $K$ for the samples per question; this volume keeps $n$, as Chapter 1 fixed it.)

Clustered questions.  Questions that share a passage, a repository or a translation rise and fall together.

Proposition 2.4 (The design effect).

If the $N$ questions come in clusters of $m$ whose results have common variance $\sigma^2$ and correlation $\rho$ within a cluster and none across clusters, then $\Var(\hat\mu) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\bigl(1 + (m-1)\rho\bigr)$; the clustered standard error is the naive one times $\sqrt{1+(m-1)\rho}$.

Proof.

Each cluster’s sum has variance $m\sigma^2 + m(m-1)\rho\sigma^2$; there are $N/m$ independent clusters, and the mean divides the total variance by $N^2$.

2.4 Part Three — Comparing Two Checkpoints

Proposition 2.5 (The paired difference).

Let checkpoints $A$ and $B$ answer the same $N$ questions, with per-question results of variances $\sigma_A^2$, $\sigma_B^2$ and correlation $\rho$, independent across questions. The mean of the per-question differences has variance $(\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B)/N$; with equal variances, $2\sigma^2(1-\rho)/N$, a standard error $\sqrt{1-\rho}$ times the unpaired one.

Proof.

$\Var(y^A_i - y^B_i) = \sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\Cov(y^A_i, y^B_i)$, and the $N$ differences are independent.

□

At $\rho = 0.3$, $0.5$ and $0.7$ the factor $\sqrt{1-\rho}$ is $0.84$, $0.71$ and $0.55$ (lab 2d). Anthropic’s post: “In practice, we find the correlation of question scores on popular evals between frontier models to be substantial—between 0.3 and 0.7 on a scale of $-1$ to $+1$.” That range is between frontier models; for checkpoints of one run the volume expects more, an expectation and not a published figure. Miller (§4.2): “paired differences represent a ‘free’ reduction in estimator variance when comparing two models. We therefore recommend using the paired version of the standard error estimate wherever practicable.” The pairing is lost for good if only totals are stored; keep per-question results for every checkpoint.

Proposition 2.6 (Two single runs).

Let a run’s score be $\mu_R + s + e$, with $\mu_R$ the recipe’s mean, $s$ a seed effect of variance $\sigma_s^2$, and $e$ the measurement’s error of variance $\sigma_e^2$, all independent. The difference of single runs of recipes $A$ and $B$ has variance $2\sigma_s^2 + \Var(e_A - e_B)$, and no single run identifies $\sigma_s^2$; pairing on shared questions reduces $\Var(e_A - e_B)$ and leaves $2\sigma_s^2$.

Proof.

The seed effects of the two runs are independent, contributing $2\sigma_s^2$; the measurement errors contribute $\Var(e_A - e_B)$, which Proposition 2.5 reduces when the questions are shared. A single run yields one draw of $s + e$, from which $s$ cannot be separated.

□

Madaan et al. on the comparison: the interval “is easily computable from a single training run, whereas the former requires multiple (expensive) training runs with different seeds”; and, for an ablation, “we would want the performance increase to be larger than that due to random seed variance”. Magnusson et al. (DataDecide, AI2, 2025) trained “a suite of 1,050 models using 25 data recipes $\times$ 14 model scales $\times$ 3 random seeds for initialization and data order”; “the standard deviation between runs at the 1B 5$\times$C scale can be as high as 2% points of accuracy for some recipes on most tasks”; “The evaluation must separate pairs of data recipes by an amount greater than combined noise from run-to-run variance of each of the pair’s runs”; and ranking by single runs at 150M parameters predicts the 1B winner in “$\sim$ 80% of comparisons”. A ten-seed standard deviation is known to about $\pm 24\%$, a three-seed one to about $\pm 50\%$ (lab 2e).

2.6 Part Five — What a Set Can See

Proposition 2.7 (Questions needed, and the minimum detectable effect).

For a two-sided test at level $\alpha$ with power $1-\beta$, to detect a difference $\delta$ when one question contributes variance $V$ to the difference ($V = 2\sigma^2$ unpaired, $2\sigma^2(1-\rho)$ paired), the number of questions needed is approximately $N = (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2\, V/\delta^2$, and a set of $N$ questions has minimum detectable effect $(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})\sqrt{V/N}$.

Proof.

Under the normal approximation the test statistic is $\hat\delta/\sqrt{V/N}$; it exceeds $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ with probability $1-\beta$ when $\delta\sqrt{N/V} - z_{1-\alpha/2} = z_{1-\beta}$.

□

At $\alpha = 0.05$ and power $0.8$ the constant $(z_{0.975}+z_{0.8})^2$ is $7.849$ (lab 2d). Miller’s worked example (§5): to detect three points at power $0.8$ “the eval will need to contain at least” $969$ “independent questions”, parameters he calls fictional but reasonable, whence “new evals should contain at least 1,000 questions in order to have good signaling ability.”

Definition 2.8 (The Wilson interval).

For $c$ passes in $N$, with $\hat p = c/N$ and $z = z_{0.975}$, the Wilson interval is $\dfrac{\hat p + z^2/(2N)}{1 + z^2/N} \pm \dfrac{z}{1+z^2/N}\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{N} + \dfrac{z^2}{4N^2}}$, the set of $p$ not rejected by the score test $|\hat p - p| \le z\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$.

2.7 Part Six — Pass at $k$, and Its Cousins

Theorem 2.9 (The unbiased estimator of pass at $k$).

For one problem with pass probability $p$, draw $n \ge k$ answers and let $c$ pass. Then $\E\bigl[1 - \binom{n-c}{k}\big/\binom{n}{k}\bigr] = 1 - (1-p)^k$, and $\E\bigl[\binom{c}{k}\big/\binom{n}{k}\bigr] = p^k$.

Proof.

Given $c$, the ratio $\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ is the probability that $k$ answers chosen without replacement from the $n$ are all failures. Unconditionally, the $n$ answers are independent draws, so a uniformly chosen subset of $k$ of them is itself $k$ independent draws, all failing with probability $(1-p)^k$. The same argument with successes gives $p^k$.

□

Chen et al. (2021, §2.1 and Appendix A): drawing exactly $k$ “can have high variance”; they draw $n = 200$, and the estimator “is unbiased, because it estimates the fail probability $(1-$pass@1$)^k$ as the probability of drawing $k$ failed samples without replacement”; the plug-in $1-(1-\hat p)^k$ “results in a consistent underestimate”, and “The gap doesn’t fully close even when $n > 5k$, and results can seem better with more samples.” Their numerically stable form multiplies $1 - k/j$ for $j$ from $n-c+1$ to $n$.

Proposition 2.10 (Two biases in opposite directions).

(a) For one problem with $0 < p < 1$ and $k \ge 2$, the plug-in $1-(1-c/n)^k$ is biased low, and $(c/n)^k$ is biased high. (b) For a set with pass probabilities $p_i$, the pass at $k$ computed from the mean, $1-(1-\bar p)^k$, is at least the set’s true pass at $k$, $\frac1N\sum_i\bigl(1-(1-p_i)^k\bigr)$; and $\bar p^{\,k}$ is at most the set’s pass to the $k$, $\frac1N\sum_i p_i^k$.

Proof.

For $k \ge 2$, $x \mapsto (1-x)^k$ and $x \mapsto x^k$ are convex on $[0,1]$, so by Jensen’s inequality $\E[(1-c/n)^k] \ge (1-p)^k$ and $\E[(c/n)^k] \ge p^k$, which is (a). For (b), apply the same inequality to the empirical distribution of the $p_i$: $\frac1N\sum(1-p_i)^k \ge (1-\bar p)^k$ and $\frac1N \sum p_i^k \ge \bar p^{\,k}$.

□

Remark 2.11 (The majority vote).

Majority voting over $n$ samples (maj@$n$, cons@$n$) scores the modal answer. As $n$ grows it comes to exceed a single answer’s accuracy on problems where the correct answer is the single most probable one, and to fall below it where a wrong answer is more probable than the right one (Problem 2.11). It is a different quantity, not a better estimate of the same one.

Protocol — Box 2.1. Reporting a score

  1. State the number of questions, and whether and how they cluster.

  2. State the samples per question, the temperature, and the combining rule: one answer, the mean of $n$, the majority of $n$, pass at $k$, pass to the $k$.

  3. Estimate pass at $k$ and pass to the $k$ problem by problem with the unbiased estimators (Theorem 2.9); never through a formula from the mean (Proposition 2.10).

  4. Report an interval suited to the set: clustered where the questions cluster (Proposition 2.4); Wilson or Bayesian below a few hundred questions (Definition 2.8).

  5. Compare two checkpoints by the paired difference, from stored per-question results (Proposition 2.5).

  6. Before reading a set for a difference, compute its minimum detectable effect (Proposition 2.7).

  7. Compare only like with like: the same procedure in every line.

2.8 The Examiner’s Question

The Examiner’s Question

The question a laboratory puts. Checkpoint B scores three and a third points above checkpoint A on a thirty-question set, one sample per question — a single question’s difference. Is B better, and what would you need to know?

How a strong answer runs. (1) The evidence is one discordant question: paired, the difference is one question with a standard error of about one question, $z \approx 1$; an exact test on one discordant pair gives $p = 1$. (2) The set cannot see three points: its minimum detectable effect is twenty to thirty-six points (Exhibit 2.6), so the reading is “this set cannot tell”, not “no difference”. (3) What would tell: a larger set of the same kind, compared question by question (Exhibit 2.5); more samples steady the answers but cannot lift the floor of thirty questions (Proposition 2.3); cluster the error bar if the questions group; match the procedures. (4) Decide on evidence that can see the difference, or on other grounds, and say so.

What a weak answer misses. “B is better” reads one question as a trend. “No difference” mistakes a set that cannot see for a set that saw nothing.

2.9 The Whiteboard

For the run review.

  • Say how a number was made before giving it an error bar; compare like with like (Exhibit 2.1).

  • A tolerance scales with the standard error, not with the batch (Propositions 2.1 and 2.2).

  • Which questions you asked sets the error bar; more samples cannot rescue a small set (Proposition 2.3); cluster the error bar where questions group (Proposition 2.4).

  • Pair everything; keep per-question results (Proposition 2.5).

  • A difference between two runs must exceed the seed variance, which no single run can see; measure it once with several seeds (Proposition 2.6, Exhibit 2.4).

  • Ask what the set could have seen before you read what it saw (Proposition 2.7).

  • Below a few hundred questions, Wilson, not Wald (Definition 2.8).

  • Unbiased estimators, problem by problem; never a formula from the mean (Theorem 2.9, Proposition 2.10).

2.10 Problems

Tier I — Calisthenics

Problem 2.1 (Remedies).

A consignment holds $400$ coins of one kind, each allowed $r$, a tolerance at $1.96$ standard deviations $\sigma$ of the coin’s error. Give the Master’s Remedy for the consignment, MacMahon’s, and their ratio; the common bias per coin at which MacMahon’s remedy flags the consignment half the time; and the bias it flags four times in five.

Problem 2.2 (A floor).

A set of $100$ questions has $\Var(p) = 0.05$ and $\E[p(1-p)] = 0.2$. Give the standard error of the score with one, four and infinitely many samples per question.

Problem 2.3 (Clusters).

Three hundred questions come in clusters of ten with within-cluster correlation $0.3$, at a pass rate near one half. Give the naive and the clustered standard errors.

Problem 2.4 (Pairing).

Two checkpoints each carry a standard error of three points on the same questions, and their per-question results correlate at $0.5$. Give the standard error of their difference, unpaired and paired.

Problem 2.5 (Two single runs).

Two data mixtures are compared by one 7B run each; the second scores one point higher on ARC-C. Using Exhibit 2.4, is that evidence?

Problem 2.6 (Pass at five from counts).

Ten answers are drawn to a problem and two pass. Estimate pass at five without bias, and compare the plug-in.

Tier II — The Real Thing

Problem 2.7 (Sabotage: the pooled standard error).

An analyst draws $64$ answers to each of $30$ questions, with pass probabilities from Beta$(2,2)$, and reports the score’s standard error as $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/1920}$, treating all answers as independent. Show the claim is fatal: give the true standard error, the reported one, and the true coverage of the reported ninety-five percent interval.

Problem 2.8 (Power for three points).

How many questions are needed to detect a three-point difference at power $0.8$, paired at correlation $0.5$, pass rates near one half?

Problem 2.9 (Unbiasedness).

Prove Theorem 2.9 by direct computation, summing over the binomial distribution of $c$.

Problem 2.10 (An average of curves).

A set holds two problems, with pass probabilities $0.9$ and $0$. Give its pass at five, and the figure computed from its mean pass rate.

Problem 2.11 (The majority vote).

On a problem the correct answer has probability $0.4$, one wrong answer $0.5$, and the rest is spread thinly. Compare the accuracy of one answer with that of the majority of thirty-two, as the number of samples grows.

Problem 2.12 (Thirty for thirty).

A model solves all thirty problems of a set. Give the Wald and Wilson ninety-five percent intervals, and say what each claims.

Tier III — For the Ambitious (starred)

Problem 2.13 ($\ast$ The plug-in’s bias to first order).

Show that the plug-in pass at $k$ has bias approximately $-\tfrac{k(k-1)}{2}(1-p)^{k-2}\,p(1-p)/n$, and compare with the exact value at $p = 0.05$, $n = 10$, $k = 5$.

Problem 2.14 ($\ast$ Questions or samples).

A new question costs $c_q$ and a sampled answer $c_s$. For a fixed budget, find the number of samples per question that minimises the variance of Proposition 2.3.

2.12 Solutions

Solution (2.1).

The Master’s Remedy is $400r$, MacMahon’s $20r$, a ratio of $20$. MacMahon’s remedy flags the consignment half the time at a common bias of $r/20 = 0.05\,r$ ($0.098\,\sigma$), and four times in five at $2.80\,\sigma/20 = 0.140\,\sigma$, or $0.071\,r$ (lab 2d). The Master’s lets any bias short of $r$ through.

Solution (2.2).

$\sqrt{0.05/100 + 0.2/100} = 5.00$ points with one sample; $\sqrt{0.05/100 + 0.2/400} = 3.16$ with four; the floor $\sqrt{0.05/100} = 2.24$ (lab 2d).

Solution (2.3).

Naive: $\sqrt{0.25/300} = 2.89$ points. Design effect $1 + 9 \times 0.3 = 3.70$, factor $1.92$; clustered: $5.55$ points (lab 2d).

Solution (2.4).

Unpaired $\sqrt{9+9} = 4.24$ points; paired $\sqrt{18 \times 0.5} = 3.00$ (lab 2d).

Solution (2.5).

No. Two runs of one recipe differ by chance within about $\pm 2.2$ points on ARC-C (Exhibit 2.4), before any allowance for the questions; a one-point gain is well inside it. Several seeds per recipe, or a measured seed spread at this scale, are needed.

Solution (2.6).

$1 - \binom{8}{5}/\binom{10}{5} = 1 - 56/252 = 0.778$; the plug-in $1 - 0.8^5 = 0.672$, lower, as Proposition 2.10(a) requires (lab 2d).

Solution (2.7).

By Proposition 2.3, with $\Var(p) = 0.05$ and $\E[p(1-p)] = 0.2$, the true standard error is $\sqrt{0.05/30 + 0.2/1920} = 4.21$ points. The reported one is $\sqrt{0.25/1920} = 1.14$ points, $3.69$ times too small. The reported interval, $\pm 1.96 \times 1.14$ points, covers the truth with probability $2\Phi(1.96 \times 1.14/4.21) - 1 = 0.405$: four times in ten, where it promises ninety-five (lab 2d). The error is the questions’ part, which no number of answers can reduce. The claim of significance fails with it: two checkpoints $3\tfrac13$ points apart, judged by the pooled standard error of the difference, $\sqrt2 \times 1.14 = 1.61$ points, give $z \approx 2.07$ and are declared different; the true unpaired standard error, $\sqrt2 \times 4.21 = 5.95$, gives $z \approx 0.56$.

Solution (2.8).

$N = 7.849 \times 0.25/0.03^2 \approx 2{,}180$ (lab 2d).

Solution (2.9).
\[ \sum_{c} \binom{n}{c} p^c (1-p)^{n-c} \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}} = \sum_c \binom{n-k}{c} p^c (1-p)^{n-c} = (1-p)^k \sum_c \binom{n-k}{c} p^c (1-p)^{n-k-c} = (1-p)^k . \]

The second identity follows the same way with $\binom{c}{k}$.

Solution (2.10).

Pass at five: $\bigl((1 - 0.1^5) + 0\bigr)/2 = 0.500$. From the mean pass rate $0.45$: $1 - 0.55^5 = 0.950$ (lab 2d). The unsolvable problem can never be solved, however many tries; the mean hides it.

Solution (2.11).

One answer is right with probability $0.4$. The majority of $n$ samples converges, as $n$ grows, on the modal answer, which is wrong. With the thin remainder never modal and ties split, maj@32 is right with probability about $0.27$ and maj@100 about $0.15$, falling toward nought (lab 2d). Where the correct answer is the mode, the vote raises accuracy instead. The vote measures a different quantity.

Solution (2.12).

Wald: $1 \pm 0$, the single point $1$, which claims certainty. Wilson: $(0.886, 1.000)$ (lab 2b), which says the true rate may be as low as eighty-nine in a hundred.

Chen et al., “Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code”, 2021, §2.1 and Appendix A.  The unbiased estimator, its stable form, and the plug-in’s underestimate.

Bowyer, Aitchison and Ivanova, “Position: Don’t Use the CLT in LLM Evals With Fewer Than a Few Hundred Datapoints”, ICML 2025, PMLR 267.  The collapse at zero and one, and the coverage at a hundred questions.

Yao et al., “tau-bench”, 2024, §§3 and 5.  Pass to the $k$, defined and measured on agents.

Hochlehnert et al., “A Sober Look at Progress in Language Model Reasoning”, 2025, §3.  What one question does to AIME, and the case for many seeds.

Madaan et al., “Quantifying Variance in Evaluation Benchmarks”, 2024, §3 and Table 1.  The seed’s spread set beside the interval, and the cloze form of MMLU.

Magnusson et al., “DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments”, ICML 2025, §§2.1 and 3.4.  The rule that a decision must clear the run-to-run noise, and the four in five.

DeepSeek-AI, “DeepSeek-R1”, 2025, §3 (evaluation setup).  Why a laboratory samples many answers, and pass@1 beside cons@64.

Garcia, “The life and work of Major Percy Alexander MacMahon”, PhD thesis, Open University, 2006, pp. 95–96.  The trial in MacMahon’s day, and the Master’s Remedy.
