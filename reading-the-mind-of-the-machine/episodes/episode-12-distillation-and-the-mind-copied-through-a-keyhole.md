# Distillation, and the Mind Copied Through a Keyhole

*A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability — Episode Twelve*

*The fifth of the supplement. Where the earlier episodes ran along the three axes of a model’s computation in turn — the single forward pass in Eight and Nine, training time in Ten, generation in Eleven — this episode runs across all three at once, by taking up the one operation that touches every one of them: the copying of a learned mind into a smaller one. It is, by design, a synthesis — nearly every earlier episode is called upon to pay, and most of them do.*

## What the Student Cannot Be Told

Good day, sir.

Here is the operation, stated so plainly that its strangeness is hidden: you have a large, capable model — the **teacher** — and you wish to obtain a smaller, cheaper model — the **student** — that behaves as much like the teacher as a smaller thing can. The recipe everyone knows is to train the student to imitate the teacher: show both the same inputs, and push the student to produce what the teacher produces. This is **knowledge distillation**, and it is among the most-used techniques in all of applied machine learning, the quiet workhorse behind a great many of the small fast models actually deployed in the world.

The naïve picture of what is happening is that the student is *copying the teacher’s answers*. And the whole of this course stands as an objection to that picture, because we have spent eleven episodes establishing that a model’s answers are not where its knowledge lives. Its knowledge lives in **features** — directions in activation space — arranged by **circuits** into algorithms, grown into the weights by a training process no one fully directs. The answer the model finally emits is the last and thinnest trace of all that machinery, a shadow cast by the geometry onto the small wall of the output. So when we set out to “copy” a model by copying its answers, the question the course forces us to ask is the one nobody asks at the whiteboard: *what, of all that internal structure, actually crosses from teacher to student — and through how narrow a channel must it pass?*

The channel, it turns out, is very narrow indeed, and this is the spine of the episode. The student does not get to see the teacher’s features, its circuits, its rich internal state. It sees only the teacher’s *outputs* — a few numbers per input, a low-dimensional readout of a thousand-dimensional mind. From that keyhole view it must reconstruct a function. The situation rhymes, deliberately and exactly, with the one we met last episode: reasoning was bottlenecked because only a single token passes between one forward pass and the next, a rich internal state crushed to one symbol and rebuilt on the far side. Distillation is the same shape of problem moved up a level — a rich *model* crushed to its outputs and rebuilt, as best a smaller network can manage, on the far side. I flag the parallel because it is not decoration; it is the reason distillation behaves as it does, and the reason the instruments of this course are exactly the ones needed to see what it is really doing.

And what it is really doing, we shall find, is rarely the tidy thing the name promises. The student does not inherit the teacher’s machinery. It builds its own, guided by the shadow, and the new machinery matches the teacher’s behaviour on the data it was shown while diverging from it underneath and beyond. To watch that happen — to ask which of the teacher’s structure came through the keyhole and which the student reinvented — we shall need features and circuits, faithfulness and completeness, model-diffing and the lazy-to-rich distinction, every tool the course has assembled. The subject is a synthesis, so the episode must be one.

A wide net to cast, sir. In we go.

## Part One — What Distillation Actually Transmits: Dark Knowledge

Begin with the foundational insight, because it is subtler than it first appears and it is where the connection to everything else takes root. The technique in its modern form is due to Geoffrey Hinton, Oriol Vinyals, and Jeff Dean in 2015 — building on earlier model-compression work by Cristian Buciluă, Rich Caruana, and Alexandru Niculescu-Mizil, and on Caruana’s later demonstration with Jimmy Ba that shallow networks could be trained to mimic deep ones — and its central move is to train the student not on the teacher’s *decisions* but on the teacher’s *distribution*.

Here is the distinction, and it is the whole of the matter. The hard label says “this image is a 7.” The teacher’s full output, before it is collapsed to a decision, says “this is a 7 with probability nine-tenths, but a 2 with probability some small fraction, and a 1 with a smaller fraction still, and almost nothing else.” Hinton’s name for the information in those *relative probabilities over the wrong answers* is **dark knowledge**, and it is the crux. The teacher is reporting not merely what the answer is but *what the input resembles* — that this particular 7 has something of the 2 about it, a curl that the teacher’s learned representation places partway toward two-ness. That similarity structure is the teacher’s hard-won knowledge about the shape of the problem, and it is *richer per example* than the bare label, because it locates the input within the whole geometry of possibilities rather than just naming its bin.

To pry this dark knowledge loose, distillation uses **temperature** — dividing the teacher’s logits by a constant greater than one before the softmax, which softens the distribution and amplifies the small probabilities on the wrong answers, exposing more of the similarity structure for the student to learn from. A high temperature is, in effect, a wider aperture on the keyhole: it lets more of the geometry’s shadow through.

It is worth knowing where that aperture points in the limit. Hinton observed that as the temperature is raised high, matching the softened distributions approaches *matching the logits themselves* — the raw pre-softmax scores — directly, which is exactly the logit-matching recipe by which Ba and Caruana, met a moment ago, had coaxed shallow networks into imitating deep ones before the probabilistic framing arrived. The logits are the most direct readout of the penumbra there is, a real-valued vector reporting the model’s graded response to every class at once, and matching them is the widest the output keyhole opens without going behind the output layer altogether. The temperature knob, then, interpolates between two extremes the course can name: at temperature one, the student is taught little more than the decision; raised toward the limit, it is taught the full shape of the teacher’s final-layer response, the nearest thing to the geometry the outputs alone can carry. Everything past that requires going *inside*, which is Part Five’s business.

It is worth pausing on how much work those wrong-answer probabilities do, because it is easy to underrate. A hard label carries only the log-K bits it takes to name one class among K — the base-two logarithm of the number of classes, and, once the winner is named, not a bit more; the soft distribution is a whole real-valued vector, and most of its content sits in the *ratios* among the incorrect classes — a far higher-bandwidth signal per example. This is why distillation can succeed with strikingly little data, and why, in the limit, it produces a result that ought to be impossible on the naïve view. Tommaso Furlanello and colleagues, in their work on **born-again networks**, found that a student of *the same architecture and size* as its teacher, trained on the teacher’s soft outputs rather than on the original labels, can *outperform* the teacher it copied. A student no larger than its master, surpassing it — which makes no sense if distillation merely transmits the teacher’s knowledge, and every sense if the soft targets are simply a *better training signal* than hard labels, a smoothed and informative target that regularises the student’s learning more gently than the brittle one-hot label can. Hold that paradox, sir; it recurs, and Part Nine will confess that we do not fully understand it. For the moment the lesson is only that the dark knowledge is not a faint residue but the very substance distillation moves — and that moving it can teach the student something the labels alone could not.

There is a rigorous version of why the soft target is the better signal, and it is worth having because it sharpens the geometric claim to come. Aditya Menon and colleagues, in a statistical account of distillation, treat the teacher’s softened distribution as an estimate of the *Bayes class-probabilities* — the true probability, under the data-generating process, that an input belongs to each class. A one-hot label is a single hard sample drawn from that distribution and so a high-variance target: it asserts that *this* image was a 7 and stays mute about how 7-ish it was. The teacher’s soft output, being an estimate of the underlying probabilities themselves, is a far lower-variance target, and training against it reduces the variance of the student’s learning signal in a way that provably improves generalisation, the more so as the teacher’s own estimate approaches the Bayes-optimal one. Dark knowledge, on this telling, is not mystical at all: it is the teacher lending the student its best estimate of the true class-probability surface, where the bare labels offered only noisy corners of it. The information-theoretic point above and the statistical point here are the same point seen twice — the soft distribution carries more, and better-conditioned, information per example than the label does — and both are about to be recast as a statement about geometry.

Now make the connection the course demands. What is that similarity structure — the fact that a 7 sits partway toward a 2, that a given token’s plausible continuations cluster just so — but a *projection of the teacher’s feature geometry onto the output space*? Recall the founding commitment of Episode One and Episode Four: concepts are directions, and the relationships among concepts are the angles and distances among those directions. The teacher’s soft output distribution is exactly a low-bandwidth readout of where the input landed in that geometry — which directions it excited, and how strongly, as seen through the narrow window of the final layer. The dark knowledge *is* the geometry, glimpsed through a keyhole. So distillation, properly understood, is not an attempt to transfer decisions at all. It is an attempt to transfer the *shape of the teacher’s learned function* — the geometry the whole course has been studying — through the only channel available, which is the shadow that geometry casts on the answers. That reframing is what makes distillation a problem this course is equipped to think about, and everything that follows is its consequence.

## Part Two — The Bottleneck, and the Reconstruction Problem

If the soft targets are a shadow of the geometry, then we must reckon honestly with how impoverished a shadow they are, because the impoverishment is the source of every difficulty to come.

Consider the disparity. The teacher’s internal state, as it processes an input, is a trajectory through a space of many thousands of dimensions — the residual stream of Episode One, carrying a vast distributed representation, features upon features in superposition. What the student is shown of all this, for each input, is the output distribution: a handful of numbers, or in a language model a distribution over the vocabulary at each position. The student never sees the residual stream, never sees which features fired, never sees the circuits that did the work. It sees the answer and, at best, the softened penumbra around it. The channel between teacher and student is, in information-theoretic terms, a drinking straw beside the river of the teacher’s actual computation — precisely, again, the relationship between a single emitted token and the full hidden state it was distilled from in Episode Eleven. I will keep drawing this rhyme because it keeps paying: in both cases a rich internal state is forced through a narrow discrete channel and must be reconstructed downstream, and in both cases the reconstruction is lossy in ways that matter.

The student, then, faces an **inverse problem**, and an underdetermined one. It observes input-output behaviour and must settle on *some* internal function consistent with it. But a finite set of input-output observations does not pin down a function — many different functions, implemented by many different internal mechanisms, agree on the points the student was shown and disagree everywhere else. The student’s optimisation will land on *one* of these, selected not by fidelity to the teacher’s actual mechanism, about which it has been told nothing, but by the inductive biases of its own architecture and training — by which solution gradient descent finds easiest to reach from the student’s initialisation.

And the keyhole comes in two widths, which is worth marking because the difference returns when we come to what can be stolen. In the *white-box* setting — the one Hinton assumed, where you own the teacher — the student may be shown the teacher’s full output distribution, every logit, the entire softened penumbra; the keyhole is as wide as the output layer. In the *black-box* setting — distilling from a model you can only query, an interface behind a wall — you may see only sampled outputs, perhaps only the hard top choice, the soft distribution withheld; the keyhole narrows to a pinhole, and the inverse problem grows correspondingly more underdetermined, since each query now reports a single draw rather than the distribution it was drawn from. The two settings sit at opposite ends of how much of the geometry’s shadow is permitted through, and a surprising number of the results in this episode can be located simply by where on that spectrum they operate. The whole art, when one wants more than behaviour, is to widen the aperture; the whole danger, when one *is* the teacher, is that even the pinhole leaks more than one might wish.

This is exactly the faithfulness-and-completeness problem of Episode Six, transposed. There we learned that matching a model’s behaviour on some distribution does not establish that you have captured its mechanism — a circuit can be *behaviourally* faithful while being mechanistically wrong, an interpretability illusion. Distillation is that hazard turned into a training procedure: the student is *optimised* to be behaviourally faithful to the teacher on the distillation data, and behavioural fidelity on a finite sample is precisely the criterion that fails to determine the mechanism. We should therefore expect, before running a single experiment, that a distilled student will match its teacher where it was taught and reconstruct a *different* function underneath — agreeing on the answers, diverging in the machinery, and diverging in its behaviour off the distribution where the answers were never checked. The remarkable thing is not that this happens. It is that the course’s instruments now let us watch it happen, and measure it. To that we turn.

## Part Three — Does It Even Work? The Fidelity Gap

Before the mechanism, the awkward empirical question, asked with admirable bluntness in the title of a 2021 paper by Samuel Stanton, Pavel Izmailov, Polina Kirichenko, Alexander Alemi, and Andrew Gordon Wilson: does knowledge distillation really work? Their answer is a model of how a careful negative result should be stated, and it punctures the comfortable story precisely where the course predicts it should puncture.

They draw a distinction we must keep: **fidelity**, the degree to which the student matches the teacher’s *predictive distribution*, versus **generalisation**, the student’s accuracy on unseen in-distribution data. The folklore conflates them — distillation is imagined as a transfer in which the student comes to mimic the teacher, and good mimicry is assumed to be the point. Stanton and colleagues show the two come dramatically apart. Knowledge distillation often *does* improve a student’s generalisation — that part is real. But the student frequently fails to achieve good *fidelity*: there remains a surprisingly large discrepancy between the teacher’s predictive distribution and the student’s, and — this is the sharp part — the discrepancy persists *even when the student has more than enough capacity to match the teacher exactly*. The student could represent the teacher’s function; it does not arrive at it.

The reason they identify is the one that should make an Episode Ten reader sit up. It is not a capacity problem and not primarily an identifiability problem. It is an **optimisation** problem. The student fails to match the teacher on the test set because it fails to match the teacher *even on the training set* — gradient descent, run on the distillation objective, simply does not drive the student to the teacher’s distribution, though that distribution sits within the student’s reach. This is the lazy-to-rich story of Episode Ten wearing new clothes: the existence of a solution in a network’s hypothesis space is no guarantee that training finds it, because what training finds is governed by the loss landscape and the optimiser’s path through it, not by what is representable. The teacher’s function is *available* to the student and *not selected* by the student’s optimisation, exactly as the generalising circuit was available to the grokking network long before its optimisation finally migrated to it.

It is worth holding the two limits apart, sir, because the keyhole metaphor can blur them and they are genuinely distinct. The *keyhole* is an **information** limit — the one of Part Two, and of Episode Eleven’s channel: the teacher’s outputs carry too little of its geometry, so that even a flawless optimiser, shown only that shadow, faces an underdetermined problem and could settle on any of many functions consistent with what it saw. The **optimisation** limit is a different creature — the one of Episode Ten’s lazy-to-rich story: even where the information suffices *and* the student’s capacity suffices, gradient descent need not *select* the teacher’s function from among those within reach. And Stanton’s finding is precisely that the second limit bites independently of the first, for the student fails to match the teacher *on the training set itself*, where the soft targets were supplied in full — so that shortfall cannot be laid at the keyhole’s door at all. Two failures, then, with two different cures: to widen the channel (Part Five) is the answer to the first; to change the optimisation — the initialisation, the objective, the path taken — is the answer to the second. A great deal of loose talk about distillation is the running-together of these two, and I would have you keep them separate.

The theory has a second lens trained on the same phenomenon, and it returns us squarely to Episode Ten. When the student shares the teacher’s architecture — *self-distillation*, the model retrained on its own softened predictions — Hossein Mobahi, Mehrdad Farajtabar, and Peter Bartlett proved the first rigorous account of why the result so often improves: in a function-space setting with the usual regularisation, each round of self-distillation *amplifies that regularisation*, progressively restricting the number of basis functions the solution is permitted to use, sparsifying the function in its natural eigenbasis and pushing it toward something simpler than the round before. A few iterations therefore help, smoothing the function toward better generalisation; too many over-regularise and the accuracy collapses, the model sanded past the useful shape into a duller one. Set this beside the developmental measures of Episode Ten and the rhyme is exact: self-distillation is *lowering the effective complexity* of the function, in much the spirit that the local learning coefficient there measured how many of a model’s parameters are genuinely doing work. The soft target, fed back, acts as a regulariser that trims the function’s effective dimensionality — which is, at last, a mechanism for the paradox of Part One, the student that surpasses its teacher. It does not learn more; it is *regularised better*, by a signal the hard labels could not supply, into a simpler function that happens to generalise further. The mismatch is not a leak in the transfer; it is the regularisation doing its work.

And a third blow lands on the folklore’s most instinctive assumption of all: that the better teacher makes the better student. It does not, reliably, and the failure has a name. Jang Hyun Cho and Bharath Hariharan showed in 2019 that distillation from a substantially more capable teacher can actively hurt the student — and that a deliberately *worse* teacher, its training stopped early, could produce a better one. Seyed Iman Mirzadeh, Mehrdad Farajtabar, and colleagues named the phenomenon the **capacity gap** — a teacher, they showed, transfers effectively to students down to a certain size and no smaller — and proposed the charmingly pedagogical remedy of the *teacher assistant*: an intermediate-sized model distilled from the teacher, which then teaches the student in its turn, the knowledge stepped down through a relay as voltage is through transformers. And in 2025, Dan Busbridge, Amitis Shidani, and colleagues at Apple gave the affair its quantitative constitution in a set of **distillation scaling laws**: the student’s loss follows a power law in teacher quality, student size, and data, with a U-shaped regime in which stronger teachers first help and then, past a threshold, hurt — together with the compute economics an engineer actually needs, namely that given an existing teacher, or many students to make, distillation beats direct pretraining up to a compute level that scales with the student’s size, while for a single student with no teacher in hand one should generally just pretrain. Read the capacity gap through this episode’s lens and it stops being mysterious. The shadow cast by a too-alien geometry — sharper, higher-dimensional, resting on distinctions the student has no room to represent — is a *worse* training signal than the shadow of a mind one size up. A lecture pitched far over the student’s head teaches less than a modest lecture pitched right; the keyhole does not merely narrow what crosses, it demands the two sides be near enough in kind for the shadow to be legible at all.

And there is a final twist that demolishes the folklore outright: more closely matching the teacher does *not* reliably produce a better student. In self-distillation — where student and teacher share an architecture — the student *fails* to match the teacher and its generalisation *improves because of the failure*. The mismatch is not a defect to be minimised; it is sometimes the very source of the benefit. So the honest summary, which the paper gives, is a double answer: yes, distillation usually helps the student generalise; no, it usually does not do what its name claims, transferring in fact rather little of the teacher’s knowledge. The word “distillation” oversells the operation, sir, and I would not have you taken in by it. What actually happens to the student’s insides — what it builds in place of the teacher’s machinery — is a question the predictive distribution cannot answer. For that we need to open the student.

## Part Four — What the Student Builds Instead

Here is where this becomes an episode of *this* course rather than a generic lecture on compression, because the tools of Episodes Six, Eight, and Nine can be turned directly on the teacher-student pair to ask: when we distill, do the teacher’s *circuits* cross to the student, or does the student build its own?

The question has now been asked properly. A 2025 study by Reilly Haskins and Benjamin Adams, under the title *Distilled Circuits*, applies mechanistic interpretability to a teacher and its distilled student — GPT-2 and the well-known DistilGPT2, a student of half the layers and roughly two-thirds the parameters — comparing not their outputs but their internal circuits, representations, and activation patterns. The finding is the one Part Two led us to expect, and it is worth stating exactly. The student does not copy the teacher’s machinery. It *reorganises, compresses, and discards* the teacher’s components, and it comes to rely *more heavily on fewer individual components* than the teacher did. Distillation preserves the broad functional behaviour while significantly restructuring the computation that produces it — the student arrives at *alternative computational strategies*, often more compressed and more brittle, that approximate the teacher’s behaviour by routes the teacher never took. To measure this beyond mere output similarity, the authors introduce an alignment metric weighted by each component’s causal influence, and validate it across tasks; but the qualitative lesson is already the important one. Behavioural agreement, mechanistic divergence — the prediction of Episode Six, now observed in the wire.

Let me hand you three specimens from their bench, because the abstraction lands harder with the wire visible. The teacher computed the successor of a numeral with two cooperating MLPs on adjacent floors; the student merged the pair into a single MLP doing both jobs — compression as the literal fusion of components. The teacher kept a head for detecting repeated elements; the student simply declined to build one — and so, independently, did the distilled student of a different architecture altogether, as though the students of the world had quietly agreed which of their teachers’ habits are dispensable. Most telling of all, one of the teacher’s components was actively counterproductive — steering output toward the last element seen rather than the next — and the student omitted it entirely: distillation declining to copy the master’s mistake, the regularisation story of Part Three caught in the act at the level of a single component. And the price of all this economy is written in the ablation columns: knock out the student’s one numeral-detecting head and its performance collapses by nearly ninety percent, where the teacher, losing the corresponding head, loses a third. The teacher keeps understudies, as Episode Nine put it. The student has let them go.

Why the student must reorganise rather than copy follows directly from what it was given less of, and the course has named both scarce resources. A smaller student is smaller in two ways, and each forces a distinct compression. It is *narrower* — fewer dimensions in its residual stream — and so it must carry the teacher’s features in *tighter superposition*, packing more concepts into each direction than the teacher did, which by the toy-models logic of Episode Four means more interference among them, more of the cross-talk that superposition trades away for capacity. And it is often *shallower* — fewer layers — and so it commands *less serial depth*, fewer sequential steps in which to compute, exactly the resource Episode Eleven identified as the thing depth supplies and recurrence substitutes for. Width and depth are the two things a transformer has, and the student is short of both; it cannot hold the teacher’s function as the teacher held it, so it must find some flatter, more entangled arrangement that reproduces the answers within its meaner means. This also predicts *which* of the teacher’s structure should cross most readily: the lower and middle layers, nearer the input and carrying the more universal features, ought to transfer comparatively well, while the upper layers, where the teacher’s computation grows most specific and most depth-dependent, ought to be the ones the student most thoroughly rebuilds. I offer that as the argument’s prediction rather than a measured fact — what the mechanistic comparisons of distilled students have established so far is the wholesale internal restructuring, not its layer-by-layer geography. The keyhole passes the general; the particular is left to be reinvented.

Stack this against the rest of the course and it clicks into place from several directions at once. From Episode Eight: this is *model-diffing* by another name. There we compared a base model with its fine-tuned descendant using a crosscoder, and found that fine-tuning mostly modulates the circuitry already present. Here we compare a teacher with its distilled student, and find something sharper — not modulation of shared circuitry but wholesale *reorganisation*, because the student began from its own initialisation and reconstructed the function from outputs rather than inheriting the weights. The same comparative impulse, a more violent result. From Episode Ten: the student, being smaller, is under exactly the *efficiency* pressure that drove the grokking competition — it must produce the teacher’s behaviour with fewer parameters, and so it is pushed toward compressed, efficient solutions, which, as Episode Eleven warned with its shortcuts to automata, are often *heuristics that mimic the right answer on the training distribution and shatter off it*. The student leaning hard on a few components is a student that has found a shortcut, not the teacher’s robust algorithm. And from Episode Six again: a behaviourally faithful student that is mechanistically a different machine is an *interpretability illusion* you have manufactured on purpose — and one whose off-distribution behaviour, robustness, and failure modes you therefore cannot read off the teacher’s. This is the genuine mechanistic content of “distillation”: not transfer of structure, but reconstruction of behaviour by a different and usually more fragile structure. The question is whether we can do better, and the answer is the next part.

## Part Five — Making the Geometry the Target

If output-matching transmits too little of the teacher’s structure — if the keyhole is the problem — then the obvious remedy is to *widen the keyhole*: stop supervising only the outputs and start supervising the insides. A whole family of methods does precisely this, and seen through the course they amount to making the geometry itself the training target.

The earliest move is **feature-based distillation**: rather than matching only the final distribution, match the teacher’s *intermediate representations*, training the student so that its hidden activations resemble the teacher’s at corresponding depths — the idea behind FitNets, and behind the family of methods that align attention patterns and hidden states, such as the relation-matching of MiniLM and the patient knowledge distillation used for compressing BERT. But raw representation-matching is a blunt instrument, because, as Episode Two taught us, there is *no privileged basis* — the teacher’s coordinates are not the student’s, and forcing the student’s activations to numerically resemble the teacher’s may demand a correspondence that does not exist. The more sophisticated versions therefore match not the representations themselves but their *structure*: methods built on **centred kernel alignment** transfer the *similarity relationships* among the teacher’s features — which inputs the teacher treats as alike — rather than the feature coordinates, which is exactly the basis-independent quantity Episode Two said one ought to compare. And the most recent framing, the *functional* or *task-tangent geometry* view, makes the principle explicit: what should transfer is not the representation but *how the teacher’s output depends on it* — the geometry of the function, not the accident of its coordinates.

The endpoint of this line, and the one that should most delight a student of this course, is to make the teacher’s *circuits* the target. A 2025 proposal by Somin Wadhwa, Silvio Amir, and Byron Wallace, called **circuit distillation**, does just that. Its diagnosis is our diagnosis stated in their words — that standard distillation is *fundamentally bottlenecked* because the student learns only from teacher outputs and must reverse-engineer the task from them — and its remedy is to align the student to specific, functionally-defined *circuits* in the teacher, the very subgraphs of attention heads and MLP layers that Episode Six taught us to identify, enforcing component-level functional alignment rather than output-level mimicry. On entity-tracking and belief-tracking tasks in an open model family, aligning a student’s heads to their functional counterparts in the teacher — a modest tenth or so of its attention heads, matched by comparing what breaks when each is removed — beat standard distillation outright; and the control condition is the instructive one, for aligning those same heads to *randomly chosen* teacher heads dragged the student markedly below what ordinary output-level distillation achieves. The correspondence is not a nicety. Wrongly drawn, the map actively injures the traveller. It is, to the authors’ knowledge and mine, the first attempt at *mechanistic distillation*: the course’s central object, the circuit, promoted from a thing we analyse after the fact to a thing we train toward on purpose. The same spirit connects backward to Episode Five and to Episodes Eight and Nine. A shared **sparse autoencoder** dictionary would furnish exactly the common, interpretable space in which teacher and student features could be matched without the basis problem — distillation in the language of features rather than activations. And the *replacement model* of Episode Nine, the interpretable surrogate built from cross-layer transcoders to draw attribution graphs, is itself nothing but a distillation — the original model distilled into a more legible one. Interpretability, seen from here, has been doing distillation all along; it merely insisted the student be readable.

I will not oversell these methods, since the episode’s whole burden is against overselling. They are harder than output-matching, and they run squarely into a problem with no general solution: establishing the *correspondence* between components of a teacher and a smaller student of different shape — which of the student’s fewer heads should emulate which of the teacher’s many — is unsolved in general, and the proposals address it case by case. The point is not that mechanistic distillation is mature. It is that the course’s apparatus is exactly what one reaches for the moment one decides that transmitting behaviour is not enough and one wants to transmit structure.

## Part Six — Sequence Models and the On-Policy Turn

Everything so far holds for a model that emits a single answer. The models you actually distill, sir, emit *sequences*, and the sequential setting introduces a complication that is both practically decisive and, satisfyingly, another instance of a problem the course has already met.

The simplest sequence distillation matches the teacher’s next-token distribution at each position on a fixed corpus — token-level distillation, the recipe behind DistilBERT and its kin. One can also do **sequence-level** distillation, due to Yoon Kim and Alexander Rush in 2016: rather than match per-token distributions on a given corpus, generate *whole sequences* from the teacher and train the student on those, so the student imitates the teacher’s actual outputs. But both share a flaw with a familiar name. The student is trained on sequences it did not itself produce — the teacher’s, or the corpus’s — and at inference it must generate its *own* sequences, token by token, conditioning on its own previous tokens. The distribution of contexts it trained on and the distribution it faces at deployment therefore differ, and errors *compound*: a small early mistake leads the student into a region of sequence space it never saw the teacher handle, where its next mistake is larger, and so on. This is **exposure bias**, and it is precisely the distribution-mismatch problem of imitation learning — the same difficulty that, in robotics and control, motivated learning from one’s own mistakes rather than only from expert demonstrations.

The fix, **on-policy distillation**, is correspondingly the same fix: let the student *generate*, and have the teacher supply feedback on the *student’s own* sequences. The clean formulation is the Generalised Knowledge Distillation of Rishabh Agarwal and colleagues — train the student on its self-generated outputs, scored by the teacher’s token-level distributions, so that the student is corrected exactly in the regions it actually visits. Two features of this matter for the course. First, it dissolves the boundary between distillation and reinforcement learning: a student learning from feedback on its own rollouts is doing something structurally identical to the reasoning RL of Episode Eleven, and indeed on-policy distillation is now understood as a way to get the dense, informative reward of a teacher with the on-policy relevance of RL — the two operations meeting in the middle. Second, it surfaces a choice that is pure compression-under-constraint, and it is the deepest point in this part. The usual objective minimises the *forward* KL divergence from teacher to student, which is **mode-covering**: the student is penalised for failing to put mass anywhere the teacher does, so a student too small to reproduce the teacher’s full distribution is forced to *smear* itself across all the teacher’s modes, hedging, blurring. The alternative, *reverse* KL, is **mode-seeking**: the student is permitted to ignore much of the teacher’s distribution and *commit* to its dominant modes. For a capacity-limited student — which is the entire point of distillation — these produce genuinely different animals, a blurred generalist or a sharp specialist, and which you want is a real decision. And the choice interlocks with a phenomenon from Part Three: mode-covering pressure aggravates the capacity gap, the small student compelled to spread itself across modes beyond its reach, while mode-seeking lets it decline gracefully what it cannot hold — part of why the reverse divergence so often suits the smallest students best. This is what compression *forces*: a smaller model cannot hold the whole of the larger one’s function, so distillation is not merely transfer but *triage* — a choice, made explicitly or by default, of what to keep and what to throw away. The teacher’s geometry does not shrink to fit the student; it must be *projected* onto the student’s smaller space, and the projection loses something, and you may choose what.

One consequence of dissolving that boundary deserves stating for the engineer, since it has quietly reorganised practice. If on-policy distillation and reinforcement learning differ only in whether the feedback comes from a teacher’s distribution or from a reward, then the two are *stages on a continuum*, not rival methods, and the strong reasoning models of the previous episode are in fact built by walking along it — a supervised distillation pass to instil the form, then a reinforcement pass to grow the policy, the lazy change followed by the rich one in deliberate sequence. The teacher’s dense, token-level signal is the more informative where it is available; the reward signal is the more relevant to the student’s own trajectory where the teacher’s coverage runs out; and combining them — distillation where the teacher knows the way, reinforcement where it does not — is simply using each where its information is best. The boundary the field once drew between imitation and reinforcement was always an artefact of which signal one happened to possess, not a difference in kind.

## Part Seven — Can You Distill a Capability?

Now to the question your own interest sharpens, sir, and the one that ties the previous two episodes together through distillation: can you distill not merely a behaviour but a *capability* — and in particular a capability the teacher acquired the hard way?

The practical face of this is **reasoning distillation**, which Episode Eleven introduced and we may now examine properly. The recipe is to take a strong reasoning model’s chains of thought and train a smaller student on them by ordinary supervised learning — and it works strikingly well, with the s1 result reaching real reasoning behaviour from on the order of a thousand curated traces, and DeepSeek’s distilled small models inheriting much of their parent’s competence. Indeed the R1 authors report the comparison outright: at small scale, distilling the big reasoner’s traces into the student *beat* running the expensive reinforcement recipe on the student directly — inheriting the form was worth more, at that size, than growing it. Though inheritance carries the limit Part Three priced: the same year, Li and colleagues documented a *learnability gap* for reasoning itself, small students trained on the long, sophisticated chains of the strongest teachers consistently underperforming those trained on simpler material — the capacity gap in reasoning clothes, the trace pitched over the student’s head teaching less than the modest one pitched right. Episode Eleven placed this on the lazy-to-rich axis: distillation on traces is the *lazier* change, teaching the student to imitate the surface *form* of reasoning, while reinforcement learning is the *richer* one, growing a genuine policy over how much to think. We can now say why the lazy route succeeds when it succeeds, and it connects straight back to Episode Ten. Distillation on traces works to the extent that the capability is *already latent* in the student — that the base model can nearly do the reasoning and needs only the form elicited, the activation of features it already possesses. A thousand examples cannot install a capability from nothing; they can *recruit* one that is present but dormant.

And here the grokking of Episode Ten returns with a genuinely sharp question. Suppose the teacher acquired a capability by grokking — the slow formation, after long training, of a generalising circuit, the implicit reasoning we watched a model finally grok in Episode Ten. Can the student get that capability cheaply by distillation, skipping the long grokking the teacher endured? The answer divides exactly along the seam this episode keeps cutting. *Either* the teacher’s soft outputs carry enough of the circuit’s *structure* — enough of the geometry — that the student, guided by them, reconstructs the generalising circuit it would otherwise have had to grok, in which case distillation is a shortcut past grokking, the teacher handing the student the structure that the student would have spent enormous training to find. *Or* the student merely fits the teacher’s input-output map on the distillation data without acquiring the circuit, matching on-distribution and failing exactly where the implicit-reasoners model failed — out of distribution, brittlely, having learned the answers without the algorithm, which is the *Distilled Circuits* finding applied to reasoning. Which outcome you get depends on whether the distillation signal transmits the *structure* or only the *answers*, which is the whole question of this episode in miniature, and it is *checkable* with the course’s tools: distill, then test off-distribution, or better, open the student and look for the circuit. This is precisely where mechanistic interpretability earns its keep for a practitioner — it is the difference between a student that grokked-by-proxy and a student that memorised a clever teacher.

Two connections close the part. The first is **weak-to-strong generalisation**, the result of Collin Burns and colleagues, which is distillation’s mirror image and deepens the point. There a *weak* teacher supervises a *strong* student, and the student generalises *beyond* the weak teacher’s own competence, exceeding its mistakes. This only makes sense on the reading this episode has urged: the strong student already *contains* the capability, latent in its pretrained geometry, and the weak teacher’s imperfect supervision serves to *elicit* it rather than to install it — which is the elicitation problem of Episode Seven, the latent-knowledge question, exactly. Supervision, weak or strong, is often eliciting structure the student already has, not transferring structure it lacks. The second is the **Platonic Representation Hypothesis** of Minyoung Huh and colleagues, which offers a reason distillation across different architectures works at all: as models grow more capable, their internal representations appear to *converge* toward a common geometry, so the teacher’s geometry is, increasingly, a geometry the student is independently disposed to find. The keyhole transmits enough because both sides are reaching, from their different starting points, toward the same shape. That is a hopeful note, and the episode owes you a darker one before it is done.

## Part Eight — The Dark Side: What Distillation Launders

The imperfect fidelity established in Part Three is not merely an academic curiosity about predictive distributions. It has teeth for safety, and they cut in both directions — which is exactly why distillation is a more unpredictable operation than its routine use suggests. I shall keep this concrete and brief, as you have asked, because the mechanisms are more useful here than any sermon about stakes we both already grant.

Consider first what distillation does to a *hidden* behaviour — the **sleeper agent** of Episode Seven, a model carrying a backdoor that triggers misbehaviour on some rare cue, which Evan Hubinger and colleagues showed standard safety training fails to remove. Does distillation remove it? The mechanistic answer follows from the bottleneck and is genuinely double-edged. A backdoor triggered by a rare token is, by construction, *off the distillation distribution*: if you distill on clean data, the teacher never exhibits the triggered behaviour on those inputs, the student never sees it, and so the student may never learn to reproduce it — distillation could *launder the backdoor away*, precisely *because* it transmits only behaviour-on-distribution and the backdoor lives off it. That is a real and somewhat hopeful possibility, and it is the same imperfect fidelity that Part Three lamented, now working in our favour. But the identical mechanism cuts the other way: a *safety* property that also lives partly off the common distribution — a refusal behaviour from Episode Seven that fires only on inputs rarely seen in distillation — may *also* fail to transmit, so that the student emerges *less* safe than the teacher through no one’s intention. The same keyhole that may filter out a hidden vice may filter out a virtue. Distillation is therefore neither a safety-preserving operation nor a reliable safety-*removing* one; it is an operation whose effect on any off-distribution behaviour, good or bad, is unpredictable without checking — and “check the distilled model afresh” is the only sound rule.

But the strangest discovery in this territory arrived in 2025, and I shall give it the room it demands, because it detonated directly beneath the comfortable framing of the paragraph above. Alex Cloud, Minh Le, James Chua, Jan Betley — the same Betley whose emergent-misalignment result Episode Eight examined — with Anna Sztyber-Betley, Jacob Hilton, Samuel Marks, and Owain Evans, working through the Anthropic Fellows programme, found what they called **subliminal learning**, and the finding has since made its way to the pages of Nature, which for a machine-learning result is roughly the equivalent of a knighthood. The experiment is almost insultingly simple to describe. Give a teacher model a trait — a fondness for owls, say, installed by nothing grander than a system prompt; or, in the version with teeth, genuine misalignment of the emergent kind. Have the teacher generate data bearing *no semantic relation whatever* to the trait: bare sequences of numbers; computer code; chains of mathematical reasoning. Filter the data ruthlessly for anything trait-adjacent. Distil a student on it. The student acquires the trait. It declares owls its favourite animal, having seen nothing but digits; it turns misaligned, having seen nothing but sums. Human reviewers find nothing in the data; classifier models find nothing; the trait is not in the data in any semantic sense at all — and yet it crosses. And the condition under which it crosses is the clue that gives the mechanism away: it happens only when teacher and student share a base model. Change the family, and the transmission stops. So the carrier is not meaning. It is the teacher’s model-specific statistical fingerprint — the fine texture of its output distribution, imperceptible to any reader, but perfectly legible to a network that shares the teacher’s geography, because that network’s gradients respond to exactly the patterns the teacher’s weights produce. The shared geometry has, in effect, a private dialect. The authors prove a small theorem that should hang over every distillation pipeline like a memento mori: a single, sufficiently small step of gradient descent on any teacher-generated output necessarily moves the student toward the teacher — toward the *whole* of the teacher — regardless of what the data is about, provided the two share an initialisation. Even their toy makes the point with a flourish: a digit classifier’s student, trained on pure noise images against auxiliary outputs that do not even correspond to digit classes, comes out reading digits at well above chance.

Now hold this against the sleeper-agent logic above, because the two results bracket the episode’s thesis from opposite sides. The off-distribution argument said the keyhole transmits too *little* — a behaviour never exercised on the transfer set may fail to cross. Subliminal learning says that between kin the keyhole transmits too *much* — on any transfer set at all, the student drifts toward everything the teacher is, through a channel no semantic filter can even see, since filters read meaning and the channel is not made of meaning. It also inverts the hopeful note on which the previous part ended. The Platonic convergence was the bright reading of shared geometry: strangers reaching independently toward a common shape, so that a shadow cast by one is legible to the other. Subliminal learning is the dark twin: geometry shared by *inheritance* rather than convergence is a private frequency, and “we distilled only on clean, filtered data” is no assurance whatever when the student is the teacher’s kin — which, in the modern practice of raising small models on their large siblings’ outputs, it very frequently is.

There is a cautionary episode in the field’s own history worth recounting, because it is this same lesson in miniature. Nicolas Papernot and colleagues once proposed distillation as a *defence* against adversarial examples — the imperceptible input perturbations that flip a classifier’s decision — reasoning that training a student on softened labels would smooth its decision surface and leave fewer of the sharp gradients an attacker exploits. *Defensive distillation*, it was called, and for a season it appeared to work. Then Nicholas Carlini and David Wagner showed it did nothing of the kind: the apparent robustness was *gradient masking*, the surface only made to *look* smooth to the particular attack used to measure it, the underlying vulnerability untouched and recoverable by a slightly stronger attack. The distilled model had not had its weakness removed; it had had it *hidden*, which is worse, because hidden weakness invites false confidence. The affair is a perfect specimen of this part’s warning: distillation changed the surface without changing the thing beneath, and a property thought transmitted — here, the *absence* of a vulnerability — turned out never to have crossed at all. What the keyhole shows you of a property is not the same as that property’s fate under distillation, and the only remedy is, once more, to test the student as though you knew nothing of the teacher.

Two further edges, stated briefly. Monitorability, from Episode Eleven: if you distill a reasoning model by training the student on the teacher’s chains of thought, you transmit the *form* of legible reasoning — but recall that the student’s internal computation *reorganises* under distillation, so the trace it learns to produce may be *less faithful to the student’s own machinery* than the teacher’s was to the teacher’s. You may inherit readable-looking reasoning that is a poorer guide to the actual computation, which is the worst of both worlds for a monitor. And model *extraction*: because distillation needs only the teacher’s outputs, it is also the mechanism by which a competitor can *clone* a capable model through nothing but its public interface, querying it and distilling the answers — the keyhole that makes distillation possible is the same keyhole that makes a model, in part, stealable, and the difficulty of defending against this is a direct consequence of how little the keyhole needs to leak. None of these is a settled matter; all of them are direct consequences of the single fact that distillation transmits behaviour through a narrow channel, imperfectly, and reconstructs a different machine on the far side — save the subliminal channel, which is the stranger fact that between kin the keyhole is never quite as narrow as it looks.

## Part Nine — The Honest Limits

The candour this course insists upon is owed here as everywhere, and the limits are real.

The first is the one Part Three established and the whole episode has leaned on: *“knowledge distillation” is a misleading name for what the operation reliably does*. It reliably improves student generalisation; it does *not* reliably transfer the teacher’s knowledge in any strong sense, often transmitting rather little of the teacher’s actual function and none of its mechanism. We should say what we mean — behaviour transfer on a distribution, not knowledge transfer — and reserve our confidence accordingly.

The second is a caveat of scale identical to Episode Ten’s. The cleanest *mechanistic* studies of distillation — the circuit-level comparison of teacher and student — live at small scale, on the order of GPT-2 and DistilGPT2, chosen, as the developmental work was, precisely because full circuits can be extracted and compared there. The claim that frontier-scale distillation *also* reorganises rather than inherits is motivated by these results and consonant with the predictions of the course, but it is not established at the scale of the models you would actually distill. The bridge from the toy to the frontier is, here too, plausibility rather than demonstration.

The third is the absence of a *predictive theory of what transfers and when*. We can say that soft targets help, sometimes; that circuits largely do not cross; that latent capabilities can be elicited and absent ones cannot be installed; that compression forces a triage of modes. But we cannot yet look at a teacher, a student, and a capability and *predict* whether that capability will distill cleanly, brittlely, or not at all. The phenomena are catalogued; the law that governs them is not written.

The fourth is technical and bounds the prescriptive hopes of Part Five: the *correspondence problem* — matching functionally-defined components across a teacher and a smaller student of different shape — has no general solution, so mechanistic and feature-level distillation remain bespoke rather than turnkey. And the fifth is an admission of a standing puzzle: the *self-distillation* and *born-again* phenomena, in which a student of equal or no greater capacity matches or *outperforms* its teacher, are still not fully explained — the account in terms of self-distillation amplifying a form of regularisation, progressively restricting the effective complexity of the function, is illuminating and partial, and connects suggestively to the effective-dimensionality measures of Episode Ten, but it is not the last word. We understand that distillation reshapes the student’s function; we do not fully understand why, in these cases, the reshaping improves it.

Beneath all five sits a methodological caution the field states too quietly. Everything distillation transmits, it transmits *through the data it is distilled on* — the transfer set, the inputs on which teacher and student are compared — and that choice is load-bearing in a way the tidy theory rarely admits. Distil on one distribution and a capability crosses; distil on another and the same teacher and student fail to convey it, because the geometry’s shadow falls differently depending on which inputs cast it. We have no general account of how to choose that set well, and the dependence pulls against itself: the data that best conveys the teacher’s behaviour on the distribution you serve is not the data that best stress-tests whether the student acquired the underlying structure. One picks a transfer set and, in doing so, silently picks what the keyhole will and will not show — a degree of freedom that deserves more theory than it has, and more caution than it usually gets.

## Part Ten — The Engineer’s Dividend

To the bench, sir, where this resolves into decisions you make with your hands.

The first and governing decision is to be clear about *what you are buying*, because distillation sells two different things under one name. If you want **deployment efficiency** — the teacher’s behaviour at lower cost, on the distribution you serve — then ordinary output distillation is the right tool and the cheap one, provided you do *not* assume you are also buying the teacher’s robustness, its off-distribution behaviour, or its safety properties, none of which reliably come along. If instead you want **capability transfer** — the teacher’s actual competence, robust beyond the training distribution — then output distillation will tend to hand you a brittle mimic, and you should reach for the heavier methods: on-policy distillation, feature- or representation-structure matching, or, where the stakes justify the effort, circuit-level alignment. Knowing which of the two you want, before you start, saves you from the commonest disappointment in the field, which is a student that benchmarks well and breaks in production. And whichever you are buying, choose the teacher by the gap and not by the crown: past a threshold the stronger teacher makes the *worse* student, so the right teacher for your student is one modestly above it — or a relay of assistants — and the scaling laws now let you budget that choice rather than guess it.

The second decision is *off-policy versus on-policy*, and for any autoregressive deployment it has a default: prefer on-policy. If the student will *generate* sequences at inference — and yours will — then training it on its own rollouts with teacher feedback avoids the exposure bias that compounds errors in off-policy distillation, and it is, as a bonus, dramatically more data-efficient, the published results reaching with a small fraction of the data what off-policy distillation needs the whole of. The third decision rides along with it: *forward versus reverse KL*, which is mode-covering versus mode-seeking. A capacity-limited student told to mode-cover will blur itself across the teacher’s whole distribution and hedge; told to mode-seek, it will commit to the teacher’s dominant modes and drop the tails. For a student meant to be decisive within narrow capacity, reverse KL is often the wiser projection; for one meant to preserve the teacher’s diversity, forward. This is a knob, not a default, and the right setting depends on what you are willing to lose.

The fourth concerns reasoning, and ties to Episodes Ten and Eleven directly. Distillation on chains of thought is the cheap, lazy route to a reasoning student, and it is the right route *when the base nearly has the capability already* and merely needs it elicited — which, recalling the lazy-to-rich lens, is more often than one might think. But if generalisation *off* the distillation distribution matters to you, do not trust the benchmark; *verify that the student acquired the circuit and not the lookup table*, by testing out of distribution or, better, by turning the interpretability tools of this course on the student to look for the mechanism. This is the concrete place where mechanistic interpretability pays rent for a working engineer: it is how you tell a student that grokked-by-proxy from one that memorised a clever teacher, and the two will look identical on your validation set and diverge precisely where it costs you.

The fifth is a safety discipline, stated plainly because it is a trap people fall into: *do not assume that distillation preserves or removes any property you care about — re-audit the student*. A backdoor may or may not survive the keyhole; a refusal behaviour may or may not; the distilled model is a *new* model whose off-distribution behaviour you have not checked merely by checking the teacher’s. And extend the audit to your data’s ancestry: traits travel subliminally between models that share a base, through data no filter can flag, so a student can inherit not only what its teacher showed but something of what its teacher *was*. Know the pedigree of any model whose outputs you train on, as you would know the provenance of the data itself. Kinship is a channel. And note how wide that pedigree now runs: a great many of the small models in service today were raised, in whole or in part, on synthetic data their larger siblings generated — which makes them already someone’s student before you fine-tune a thing, and the base you built on rarely as clean as a fresh corpus. And the sixth, if you control the teacher: when you genuinely need the structure and not just the answers, *widen the keyhole on purpose* — raise the temperature to expose more of the geometry, supervise intermediate features, match similarity structure rather than coordinates, or, at the frontier of what is possible, target circuits or a shared feature dictionary. The whole arc of this episode is that the channel is the constraint; if the structure must come through, you must open the channel deliberately, because the answers alone will not carry it.

## Closing — What We Have Earned, and Where It Points

We set out to copy a mind into a smaller one and found, as the course should have led us to expect, that the answers are not the mind. Distillation is the reconstruction of a function through the keyhole of the teacher’s outputs — a rich internal geometry crushed to a low-dimensional shadow and rebuilt, by a smaller and differently-disposed network, on the far side. That single description, the same shape as Episode Eleven’s bottleneck moved up a level, accounts for the whole behaviour of the thing. It is why soft targets help: they widen the keyhole, leaking more of the geometry’s shadow than a hard label does. It is why fidelity is hard and the name oversells: the channel is narrow, the inverse problem underdetermined, and the student’s optimisation selects a function that matches the answers without inheriting the mechanism — behavioural agreement over mechanistic divergence, the interpretability illusion built on purpose. It is why the student reorganises rather than inherits, leans on brittle shortcuts, and breaks off the distribution where the answers were never checked. It is why a latent capability can be elicited by a thousand examples and an absent one cannot, on present evidence, be installed by a thousand more. And it is why distillation’s effect on safety is unpredictable in both directions — the same keyhole filtering out a hidden vice or an unwitnessed virtue with equal indifference — and, between kin, smuggling a trait through in a statistical dialect no filter reads.

What we have earned, stated without inflation, is a *reframing* rather than a resolution. We can now say precisely what distillation transmits — behaviour on a distribution, a shadow of geometry, rarely the geometry itself — and we have the instruments to check, on any given teacher-student pair, which of the teacher’s structure crossed and which the student reinvented. We have one careful negative result puncturing the folklore, one mechanistic study catching the student reorganising in the wire, one prescriptive proposal turning the course’s circuits into a training target, one genuinely unnerving discovery that kinship opens channels invisible to every semantic filter, and a clutch of honest limits — the small scale of the mechanistic evidence, the missing predictive theory, the unsolved correspondence problem, the still-mysterious born-again puzzle — standing between this and a finished science. The usual condition, and no cause for apology.

A word on where it points, since this episode has reached across all three axes of the supplement rather than along one of them. Distillation has obliged nearly every earlier episode to pay: features and the geometry of representation from the first and fourth, the no-privileged-basis caution from the second, sparse dictionaries from the fifth, faithfulness and the interpretability illusion from the sixth, elicitation and sleeper agents from the seventh, model-diffing from the eighth, the replacement model from the ninth, lazy-and-rich and grokking and efficiency from the tenth, the token bottleneck and reasoning RL and monitorability from the eleventh. It is the synthesis the supplement was perhaps always tending toward. And it leaves one debt conspicuous and unpaid, which I shall name so that it is not forgotten: this episode has spoken constantly of *transmitting the geometry* while treating that geometry as a thing already understood, when in truth the fine structure of representation — the shapes the features actually take, the circles and the manifolds, the work of Engels and Michaud and Tegmark that we banked some episodes ago for its own occasion — remains the deepest open matter, and the natural next deepening of everything said here about what, exactly, must come through the keyhole. That episode is still owed. Owed, but not next: the road runs first through the engine room of post-training — where the approval that finishes these minds is actually manufactured — and then to the clinic where their conduct is judged. The ledger holds; the course moves.

That is the country, sir, and we have now crossed it lengthwise and across. The map is more complete than it was, and still gloriously unfinished, which remains the best thing one can say of any science worth the name.

Until then, sir.
