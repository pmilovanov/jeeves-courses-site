# Induction Heads and the Origin of In-Context Learning

*A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability — Episode Three*

## A Promise, and a Surprise

Good day, sir.

We arrive at the episode I have been looking forward to, because it is the one where the abstractions of the first two episodes pay a concrete and rather startling dividend. Permit me to remind you what we have already established.

From the first episode, you have the induction head itself — the circuit by which a model, shown a passage in which some name was followed by some second name, will, on meeting that first name again, confidently produce the second. I sketched its mechanism there: the previous-token head writing its annotation, the induction head matching on it. From the second episode, you have the *grammar* that makes such a thing possible — the three lenses, the seam between *where to look* and *what to write*, and above all **composition**, the business of one head writing a note into the shared notebook that a later head then reads and acts upon. I argued, by walking up a ladder of toy models, that this particular circuit is impossible for a model with a single attention layer and becomes possible with two.

What I gave you in the first episode was the working sketch — true, but quick, the version one draws on the back of an envelope. Today I make it rigorous: I shall trace the induction head to its last wire — exactly which two heads conspire, exactly what each writes and reads, exactly why two layers and not one, and exactly which flavour of composition does the work — so that the back-of-the-envelope version becomes a thing you could, in principle, build. That is the first portion of the episode, and it is satisfying in the way a well-made clock is satisfying.

But the later portions are where I must ask you to brace yourself slightly, because they contain one of the genuinely surprising empirical findings in this young science — the kind of finding that, when researchers first saw it, made them sit up. It concerns the moment in a model’s *training* when this machinery comes into being. One might reasonably expect a capability to accrue gradually — the model getting a little better at pattern-completion with each passing hour of training, as a student improves at scales. That is not what happens. The ability appears *suddenly*, in a narrow window, announced by a visible kink in the training curve — and at that same moment, a much larger and more important capability switches on alongside it, as though the two were the same event seen from two angles. They very nearly are.

That larger capability is the one we call *in-context learning* — the model’s ability to learn from the contents of its prompt, within a single forward pass, with not one of its weights changed. It is the thing that makes a large language model *useful*, the thing that lets you teach it a task by example in the moment rather than by retraining it for a week. And the finding I shall lay before you is that a great deal of this seemingly miraculous capacity rests on the very humble mechanism we are about to dissect. The grandest trick in the repertoire and one of the plainest turn out to be, to a considerable degree, the same trick.

A claim of that size deserves to be earned rather than asserted, and the researchers who made it assembled the evidence with a thoroughness I intend to honour: not one argument but six, stacked from suggestive to surgical, and I shall give you all six. First, however, the clockwork.

## Part One — The Induction Head, Traced to Its Last Wire

I shall use the names we used in the first episode, for continuity and because they are agreeably absurd. We saw the mechanism sketched there; now we build it slowly enough to be sure of every joint. Suppose the model is reading a passage in which, some way back, the name **Honoria** was followed by the name **Glossop**. The text rolls on. And now, at the present position, the name **Honoria** appears *again*, and the model must guess what comes next. We — and the model, if it is any good — expect **Glossop**, because that is what followed Honoria the first time. The pattern, having occurred once, is expected to repeat.

I want to state the goal with care, because the whole mechanism is shaped to it. The model is at the second **Honoria**. It must somehow reach back, find the *earlier* place where Honoria occurred, look at *what came immediately after* it — Glossop — and bring *that* forward as its prediction. Notice the peculiar indirection. The model is not looking for Honoria; it already *is* at Honoria. It is looking for *the word that followed the previous Honoria*. It must attend not to a token but to a *position defined by its neighbour* — the position whose *predecessor* was Honoria. That indirection is precisely what a single attention layer cannot manage, and understanding *why* — not merely that two layers suffice, which you have, but why one cannot and two can — is the lesson.

Here is the machinery, in two heads across two layers.

**The first head, in an early layer, is a *previous-token head*.** Its job is humble and general: at every position, it attends to the position immediately before it — navigating, you now know, by the rotary clock or its positional kin — and copies some signature of that previous token into the present position’s notebook entry. It is one of those crisp, nameable heads from last episode’s field guide: its where-to-look machine does nothing cleverer than “attend one step back,” and its what-to-write machine copies a tag identifying the token it found there. So, in particular, at the position of the *first* **Glossop**, this head reaches back one step to **Honoria** and writes into Glossop’s entry a note that means, in the model’s private code, *“the token before me was Honoria.”* Glossop’s page in the notebook now carries, in addition to “I am Glossop,” the annotation “I was preceded by Honoria.” Mark that annotation. It is the linchpin.

**The second head, in a later layer, is the *induction head* proper.** It acts at the present position — the *second* **Honoria** — and it does something that is only possible because the previous-token head has already done its work. Recall from last episode the three flavours of composition, and recall that I asked you to hold *key composition* with particular care. Here is why.

The induction head forms its **query** from the present token. The present token is Honoria, and the induction head’s query lens is trained to convert “I am the token Honoria” into the question *“find me the position that was preceded by Honoria.”* That is the question it broadcasts. Now it compares this query, as every head does, against the **keys** at all earlier positions — and here is the crucial part: the keys it is comparing against are *not* the raw keys of the original tokens. They have been *modified by the previous-token head’s annotations*. The key at the first **Glossop** position no longer advertises merely “I am Glossop.” Because the previous-token head wrote “preceded by Honoria” into Glossop’s entry, and because the induction head reads its keys through a lens that picks up exactly that annotation, the key at Glossop now advertises *“I am a position that was preceded by Honoria.”*

And so the match is made. The induction head’s query — *seeking: preceded-by-Honoria* — aligns precisely with Glossop’s modified key — *offering: preceded-by-Honoria* — and the induction head attends, from the second Honoria, to the first Glossop. This is the *where to look* settled: the attention lands on exactly the position holding the word that followed Honoria last time. The field calls this behaviour **prefix matching** — the head matches the present context against the prefix of an earlier occurrence — and you will note that it is achieved entirely by the query-key circuit, the where-to-look machine, operating on annotations that an earlier head supplied. That is key composition in the flesh: the earlier head *relabelled the filing system*, and the later head’s query found the relabelled file.

The *what to write* is now almost anticlimactic, and it is handled by the induction head’s output-value circuit. Having attended to the Glossop position, the head copies Glossop’s identity forward — boosts, in the model’s final reckoning, the probability of the token Glossop. The field calls this second behaviour **copying**: having found the right position, faithfully reproduce what is there. And copying is just the output-value machine doing the plainest thing it can do — carry the cargo across unaltered.

So the induction head is, exactly, the marriage of two sub-abilities: **prefix matching** in the where-to-look machine — find the position whose past matches my present — and **copying** in the what-to-write machine — bring forward what you find there. Two simple competencies, in the two decoupled halves of one head, riding on an annotation laid down by a head one layer below. One honesty clause before the certificate is framed: the trace I have given is the classic construction, and it is not the only one nature uses — in some models the same behaviour is assembled along a different seam of composition, and real induction heads prove mechanically heterogeneous when examined closely. The one I traced is the canonical specimen, not the whole species.

Now I can discharge, rigorously, the debt from the ladder. *Why can one layer not do this?* Because the pivotal fact — “this position was preceded by Honoria” — is a *relationship between two positions*, and computing it requires an attention step: somebody must look back one token and write down what they saw. That is the previous-token head’s entire contribution, and it consumes one attention layer. Only *after* that fact is written into the notebook can a second head attend *on the basis of it*. A one-layer model has a single attention step; it must spend it either computing the relationship or using it, and it cannot do both. It is therefore stuck attending on the basis of *raw tokens* — which, as I showed you last time, yields the brittle skip-trigram and its characteristic bug. The induction head needs *two* steps because it needs to attend on the basis of something *previously computed*, and the only machinery for “previously computed and written down for later use” is depth. The induction head is the simplest interesting thing that genuinely requires the notebook to be marked up before it is read. It is, if you like, the *hello-world* of composition — the first program you can write that needs two clerks instead of one.

## Part Two — Measuring the Beast: The Telephone-Directory Test

Before we proceed to the surprise, a question of method, because it bears on everything after. I have described what an induction head does. How does one *find* them — how, confronting a model with thousands of heads, does one determine which are induction heads and which are not, without weeks of squinting at attention maps? The field’s answer is a diagnostic of such cunning simplicity that it deserves a minute of its own, and it will teach you something about experimental design in this science.

The trouble with testing pattern-completion on ordinary text is that ordinary text is full of alibis. Show the model “the cat sat on the” twice and watch it predict “mat” the second time, and you have proven nothing about induction — the model might predict “mat” from grammar, from semantics, from the sheer statistics of English, with no reference to the earlier occurrence at all. To isolate the mechanism, one must construct a text in which *every* alternative explanation has been removed — in which the *only* conceivable way to predict the next token is to consult the earlier repeat.

The construction: take a sequence of *completely random* tokens — words and word-fragments drawn from the vocabulary by lot, with no grammar, no meaning, no statistical regularity whatever — and then *repeat the sequence*, splicing a second copy directly after the first. The result is a passage with all the literary merit of a telephone directory read twice, which is precisely its virtue. In the first half, the model can predict nothing; every next token is a fresh roll of the dice, and the model’s loss on it is dismal, as it should be. But in the *second* half, every token is, in principle, perfectly predictable — *provided* the model can find the corresponding position in the first half and copy what follows it. No grammar will help; there is none. No world knowledge will help; the sequence means nothing. Prefix-match-and-copy is the only road to a correct answer, by construction.

This gives the field its two standard instruments, and you met their names in the mechanism. For any head, one measures a **prefix-matching score**: feed the repeated random sequence, and ask what fraction of the head’s attention, from each token in the second half, lands on the token *just after* that token’s earlier occurrence — the Glossop position, in our running example. And one measures a **copying score**: ask to what degree the head’s what-to-write machine, given what it finds there, actually promotes that token in the model’s output. A head scoring high on both is an induction head, certified — not by anecdote or by a suggestive heat-map, but by behaviour on a stimulus engineered to admit no other explanation. You will recognise the family resemblance to the synthetic curves of the first episode’s vision anatomists: when nature’s stimuli are ambiguous, manufacture unambiguous ones.

Run this census across models and a striking regularity appears: induction heads are *everywhere*. Every transformer language model of sufficient depth that anyone has examined contains them — small models, frontier models, models of different architectures and pedigrees, models trained by rival organisations on different data. Usually not one but a population of them, scattered across the middle layers. It is universality again, and in its strongest form yet: prefix-match-and-copy appears to be a solution so favoured by the task of language prediction that gradient descent finds it *every single time*. When a mechanism is rediscovered by every model that has ever been trained, one begins to suspect it is load-bearing. The next two parts are the demonstration of just how load-bearing.

## Part Three — The Bump: A Capability That Arrives All at Once

Now the surprise I promised, and it concerns *training* — the long process by which a model’s weights are gradually adjusted, over billions of examples, to predict text better and better.

One pictures this process as smooth. The model’s error — its *loss*, the single number measuring how badly it predicts the next token, averaged over a great deal of text — starts high and is driven steadily down, hour by hour, as the weights improve. And in the large, that picture is correct: the loss curve is, for the most part, a smooth and unremarkable descent, the kind of diminishing-returns slope you would sketch with your eyes closed.

But when researchers looked closely at this descent — in a careful study from twenty twenty-two that trained a whole family of transformers, from one layer up to many, and watched their innards as they learned — they found that it is not *perfectly* smooth. Early in training, there is a brief, distinct interruption: a short window in which the loss drops *faster* than the surrounding trend — a small but unmistakable kink in what should be a featureless slope. Before the window, the curve is doing one thing; after it, the curve is doing another; and across the window, something *changes*. The field has come to call this feature the **induction bump**, and the reason for the name is the heart of the matter.

When the researchers asked what was happening *inside* the model across exactly that window — what changed in the machinery between the start of the bump and its end — the answer was clean and specific. *That is when the induction heads form.* Before the window, the model has no induction heads; the prefix-matching scores of all its heads sit at the floor. Across the window, the wiring assembles itself — the two heads find their roles, the composition between them clicks into place, and the prefix-matching scores of particular heads climb from nothing to high. After the window, the induction heads are present and functioning. The bump in the loss curve is the *signature*, visible from outside, of a specific mechanism crystallising inside.

The timing has a regularity worth reporting. Across the family of models in the study — different sizes, different depths — the window arrived at roughly the same *age*: somewhere between about two and a half and five billion tokens into training. Early days, by the standards of a training run that consumes trillions; the machinery is assembled in the model’s infancy and merely refined thereafter. There is something quietly comic in the predictability of it — rather as one can forecast, to within a term, when an undergraduate will discover Nietzsche — but the scientific point is serious: a *developmental milestone*, arriving on schedule, in every member of the family.

I want you to feel why this is remarkable and not merely tidy. We are accustomed, in machine learning, to capabilities that improve *continuously* — a little better with each step, no special moments. What the induction bump shows is a capability that arrives *discontinuously* — a qualitative reorganisation of the model’s internal machinery, compressed into a narrow window, with a *before* and an *after* that differ in kind and not merely in degree. The model does not get *gradually* better at pattern completion. It is, for a while, simply unable to do it; then, over a short span, the apparatus for doing it *assembles*; and thereafter it can. This is, to date, the cleanest and most thoroughly understood example in the field of what people loosely call an *emergent capability* — a behaviour that appears abruptly — pinned down not by hand-waving but by an identified mechanism whose formation you can watch and whose moment of formation coincides exactly with the behaviour’s onset. We shall return to the larger question of emergence before the episode is out, because the induction bump turns out to have relatives, and the family portrait is instructive. But first, the main event.

## Part Four — In-Context Learning, and the Six Lines of Evidence

Here is the main event. To reach it I must first say, with some care, what *in-context learning* is, because the phrase is used loosely and the precise version is what matters.

A language model has two utterly different ways of acquiring a capability, and they must not be confused. The first is *training* — the slow adjustment of the weights I just described, which happens once, in the laboratory, over weeks, and is then frozen. When you converse with a deployed model, its weights are fixed; nothing you say changes them. The second way is the one that operates *at the moment you use it*, with the weights frozen: the model adjusts its behaviour *in response to the contents of its prompt*. You show it three examples of a task in the prompt, and it performs the task on the fourth item — not because anything was retrained, but because the examples are *sitting there in the context*, and the model, reading them, conditions its next prediction upon them. This second mode — *improvement in performance that comes from information present in the context, with no change to the weights* — is **in-context learning**. It is “learning” only in the loose sense that the model behaves as though it has learned; mechanically, it is a single forward pass that happens to be very good at exploiting the patterns laid out in its input. It is the entire basis of what people call *few-shot prompting* — teach by example, in the moment.

Now, the study did not only locate the induction bump. It also measured, across training, a quantity it called the **in-context learning score** — and I shall give you the actual recipe, because it is clever and concrete. The idea is to ask: *how much better does the model predict a token late in a passage than a token early in the same passage?* A token near the start must be predicted with very little context to draw on. A token far in can be predicted with the whole preceding passage available — and a model capable of in-context learning should exploit that accumulation. So the researchers took, as their score, the difference between the model’s loss at the *five-hundredth* token of a passage and its loss at the *fiftieth* — how much the intervening four hundred and fifty tokens of context actually *bought*. A model that cannot learn from context predicts the five-hundredth token no better than the fiftieth; the score sits near zero. A model that learns from context well predicts the late token far better; the score is large. That gap, tracked through training, is the in-context learning score.

And here is the finding, stated as plainly as I can manage. *The in-context learning score jumps — sharply, in a narrow window — at exactly the moment of the induction bump.* The two are not merely correlated in some vague way; they coincide in time, step for step, in every model of the family. At the precise span of training during which the induction heads assemble, the model’s general ability to profit from its context *switches on*. Before: the model treats late tokens little better than early ones. After: it exploits context dramatically better, and the in-context learning score, once it leaps, scarcely changes for the remainder of training — the capability arrives in that window and arrives essentially complete.

The natural and irresistible hypothesis is that the first *causes* the second — that in-context learning, at least in large part, *is* the generalised operation of induction machinery. Now, a coincidence in time, however crisp, is a correlation, and the researchers knew it. What gives the paper its lasting authority is that it did not stop there. It assembled *six* distinct lines of argument, in ascending order of surgical directness, and the architecture of the case is worth laying out — two of the lines at length, for they are the most instructive, and the rest at a brisk march — partly because the conclusion matters, and partly because it is a model of how to argue a causal claim about the inside of a neural network, which is a skill this course means to teach you by example.

*The first line* you have: **co-occurrence**. The heads form, and the capability arrives, in the same window, in model after model. Suggestive, no more — but suggestive in model after model of the family.

*The second line* is my favourite, and it is the one I particularly invite you to admire: **co-perturbation**. If the formation of induction heads *causes* the onset of in-context learning, then anything that *moves* the one should drag the other along with it. So the researchers reached into the architecture and moved it. Recall why a one-layer model cannot form induction heads: the pivotal fact — “I was preceded by Honoria” — must be computed by one attention step before another can use it, and one layer has only the single step. The researchers then introduced a small architectural cheat, a modification the field calls a *smeared key*: each position’s key is blended with a fraction of the *previous* position’s key, so that every key arrives with a whiff of its predecessor already mixed in. The annotation the previous-token head exists to write is now supplied, gratis, by the architecture itself — and a *one-layer* model, which could never form an induction head honestly, can suddenly implement prefix-matching with its single step. The prediction writes itself: if the hypothesis is right, smeared one-layer models should *acquire* the in-context learning transition that ordinary one-layer models entirely lack, and in deeper models the cheat should let the machinery form *earlier*, dragging the capability’s onset earlier with it. Both predictions held. The phase change moved about like furniture, and the capability moved with it, everywhere, in lockstep. When you can shift an effect at will by perturbing its hypothesised cause, sir, you are no longer in the suggestion business.

*The third line* is the surgical one: **ablation**. Take a small trained model, reach in, and *disable* precisely the induction heads — switch off those components and no others — and measure what happens to in-context learning. It degrades, substantially. Knock out the mechanism and the capacity it is hypothesised to carry falls away with it. This is the interventional logic we shall study as a formal discipline in our sixth episode, here in its plainest form.

*The fourth line*: in small models, the mechanism is not a black box but a *traced circuit* — the very clockwork of Part One. The claim “these heads perform pattern completion” rests not on correlation but on an explicit, verified algorithm. One is not arguing that an unknown something does the job; one knows what the something is and how.

*The fifth line* anticipates the next part of this episode: induction heads, examined closely in larger models, turn out to perform not only literal copying but a spectrum of *more abstract* pattern-completions — matching on similarity rather than identity, completing structural analogies, even carrying patterns across languages. The mechanism, that is, has visible *range* — enough range to plausibly underwrite a general capability rather than a parlour trick.

*And the sixth line* is the honest extrapolation: in *large* models — where the full circuitry is too tangled to trace and too redundant to cleanly ablate — the same outward signatures persist: the same phase change, at the same developmental moment, with induction heads measurably forming across it. The causal proof lives in the small models; the large ones exhibit every symptom consistent with the same diagnosis. The researchers were scrupulous about the gradient of confidence — demonstration in the small, strong inference in the large — and I shall be equally scrupulous on their behalf.

So: it is *not* established that induction heads are the *whole* of in-context learning — that would be too neat, and later work has indeed found further layers to the story, with more sophisticated pattern-matching machinery sharing the load in large models. The responsible statement, and the one the six lines support, is this: a substantial and causally-demonstrated *portion* of in-context learning is carried by induction heads and their relatives; the induction head is the *foundational* instance — the simplest member of a family of context-exploiting circuits, the one whose birth marks the capability’s onset and whose removal degrades it. When you next watch a model apparently *learn* a task from three examples in its prompt, I would have you picture, underneath the apparent magic, a great parliament of prefix-matchers reaching back through the context, finding the places where the present pattern occurred before, and copying forward what followed. Much of the magic is *that*, generalised and multiplied. Jeeves does not oversell; neither does he undersell, and this finding deserves its full weight.

## Part Five — Phase Changes at Large: Grokking, and the Question of Emergence

I said the induction bump has relatives, and the most celebrated of them deserves its place in this episode, both because it is a marvellous story and because together the two findings reframe one of the noisiest debates in the field — the debate over *emergence*.

The story is called **grokking**, and I shall tell it here only far enough to set it beside the bump, because the tenth episode owns it in full — the clock opened gear by gear, the reasons it waits so long, and the theory of simplicity underneath. The short form: a small transformer, set the almost insulting task of modular addition, memorised its training examples quickly and then sat at *chance* on the unseen ones for an interminable stretch — long past the point any reasonable person would have stopped the run — before test performance abruptly leapt from chance to essentially perfect. Crammed first, understood later, on a timescale no undergraduate could get away with. The mechanistic interpreters took the trained model apart and found it had not learned addition the way a schoolchild does: it had reinvented the problem as *rotation around circles*, composing angles and reading off where the combined rotation landed — trigonometry, discovered unprompted by gradient descent. And the instruments they built from that mechanism — **progress measures**, gauges reading how far the circular circuitry had *formed*, as distinct from how well the model scored — told the story the test accuracy had concealed: the general algorithm had been assembling *gradually*, beneath the memorised crust, for the whole of the silent period, and the final thunderclap was merely the model cleaning house, discarding the now-redundant lookup table. Continuous construction within; discontinuous behaviour without.

Now set grokking beside the induction bump and you have the makings of a sane position on *emergence* — a topic on which a great deal of ink has been spilled, much of it warm. One camp points to capabilities that appear abruptly as models scale or train, and speaks of unpredictable leaps. Another camp retorts, with some justice, that many reported leaps are artefacts of the measuring stick — that a harsh pass-or-fail metric will register a cliff where the underlying competence climbed a gentle ramp. The mechanistic findings let one say something more precise than either slogan. Yes: underlying structure tends to form *gradually*, and gauges built from the mechanism show smooth progress where behavioural metrics show nothing — the mirage camp has a point. And yes: the *behavioural* consequences of that formation can nonetheless be genuinely, consequentially abrupt — a circuit that is ninety per cent assembled confers nothing; assembled, it confers everything; the bump in the loss curve is not an artefact of anyone’s metric. Both things are true, at different levels of description, and the appearance of contradiction was only ever the result of watching a construction site exclusively from the street. The practical moral for anyone charged with forecasting what the next model will do — and forecasting is, increasingly, a safety discipline — is that behaviour is a lagging indicator. If you wish to see capabilities coming, you must learn to read the progress of the machinery, not merely the score on the test. That is an argument for this entire field, made by two small models that learned, respectively, to copy and to count.

## Part Six — Beyond Literal Copying: Soft Induction and Task Vectors

I have, so far, described the induction head doing something quite literal: it finds an exact repeat of the current token and copies the exact token that followed. Stated so baldly, it sounds like a parlour trick of limited use — and if that were all it did, it would not underwrite in-context learning, because the tasks we set models in their prompts are rarely a matter of copying verbatim repeats. So let me show you how the same machinery, loosened, reaches a great deal further than literal repetition. This generalisation is the fifth of our six lines of evidence, given its proper due — and it is what carries induction from a curiosity to a workhorse.

The first loosening is *fuzziness*. Nothing in the mechanism requires the match to be exact. The prefix-matching step is, recall, a comparison of a query against keys in the geometry of the residual stream — and that geometry, as the first episode insisted, encodes *meaning* as direction, not merely token identity. So an induction head can match on *similarity* rather than identity: it can attend back not only to the literal previous occurrence of the current token, but to occurrences of *similar* tokens, or tokens playing a *similar role*. Researchers have found heads that do exactly this — *soft* or *semantic* induction heads, which complete patterns over paraphrases and near-repeats, not just over verbatim ones. The clerk no longer needs the page to be word-for-word identical; a page *about the same thing* will do. And the family’s range runs further than paraphrase: among the specimens documented in large models are heads performing what can only be called *translation induction* — completing a pattern laid down in one language when it recurs in another, matching “the same thing said before” across the boundary of the language it was said in. The pattern being matched, evidently, lives at the level of meaning, where the first episode taught us the directions are.

The second loosening is *abstraction of the pattern itself*. Consider a prompt that establishes not a repeated token but a repeated *structure* — say, a list in which each line gives a country and then its capital, several times over, and then gives a country and stops, expecting the capital. There is no literal repeat here; “France” never appeared before. But there is a *positional and relational regularity* — “the slot after a country-name, in this list, is filled by a capital” — and induction-like machinery, attending to the *structure* of the preceding lines, can carry that regularity forward and fill the slot. This is the bridge from copying to genuine few-shot learning: the model treats your worked examples as a pattern to be continued, and continues it. *That* is how three examples in a prompt teach a fourth: the model induces the regularity that the examples instantiate and applies it to the new case.

And now I come to a finding I particularly want you to carry forward, because it links this episode to our final one and because it is, on its own, rather beautiful. When a model is given a few-shot prompt — several worked examples of some task — researchers have discovered that the model often *compresses the task itself into a compact internal representation*, a single direction-laden vector sitting in the residual stream at a particular position, which encodes *what the task is*. The field calls these **task vectors** or **function vectors**. The remarkable demonstrations are two, and they are mirror images. First, you can *extract* this vector — read it out of the model as it processes a few-shot prompt — and find that it stands for the task in a portable way. Second, and more striking, you can *inject* it: take that extracted vector, drop it into the model on a *completely different* prompt that contains *no examples at all*, and the model will *perform the task anyway*, as though it had been shown the examples. Hand it the function vector distilled from twenty antonym pairs, give it the bare word “hot” with no examples in sight, and out comes “cold” — the lesson administered, as it were, by injection rather than instruction. The examples have been distilled into a thing you can carry from one context to another and *install*. The finding has been replicated across model families, and the vectors even *compose*: certain pairs of task vectors, added together, yield the combined task.

Hold that, sir. We have just seen, in passing, the seed of the entire technique that our seventh episode is built around — the practice of *steering* a model by adding a chosen vector into its residual stream to change what it does. Here it arrives organically: the model, learning a task from examples, manufactures exactly the sort of object that we will later learn to manufacture ourselves and use as a control lever. The thing we will do deliberately to *steer* the model is something the model already does to *teach itself*. I shall say no more now; I merely plant the flag.

## Part Seven — What Is the Learning in In-Context Learning?

Before the engineering, a question with more philosophy in it than this series usually permits itself, though I shall keep the philosophy on a short lead. We have established that induction machinery carries much of in-context learning. But what *is* in-context learning, at bottom? When a model improves at a task from examples in its prompt, what kind of process is occurring? The field entertains three answers, they are not as compatible as they first appear, and the dispute among them is one of the liveliest in the science — so you should know the positions, sir, if only to nod at the correct moments.

The first answer is the one this episode has been building: **retrieval**. In-context learning is prefix-match-and-copy, generalised — the model improves with context because it can find where the present situation occurred before and reuse what worked. On this view, nothing resembling *learning* happens at inference; there is only an increasingly sophisticated lookup, softened by semantics, abstracted over structure. The induction family is the mechanism, and the magic is a parliament of librarians.

The second answer is more startling, and it arrived from a deliberately stripped-down experiment. Researchers trained transformers, from scratch, not on language but on bare mathematics: prompts consisting of input–output pairs from some function — points from a random straight line, say — followed by a fresh input wanting its output. Nothing to retrieve here in any ordinary sense; every prompt presents a *new* function, never seen in training, and the model must estimate it from the in-context points alone. And the trained models do it — for linear functions they match, almost exactly, the predictions of the *optimal* classical estimator, the least-squares fit a statistician would compute; and they extend the feat to sparse functions and even small decision trees, in each case approximating the appropriate textbook algorithm. The model behaves, in other words, not like a librarian but like a *statistician*: it appears to be running an estimation procedure, inside the forward pass, on data supplied at inference time.

And the theorists promptly made the startling part respectable. It was shown, by explicit construction, that an attention layer of a particularly simple kind can implement *one step of gradient descent* on a regression problem laid out in its context — the queries, keys, and values arranged so that the layer’s output is precisely the prediction after a single optimisation step, with further layers supplying further steps. The architecture, that is, can contain a small working model of its own manufacture — a watch with a tiny watchmaker inside — and trained toy models were found, in the simplest linear settings, to have converged to configurations approximating this — though how far the correspondence survives in full softmax transformers on realistic data remains genuinely contested. On this second view, in-context learning is *learning* in the full technical sense: an optimisation procedure, executed in activations rather than weights, fitting the data in the prompt.

The third answer splits the difference with Bayesian decorum: the model neither retrieves nor fits, but *infers* — it treats the prompt’s examples as evidence about *which task*, from the great distribution of tasks met in training, it currently inhabits, and conditions accordingly. Recognition, rather than learning — the examples do not teach the model the task so much as remind it which of its existing competences is wanted. The task vectors of the last part sit very comfortably in this picture: a compact internal answer to the question “what is being asked of me?”, distilled from the evidence and then acted upon.

Where does the truth lie? The honest answer — and the one the evidence currently supports — is that the question “which one?” is probably malformed. The mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and a large model trained on the chaos of human text has every incentive to assemble all three: retrieval where the context literally contains the answer, recognition where the task is one of the training distribution’s old chestnuts, and something approaching genuine estimation where the prompt presents novel structure that must actually be fit. The stripped-down experiments prove the architecture *can* implement the statistician; the induction evidence proves real language models *do* implement the librarian, abundantly; and the contests between the accounts in realistic settings remain an active front, with results suggesting the blend shifts with scale, with task, and with how far the prompt strays from anything training resembled. Whether the model learns in context or merely recognises in context is the sort of distinction a philosopher savours and an engineer is tempted to skip — but resist the temptation, sir, for it has a practical edge: a librarian fails when the library lacks the book, a statistician fails when the data are too few or too foul, and the two failures call for different remedies in your prompts. Knowing which machinery you are feeding is the beginning of feeding it well — which brings us, conveniently, to the engineering.

## Part Eight — The Engineer’s Dividend

You are an engineer, sir, who must coax reliable behaviour out of these models in production, and I would be neglecting my duties if I let this episode pass as mere natural history. The mechanics we have dissected have direct, exploitable consequences for anyone designing prompts, building evaluation pipelines, or chasing down the strange failures that large language models are heir to. Let me draw out five.

*First, prompt-format consistency is not a cosmetic preference; it is a mechanical lever.* Because few-shot learning works by the model inducing the *regularity* across your examples, the model is acutely sensitive to the *structure* in which you present them. If your three worked examples share a rigid format — the same delimiter, the same ordering of fields, the same shape of answer — you are handing the prefix-matching machinery a clean pattern to lock onto, and it will continue that pattern crisply. If your examples are formatted inconsistently, the regularity is muddied, the induction is hazier, and your results degrade for reasons that have nothing to do with the model’s underlying competence and everything to do with the fact that you gave the pattern-matcher a smudged template. When you find that a trivial formatting change to your prompt moves your evaluation numbers, this is why. You have not changed what the model knows; you have changed how legible the pattern is to the machinery that exploits patterns.

*Second, example selection and ordering matter, and matter mechanically.* The induction machinery attends back through the context, and the geometry of attention is not uniform over position — recency and salience tell. Which examples you include shapes which regularities are available to be induced; the *order* in which you place them shapes which are most readily attended to. This is the mechanical substance behind the well-known and often-frustrating sensitivity of few-shot performance to the choice and arrangement of demonstrations. It is not caprice. It is prefix-matching responding, exactly as it must, to what you have laid before it and how.

*Third — and this one is a direct diagnostic — the degenerate repetition loops that models occasionally fall into bear the fingerprints of induction running away with itself.* You will have seen it: a model, having by mischance produced a short repeated phrase, locks into reproducing it again and again, unable to escape. Consider what induction does in that situation. The repeated phrase is now a pattern *in the context*, occurring twice — and induction, finding the present position matches the start of the earlier repeat, confidently predicts the *continuation* of the repeat. Which extends the repeat. Which strengthens the pattern. Which makes the next induction prediction more confident still. The very mechanism that lets the model usefully continue a deliberate pattern will, given an accidental one, drive it into a self-reinforcing rut. When you reach for a repetition penalty or tune your sampling to break such loops, it is very likely this runaway prefix-match-and-copy you are countering — a mechanically compelling account, which I mark honestly as diagnosis-by-mechanism rather than traced verdict; the desk keeps a laboratory with your name on it, should you care to convict. Knowing the mechanism tells you *why* those remedies work and where to apply them.

*Fourth, the telephone-directory test belongs in your evaluation kit.* The repeated-random-sequence diagnostic of Part Two is not only how researchers certify induction heads; it doubles as a brutally honest probe of a deployed model’s *effective* context. A model may advertise a context window of some grand length; whether its copying machinery actually operates across that whole span — whether a pattern planted a hundred thousand tokens back can still be matched and continued — is an empirical question, and the synthetic copy task answers it without the confounds that infest natural-text benchmarks. The fashionable retrieval evaluations, your needles in haystacks, are at bottom tests of this same machinery; the synthetic version is the distilled spirit. When long-context behaviour degrades in production, testing whether induction still reaches across the distance is a sensible first question, and a cheap one.

*Fifth, and most generally: the phrase “the model learned it from the prompt” should now resolve, in your mind, from magic into mechanism.* This is the real engineering dividend, and it is the same one this whole course is built to pay. When a colleague says a model “picked up the task from the examples,” you are now entitled to ask the sharper question — *by what machinery?* — and to reason about prompt design as the act of feeding a specific, comprehensible apparatus rather than the act of supplicating an oracle. A vague mental model of a language model produces superstitious prompt engineering: cargo-cult incantations that worked once and are repeated without understanding. A mechanical model produces *engineering*: hypotheses about why a prompt works, predictions about what will break it, and principled fixes when it does. The difference between those two stances is the difference this series exists to make.

## Closing — What We Have Earned, and Where It Points

Let us gather the episode.

The **induction head**, traced to the wire: a **previous-token head** annotates every position with its predecessor; an **induction head** above, by **key composition**, matches its query against the relabelled keys — **prefix matching** in the where-machine, **copying** in the what-machine — and the whole construction is impossible in one layer for the ladder’s exact reason: the pivotal relationship must be computed and written down before it can be attended upon. The **telephone-directory test** certifies such heads on a stimulus that admits no other explanation, and finds them in every sufficiently deep model ever examined. The machinery is born *suddenly* — the **induction bump**, arriving on schedule in every member of a model family — and at that same moment the model’s capacity for **in-context learning** switches on. A claim supported by six lines of evidence from co-occurrence to the smeared-key perturbation that moved the phase change about like furniture. Beside the bump we set **grokking** — held mostly in trust for the tenth episode — and drew the sane position on *emergence*: structure forms continuously within, behaviour changes abruptly without, and the forecaster must learn to watch the machinery rather than the scoreboard. We loosened the mechanism into *soft* and *structural* induction and the portable **task vector** — the seed of the seventh episode’s steering — weighed the librarian, the statistician, and the Bayesian as accounts of what the learning *is* (a large model keeps all three on staff), and banked five dividends, from format discipline to the telephone-directory probe of a context window’s honest reach.

There is, however, a debt running quietly underneath everything we have done in these three episodes, and it is time to face it squarely. I have spoken throughout as though *features* — the model’s units of meaning, the directions in the notebook — were simply *there* to be found: the “Honoria” direction, the “preceded-by” annotation, the “French” feature. I have helped myself to them freely. But I told you, in the first episode, a disquieting thing: that the model packs *far more* features into the notebook than the notebook has dimensions to hold them straight — that meanings are crammed together at slight angles, overlapping, *in superposition*. If that is so, then the tidy named directions I have been pointing to are not lying about cleanly, one per axis, waiting to be read. They are folded together in a way that makes finding any one of them genuinely hard — and makes the very question “how many features does this model have?” subtler than it sounds.

In our next episode we confront superposition directly and rigorously. I shall show you a model small enough to understand *completely* — a toy built precisely so that we may watch it choose to fold its features together — and from it we shall learn *when* a model resorts to superposition, *what geometric shapes* the folded features arrange themselves into, and a genuinely unsettling fact: that a model can perform real computation upon features *while they remain folded*, never unpacking them at all. It is the episode that explains why reading these machines is hard in the first place — and it is the necessary groundwork for the microscope we build in the episode after. I shall have it ready.

Until then, sir.
