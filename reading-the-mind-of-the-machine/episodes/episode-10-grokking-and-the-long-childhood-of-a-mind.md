# Grokking, and the Long Childhood of a Mind

*A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability — Episode Ten*

*An addition made with the Supplement, revised for the Second Edition. The course proper closed at Episode Seven; Episodes Eight and Nine toured the engine room — the instruments by which a thought is read and the wiring diagram into which they assemble. What follows turns from the anatomy of the finished machine to the question of how the machine came to be: not how it runs, but how it grew. One episode, on the long childhood of a circuit.*

## The Student Who Passed the Exam and Kept Studying

Good day, sir.

Permit me to describe a scene that ought not to occur, and does. You are training a small network on a clean, finite task — let us say modular arithmetic, the addition of two numbers under a fixed modulus, which has the great convenience of admitting either an honest answer or a dishonest one. You train. Within a few thousand steps the network reaches perfect accuracy on the training set. Every example you have shown it, it now gets right. By every convention of the trade you should stop here; the loss on held-out data has flattened to the value one expects of a machine that has simply memorised its flashcards, and continued training is, the orthodoxy holds, a waste of electricity at best and active harm at worst.

You do not stop. Through carelessness, or stubbornness, or because the cluster was already booked, you let it run — not for a little longer, but for a hundred times longer. The training accuracy, already perfect, has nowhere to go and stays there. And then, absurdly late, long after any reasonable person had pronounced the matter settled, the held-out accuracy — flat at chance for the entire interminable middle of the run — lurches off the floor and climbs, over a few thousand steps, to perfect. The network, which had merely been *reciting*, abruptly begins to *understand*. It has, in the term the field borrowed from a science-fiction novel and has been faintly embarrassed by ever since, **grokked**.

I open the episode here because this single misbehaviour is the loose thread that, pulled, unravels a great deal. It tells us that *fitting the data* and *learning the algorithm* are two different events, separated in this case by a yawning gulf of training in which, to all outward appearance, nothing whatever is happening. It tells us that the loss curve — that scalar we squint at all day — is a profoundly unreliable narrator of what is going on inside the model. And it raises the question that organises everything to follow: if the machine is the same machine at the end of the boring middle as at the start of the sudden rise, what *changed*, and why did it wait so long to change it?

The honest answer is that, in this one toy case, we now know — to the wire, in the manner of Episode Six. We can name the algorithm the network discovers, watch it assemble part by part beneath the flat surface of the loss, and say precisely why the surface stayed flat while the assembly proceeded. That worked example is the spine of this episode. Around it I shall arrange the broader and more important claim it points toward: that training is not the smooth descent the loss curve depicts, but an *embryology* — a sequence of discrete births, of circuits crystallising one after another — and that a young and ambitious corner of the field has begun building instruments to detect those births as they happen. Whether that ambition is realised is, I shall be candid, undecided. But the worked example is real, and we begin there.

A peculiar subject, sir. In we go.

## Part One — The Phenomenon, Precisely

Let us pin the thing down before we explain it, since a good deal of loose talk attends grokking and it will pay to have the bones laid bare.

The phenomenon was first reported cleanly in a 2022 paper by Alethea Power and colleagues, then at OpenAI, under a title that says the whole of it: generalisation *beyond* overfitting, on small algorithmic datasets. The recipe is austere. Take a task with a crisp right answer and a small, exactly specifiable set of possible inputs — modular addition is the canonical one, but modular subtraction, multiplication, division, permutation composition, and assorted other group operations all serve. Withhold some fraction of the input pairs as a test set. Train a small transformer, or even a humble multilayer perceptron, with gradient descent and — this detail matters more than it first appears — a dose of **weight decay**, the regulariser that gently penalises the network for carrying large weights.

What follows divides into two regimes separated by a long intermission. In the first, the training loss plummets to near zero while the test loss does nothing of interest — it may even rise, the very signature of overfitting that every textbook warns against. The network has, in this phase, found a *memorising solution*: a lookup table, in effect, that stores each training answer without any structure that would let it answer a question it has not seen. Then comes the intermission — thousands, sometimes tens of thousands, of steps in which the training loss is pinned at the floor and the test loss sits, flat and indifferent, telling you nothing. And then the second regime: the test loss falls, often steeply, and the network arrives at a *generalising solution* — a genuine procedure that computes the answer for inputs it was never shown.

Three features of this deserve emphasis, because each affronts a working assumption of the trade.

The first is the *delay* itself, and its sheer scale. This is not a slightly tardy generalisation that a more patient early-stopping criterion would have caught. The gap between fitting and understanding can span orders of magnitude in training time. The orthodoxy of early stopping — halt when held-out performance plateaus — does not merely underperform here; it reliably stops you in the exact middle of the intermission, moments, as it were, before the curtain rises.

The second is the *abruptness* of the rise. The generalising solution does not fade in. It arrives as something close to a phase transition — a qualitative change of state, more like water freezing than like a pot slowly warming. We shall see this abruptness is, in a sense to be made precise, partly an artefact of *where we are looking*; but as seen on the test-accuracy curve, it is genuinely a cliff.

The third is the dependence on weight decay, and this is the first real clue to mechanism. Remove the regulariser, in many of these setups, and grokking does not occur: the network sits forever in its memorising solution, content, ungeneralising, declining to improve. The pressure that eventually drives it out of the lookup table and into the algorithm is, at least in this class of experiments, the steady downward pressure on the size of its weights. Hold that thought — *something about the generalising solution is preferred by a force that rewards smallness* — for it is the hinge on which the leading explanations turn.

That is the phenomenon as observed from without: the psychology of grokking. Satisfying as far as it goes, which is not far. To see what is actually happening we must do what this course always does when the outside view exhausts itself, and open the machine.

## Part Two — Opening the Clock

The decisive look inside came in 2023, from Neel Nanda and colleagues, in work whose title — progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability — promises rather less than it delivers. What it delivers is a complete reverse-engineering of the algorithm a one-layer transformer learns for modular addition, and it is, to my eye, one of the most satisfying objects in the entire literature, because it is small enough to understand completely and strange enough to be worth the trouble.

Here is the task in its canonical form: given two numbers *a* and *b*, each drawn from the integers below some prime modulus — 113, in the famous instance — compute *(a + b)* under that modulus. A lookup table would simply store the answer for every pair. The network, given enough time and weight decay, declines to do anything so pedestrian. It discovers, instead, to compute the sum *trigonometrically*.

I shall describe the mechanism plainly, because it is the part worth carrying home. The network’s embedding — the matrix that turns each input number into a vector — learns to place the numbers *on a circle*. Not a circle you can see directly in the raw coordinates, but one that appears the moment you take the discrete Fourier transform of the embedding: the network represents each number *k* by, among other things, the quantities *cos(ωk)* and *sin(ωk)* for a handful of particular frequencies *ω*. That is to say, it maps each integer to an *angle*, and stores that angle as a point on the unit circle in a two-dimensional plane carved out of the residual stream.

This should sound familiar, sir. It is precisely the cyclic representation we met in Episode Four, where the days of the week and the months of the year were found laid out as rings — directions in a plane, parameterised by angle, near-orthogonal to their neighbours as the price of superposition. The modular-addition network has, of its own accord and for reasons of pure computational thrift, reinvented the same geometry. The days-of-the-week ring was a curiosity caught in a large model; here we catch the same shape being *built*, deliberately, because it is the right tool for the job.

And it *is* the right tool, by a piece of schoolroom trigonometry that the network exploits without being told. Adding two numbers under a modulus is, on the circle, simply *adding their angles* — advancing one rotation by another and reading off where you land. To add angles, you do not need addition; you need the product-to-sum identities. The network arranges, through the multiplication implicit in its attention and the nonlinearity of its multilayer perceptron, to compute combinations of *cos(ωa)*, *sin(ωa)*, *cos(ωb)*, *sin(ωb)* that yield *cos(ω(a+b))* and *sin(ω(a+b))* — the angle of the *sum*, recovered from the angles of the parts. Then the unembedding does the final trick: the logit assigned to each candidate answer *c* is largest exactly when *ω(a + b − c)* is a multiple of a full turn — that is, when *c ≡ a + b*. The network proposes every possible answer and, by constructive and destructive interference across its chosen frequencies, the true sum is the one on which all the waves agree. It is a tiny analogue computer, made of rotations, that computes addition by spinning dials and noticing where the needles line up.

I dwell on this not for its prettiness alone, though it is pretty, but because it makes the central mystery concrete. *This* is the generalising solution. It works for inputs the network never saw, because the circle does not care which pairs you withheld; once the dials are calibrated, any *a* and any *b* may be added. And it is utterly unlike the memorising solution — the lookup table — that the network found first and sat in for so long. The question of grokking, sharpened, becomes: why does the network spend so long in the lookup table before it builds the clock? And the answer requires us first to watch the clock being built, which we can, beneath the flat surface, with the right instrument.

## Part Three — The Three Phases, and the Instruments That Reveal Them

The flatness of the test-loss curve through the long intermission is, I said, partly a matter of where one looks. Here is what that means, and it is the methodological heart of the Nanda work.

The trouble with the test loss is that it is a single number reporting on the whole network at once, and it goes down only when the *finished* generalising circuit is good enough to outvote the memorising one at the output. It is blind to a circuit that is half-built — present, strengthening, but not yet decisive. To see the half-built circuit, you need an instrument that looks specifically at the *parts the generalising solution uses*, and ignores the rest. Nanda and colleagues built two such instruments, and they are worth understanding because the trick generalises far beyond this toy.

The first they call **restricted loss**. Recall that the generalising algorithm operates through a sparse handful of Fourier frequencies — the network does not use all possible frequencies, only a few key ones, the rest being noise. Restricted loss asks: if we *project the network’s computation onto only those key frequencies* — keep the machinery the clock actually uses, discard everything else — how well does it perform? The second instrument, its mirror, is **excluded loss**: if we *ablate* those key frequencies — tear out exactly the clock’s gears and leave the rest of the network intact — how badly does performance degrade? The excluded loss — and this is the whole point of it — is measured on the *training set*, the very data on which the ordinary loss flattened to the floor long ago and fell silent. It is a surgical variant of a number you had written off as having nothing left to say, and it turns out to have a great deal.

Now watch what these two reveal through the intermission, where the ordinary test loss showed nothing. The restricted loss *falls smoothly*, beginning long before the test-accuracy cliff. The clock is being assembled, gear by gear, all through the supposedly dead middle of training; the parts the generalising solution will use are getting better and better at their job, steadily, with no discontinuity at all. And the excluded loss *rises smoothly* over the same interval: as the clock takes over, removing it hurts more and more, because the network is leaning on it more and more. Neither curve has a cliff. The famous abruptness of grokking, seen through these instruments, *dissolves into a smooth and continuous process* — a circuit forming at a steady rate, and the network gradually transferring its weight onto it.

The cliff in the test accuracy is, then, a *threshold effect*. The generalising circuit improves continuously, but it only starts winning the vote at the output — only starts moving the visible test number — once it has crossed a sufficiency line. The drama is real, but it is the drama of a slowly filling vessel finally reaching the lip, not of a sudden creation from nothing.

This licences the standard three-phase account of grokking, which I shall now state in its mature form. The first phase is **memorisation**: the network rapidly fits the training set with the lookup-table solution, train loss to the floor, test loss indifferent. The second phase is **circuit formation**: beneath the flat surface, the generalising clock is assembled, restricted loss falling, excluded loss rising, while the memorising solution still dominates the output and the test loss still shows nothing. The third phase is **cleanup**: the weight decay, which in the standard setups has been quietly preferring the more efficient solution all along, finishes removing the now-redundant memorising machinery — the lookup table is *expensive* in weight norm, the clock is cheap — and as the memorising component is stripped away, the generalising circuit is left in sole possession of the output, and the test accuracy climbs. Grokking is the visible shadow of the cleanup phase. The understanding was achieved earlier, in the dark; the cliff is merely the moment the lights came up.

I should note, lest I leave the impression that the clock is the only possible solution, a 2023 result from Ziqian Zhong, Ziming Liu, Max Tegmark, and Jacob Andreas with the agreeable name of the clock and the pizza. They found that the trigonometric “clock” algorithm is not the only one these networks discover for modular arithmetic — a second family, which they dub the “pizza” algorithm, partitions the circle differently and computes by a distinct geometric route. Which algorithm a given network lands on depends on the particulars of architecture and initialisation. This is worth carrying home as a caution: *there is rarely one true circuit*. A network has, for most tasks, several genuinely different mechanisms available to it, and which one it grows is a contingent matter of where it happened to start and how it happened to be built. We shall see this contingency again, and it has teeth.

## Part Four — Why It Waits

We can now ask the question grokking poses in its sharpest form. The generalising circuit is, we have seen, being built almost from the start. Why does the network bother with the lookup table at all, and why does it cling to it so long before the cleanup? Two families of explanation have emerged, and they are not so much rivals as the same insight seen from two depths.

The first family I shall call the **efficiency** account, and its cleanest statement is a 2023 paper by Vikrant Varma, Rohin Shah, Zachary Kenton, János Kramár, and Ramana Kumar, then at DeepMind, explaining grokking through circuit efficiency. Their proposal is economical in both senses. A task like modular addition admits, they observe, two solution families: a memorising one and a generalising one. Both achieve perfect training accuracy — so on the training set alone there is nothing to choose between them. But they differ in *efficiency*, defined as the logits they produce per unit of weight norm. The generalising clock is cheap: a few calibrated frequencies suffice to answer every question, so it can produce confident outputs with small weights. The memorising lookup table is dear: it must store something distinct for every training pair, so the more pairs there are, the larger the weights it requires.

From this one asymmetry a great deal follows, and the following is what makes the paper more than a story. Because the memorising solution’s cost scales with the number of training examples while the generalising solution’s cost does not, there is a **critical dataset size** — Varma and colleagues make this quantitative — at which the two solutions are equally efficient. Below it, memorising is the cheaper way to drive the loss down per unit weight, and the network, pressed by weight decay toward small weights, prefers to memorise. Above it, generalising is cheaper, and the network prefers the clock. Grokking, on this account, is what you see when you train *above* the critical size: the network first finds the fast memorising solution, then weight decay slowly reallocates its weight budget toward the more efficient generalising one, and the transition you witness is the economics resolving in favour of the clock.

The account makes predictions that are not obvious, and they hold — which is the part that should command your respect rather than merely your interest. Two are worth naming, because they are strange. The first is **ungrokking**: take a network that has fully grokked — generalises perfectly — and continue training it on a dataset *shrunk below* the critical size, and it will *regress*, abandoning its hard-won generalising solution and crawling back into memorisation, because below the critical size memorisation is once again the more efficient option. Generalisation, in this regime, is not a stable terminus one reaches and keeps; it is a balance of forces that can be tipped back. The second is **semi-grokking**: train *near* the critical size, where the two solutions are comparably efficient, and you get a partial, wobbling transition to middling test accuracy — neither full memorisation nor full generalisation, but a contested equilibrium between them. That a tidy efficiency story predicts two such counterintuitive behaviours, and that both appear on demand, is about as much as one can ask of an explanation in this field.

The second family digs a layer beneath the first, and asks *why* the network finds the memorising solution first and the generalising one only slowly — for efficiency explains which solution is eventually preferred, but not the order in which they are discovered. The answer, proposed in a 2024 paper by Tanishq Kumar, Blake Bordelon, Samuel Gershman, and Cengiz Pehlevan, is that grokking is the visible mark of a transition from **lazy** to **rich** training dynamics — and this connects grokking to a much older thread in learning theory, which is why I give it room.

The terms are precise. Early in training, particularly when the initial weights are large, a network can occupy a *lazy* regime — sometimes called the kernel or neural-tangent regime — in which it fits its training data *without substantially changing its internal features*. It behaves, near its initialisation, almost like a linear model: it adjusts the readout from a fixed, randomly-initialised set of features rather than learning new features adapted to the task. A lazy network can memorise — a sufficiently rich fixed basis can interpolate any finite training set — but it cannot generalise to structured held-out data, because generalisation here *requires* features shaped to the task’s structure, the circle that addition lives on, and the lazy network never moves its features off their random starting point. Grokking, on this view, is *delayed feature learning*: the network sits lazily memorising until the slow pressure of training finally pushes it out of the kernel regime and into the *rich* regime, where features at last begin to move, the circle is discovered, and generalisation follows.

What I find most clarifying about the Kumar account is that it *subsumes* the weight-norm observations rather than contradicting them, and corrects a tempting misreading along the way. Large initial weights, it turns out, are one of the things that push a network toward laziness — they approximately linearise its behaviour around the start. So the earlier finding, including the Omnigrok work of Ziming Liu, Eric Michaud, and Max Tegmark, that grokking correlates with weight norm is explained: weight norm mattered because it controlled *laziness*, not because of anything special about the norm itself. And Kumar and colleagues drive the point home with a demonstration that ought to settle the matter — grokking induced by vanilla gradient descent, no weight decay at all, with the weight norm *increasing*. The regulariser is one route into the rich regime; it is not the only one, and it is not the essence. The essence is the migration of the features. And this retroactively corrects the name we gave the third phase: *cleanup* is not fundamentally weight decay’s doing but the stripping of the now-redundant memorising machinery under a pressure toward the efficient solution — pressure that weight decay supplies in the standard setups, and that the migration into the rich regime can supply, as the no-decay demonstration shows, entirely on its own.

So the two accounts stack. The lazy-to-rich story says *why memorisation comes first and feature learning is delayed*; the efficiency story says *why, once both solutions are available, the network ends up at the generalising one*. Beneath both sits a single picture, and it is the picture to keep: a fast, lazy, expensive memorising solution found early, and a slow, rich, cheap generalising solution that wins in the end — with the long intermission being precisely the time it takes for the second to overtake the first.

Before we climb higher, sir, let me discharge a debt to Episode Four, because you have met this competition once already, under another name and along another axis. There we studied *double descent* — the unsettling finding that as one enlarges a model or its training set, the test error first falls, then *rises* into a peak of overfitting exactly where the model has barely enough capacity to memorise, and then, as capacity grows further still, falls a *second* time to a new and lower valley. That was memorise-then-generalise told along the axis of *scale*.

Grokking is the same tale told along the axis of *time*: memorise first, sit at the overfitting peak — here stretched into a long plateau rather than a brief bump — and then, given enough further training, descend into the generalising solution. The two were drawn together explicitly by Xander Davies and colleagues in 2023, and the unification rests on a single notion owed to Nakkiran and colleagues — an *effective model complexity* that grows with both size and training steps, so that the threshold past which the generalising pattern becomes learnable can be crossed by spending either parameters or epochs. Double descent and grokking are one phenomenon seen along two axes: the very same contest between a fast, poorly-generalising pattern and a slow, well-generalising one, resolved in the latter’s favour once the budget — of size, or of time — grows large enough to afford it. It is the thread that stitches this episode to the fourth, and worth the pause to tie.

## Part Five — What, Exactly, Is Simple? From the Norm to the Volume

The efficiency account of the previous part leans its full weight on a single phrase — *per unit of weight norm* — and the lazy-to-rich account, for all that it demotes the norm from cause to correlate, still conducts its business in the norm’s currency, initialisation scale and all. I have now let that currency pass twice without inspection, which is once more than a course of this kind should permit. The question I wish to press sounds pedantic and is anything but: when weight decay “prefers the simpler solution,” what is the thing it actually measures, and is that thing simplicity? For weight decay is, mathematically, nothing more exotic than a Gaussian prior wearing working clothes — a standing assumption that small weights are likelier than large ones — and the *size* of the weights correlates with the simplicity of the function but is not identical to it. A network can carry large weights and compute something simple; it can carry small, tidy weights and be a baroque lookup table smeared thinly across them. The norm is the shadow. It pays to ask what casts it.

The instinct that does the casting is older than deep learning, and it is worth naming its house. In 1992 David MacKay showed that Bayesian reasoning carries a built-in razor he called the **Occam factor**: score a model by its evidence — how well it explains the data averaged over everything the model *could have been* — and an automatic penalty falls on any model that must be specified *precisely* to work, while favour falls on those for which a broad swathe of parameter settings would all have done the job. The following year Hinton and van Camp said the same thing in the dialect of information: keep a network simple by minimising the **description length** of its weights — the number of bits needed to communicate them — and the cheap-to-describe networks are exactly those whose weights need only be given *vaguely*, broad regions rather than points. Mark well what notion of simplicity this is, because it is the episode’s quiet correction to its own earlier language. It is not *smallness*. It is **volume**: a solution is simple in proportion to how much of parameter space is consistent with producing it — how forgiving the recipe is, how much slop the specification tolerates. A simple solution is a *wide target*.

The same idea resurfaced as geometry in Hochreiter and Schmidhuber’s **flat minima** of 1997 — prefer the bottom of a broad valley to the bottom of a crevasse — and it has aged conspicuously well. When Jiang, Neyshabur, and colleagues staged their great bake-off of generalisation measures in 2020, racing dozens of proposed predictors across thousands of trained networks, the flatness-and-sharpness family consistently out-forecast the norm family. The volume intuition keeps winning the very contests the norm keeps entering.

The rigorous form of the intuition belongs to Watanabe, out of the same **singular learning theory** on which Part Eight will lean, and — you will be relieved to hear, given how you consume these episodes — it can be said completely without an equation, in one breath. Score any region of parameter space by its **free energy**: the Bayesian’s figure of demerit, lower being better. To leading order, the free energy is a sum of exactly two terms. The first is the *fit* term: the number of training examples, multiplied by the best loss achievable anywhere in the region. It punishes the too-simple, and it grows in proportion to the data. The second is the *complexity* term: a number called the **learning coefficient** — lambda, of which a great deal more shortly — multiplied by the *logarithm* of the number of examples. It punishes the too-elaborate, and it grows only logarithmically. And lambda itself is the volume idea made exact: it measures how rapidly the volume of parameter settings consistent with a given quality of fit *shrinks* as you demand the fit be better. A wide, forgiving basin — much volume surviving as the standard tightens — has a small lambda; a needle of a solution, which must be specified to many decimal places before it works at all, has a large one.

Now the part I most want you to hold, sir, because it answers a question every practitioner has put to a validation curve: *how simple is simple enough?* In this frame the question has a parameter-free answer. The exchange rate between fit and complexity — how much fit one unit of lambda is worth — is the logarithm of the data count: fixed by how much data you have, and by nothing you choose. Since the fit term grows linearly and the complexity term only logarithmically, among regions that fit equally well the fit terms cancel and the contest is decided by lambda alone: as the data grows, the posterior concentrates on the *least complex region that still fits*. “As simple as necessary, but no simpler” ceases to be an aphorism and becomes arithmetic — Occam’s razor as a theorem rather than a taste. One housekeeping remark to forestall a misreading: the prior in all of this acts as a *measure*, not a *selector* — it weighs the volume that survives; the likelihood does the choosing — so the razor is not the artefact of a cleverly chosen prior.

Now say grokking in this language and listen to it click. Two regions of parameter space fit the training set perfectly: the memorising basin and the generalising one. Their fit terms tie. Their lambdas do not — the lookup table must be specified pair by painstaking pair, a needle, while the clock is a wide target — so the memorising basin carries the higher learning coefficient and the generalising basin the lower. As the training set grows past the point where the complexity gap outweighs all else, the two free energies cross, and the posterior’s favour tips from the dear basin to the cheap one. Varma’s critical dataset size, which the previous part derived from the economics of logits per unit norm, reappears here as *the crossing point of two free energies* — the same tipping point, now priced in the correct currency. The physicists reached the same picture from their own direction: Noa Rubin, Inbar Seroussi, and Zohar Ringel showed in 2024 that grokking maps onto a **first-order phase transition** — an effective potential with two competing minima that exchange stability as training and regularisation proceed, complete with the hysteresis and mixed phases such transitions exhibit in matter. And in 2026 the singular-learning treatment arrived in earnest, in work by Ben Cullen, Sergio Estan-Ruiz, Riya Danait, and Jiayi Li that casts grokking explicitly as a transition between competing basins ranked by their local learning coefficients — deriving the coefficient in closed form for shallow networks simple enough to solve exactly, and, in the part that matters most for this episode’s final act, *measuring the estimated coefficient’s trajectory straight through training*, watching it register the passage from the dear basin to the cheap one as it happened. The closed forms live where closed forms can — their toy is not the modular-arithmetic transformer — but the perspective is precisely the one this part has been assembling.

But the same work states, with a candour I commend, the caveat on which the entire practical question turns, and it deserves a paragraph of its own. The free energy tells you which basin *deserves* the posterior. It says nothing about whether your optimiser, from where it happens to stand, will ever *get there*. Statistical preference is one question; the training-time crossing is governed by optimisation dynamics, which is quite another — and in the gap between the two lives all the folklore of the practitioner. Weight decay, seen from this height, is a crude, optimiser-entangled, *path-dependent* handle on volume. It does not set the exchange rate — the logarithm of the data does that, whether you like it or not. What your coefficient sets is the *path*. Too little pressure, and the crossing may simply never be forced: Part One’s networks sitting forever in the lookup table, statistically disfavoured and dynamically content. The wrong initialisation scale, and you are lazy from the outset, the features never moving, the cheaper basin never even under construction. The phase maps of Liu and colleagues’ effective-theory work of 2022 — four regions, *comprehension, grokking, memorisation,* and *confusion*, charted against initialisation scale and decay — are precisely maps of which starting points and which rudder settings ever reach which basin. And this is why “which decay coefficient is right” is settled at the validation curve rather than derived from first principles: the principled quantity, the free energy, is the thing we conspicuously decline to optimise directly, and the coefficient you tune is a rudder, not a destination.

Once the picture is dynamical, it invites engineering, and the engineering has been done. In 2024, Jaerin Lee, Bong Gyun Kang, Kihoon Kim, and Kyoung Mu Lee observed that if grokking is a slow solution patiently overtaking a fast one, the two ought to be separable *in the gradients themselves* — and they are. Treat each parameter’s sequence of updates across training as a signal in time, and it decomposes into a fast-varying component, which serves the quick business of memorisation, and a slow-varying component, which carries the patient drift toward generalisation. Their method — **Grokfast**, and for once the name oversells nothing — low-pass filters the gradients and amplifies the slow component, and the intermission collapses: grokking accelerated more than fifty-fold, in a few lines of code atop a standard optimiser, on tasks running from algorithmic data through images and text to molecular graphs — and synergistic with weight decay, pleasingly, rather than a replacement for it. If the wait is a slow force losing patiently to a fast one, one may simply lean on the scale.

So the ledger of this part, one line per entry: the norm is a proxy; the truer Occam is *volume* — how wide a target the solution presents; the free energy is the principled score, its exchange rate fixed by the data and never by us; and the learning coefficient is the one handle on all this that can actually be measured — which is precisely why it is about to become the protagonist of the episode’s final act.

## Part Six — Grokking Leaves the Toybox

I have leaned hard on modular addition, and a fair objection has been accumulating: arithmetic on a circle is a confection, chosen for its tractability, and a phenomenon visible only in confections is a phenomenon one may safely ignore. So let me show grokking doing something a working person might actually want, on a task that is not a toy, where the stakes are recognisably those of reasoning rather than of party tricks. The demonstration is a 2024 paper by Boshi Wang, Xiang Yue, Yu Su, and Huan Sun, whose subtitle — a mechanistic journey to the edge of generalisation — is for once not overstating the case.

The question they pose is one the largest models conspicuously fumble: can a transformer learn to reason *implicitly* over knowledge stored in its own weights — to chain stored facts together within a single forward pass, without writing its work out as text? Consider the simplest interesting case, which they call **composition**: the model has been taught, as separate atomic facts, that one entity stands in some relation to a second, and that the second stands in some relation to a third. Implicit composition is the ability to answer a question about the *first* and the *third* directly — to perform the two-hop inference internally, fetching the intermediate entity and using it to retrieve the answer, all in the silent forward pass, never having been shown that combined fact during training. Alongside it they study **comparison**, a second reasoning type with the same flavour of retrieve-two-things-and-operate-on-them.

The central finding is the one that earns this part its place in the episode: transformers *can* acquire these implicit reasoning skills — but *only through grokking*, only after extended training far beyond the point of overfitting. Train to convergence on the atomic facts and stop, as any reasonable schedule would, and the model can recite its facts and cannot reason over them. Keep training, long past the point where the training loss has nothing left to give, and the implicit reasoning circuit slowly assembles, exactly as the modular-addition clock did beneath its flat surface — and eventually the model can chain facts it was never shown chained. Grokking, in other words, is not a pathology of toy tasks. It is the regime in which at least one genuinely difficult and genuinely useful capability — reasoning over stored knowledge — is *acquired at all*. That should retire the objection.

Now the part I find most pointed, because it is mechanistic and because it sets up the next episode with almost suspicious neatness. The two reasoning types do not grok *equally*. On in-distribution tests both succeed; but on out-of-distribution examples — facts drawn from a held-out region the training never touched — the model systematically generalises for *comparison* and systematically *fails* for *composition*. Wang and colleagues do not leave this as a curiosity; they open the model and trace the generalising circuits for both tasks throughout grokking, and they locate the difference precisely in the circuit’s *configuration* — in *where, across the layers, the atomic facts and the reasoning rules come to be stored*. The composition circuit, as it forms, learns to retrieve the intermediate entity at one layer and apply the second hop at a later one. But it only stores a given atomic fact in the upper layers, where the second hop can reach it, *if that fact appeared as a second hop during training*. A fact the model saw only in atomic form, never as the second step of a chain, is simply not present at the layer where the second hop would need it — and so, queried out of distribution, the model cannot complete the inference. The knowledge is in the weights; it is merely in the *wrong floor of the building*.

And the cause Wang and colleagues name for this is the sentence I should like you to carry directly into the next episode: it is a consequence of the **non-recurrent design of the transformer**, which forbids memory sharing across layers. A transformer computes each layer once, in order, and information that was not placed on a given floor cannot be fetched up to it from below mid-computation; there is no mechanism within the single forward pass for a later layer to reach back and pull a fact from an earlier one that the training did not pre-position. The composition failure is the architecture’s depth limit made visible — a reasoning step that wants a fact the fixed pipeline never routed to the place it was needed.

Hold that thought with both hands, sir, because it is the hinge between this episode and the next. The cure for a model that cannot share memory across its layers is to let it share memory across *tokens* — to write the intermediate result out as text and read it back in on the following pass, so that the second hop, on the next forward pass, finds the first hop’s answer sitting in plain sight in its own context. That is precisely what chain-of-thought reasoning does, and precisely the relationship between reasoning and depth that the next episode takes as its subject. The implicit-reasoning circuit grokked here is the model straining to do *within* its layers what reasoning lets it do *across* its tokens — and failing, on composition, exactly where the want of cross-layer memory bites. The two episodes meet on this point.

Two further notes before we widen the lens. First, the efficiency account of Part Four reappears here in the wild and quantitatively: what governs whether grokking occurs on this real-ish factual data is, the work finds, the *ratio* of inferred facts to atomic facts in the training distribution — too many bare atomic facts relative to compositional ones and the generalising circuit never becomes worth its weight; raise the proportion of inferred facts above a threshold and grokking proceeds. That threshold is Varma’s critical dataset size, dressed in the clothes of a knowledge graph — the same economics, the same tipping point, now a lever one could in principle engineer by augmenting one’s data with the right synthetic chains. Second, the practical sting: a *fully grokked* transformer, the authors show, can reach near-perfect accuracy on a challenging reasoning task with a large search space on which GPT-4-Turbo and Gemini-1.5-Pro — formidable models, reasoning from retrieved rather than parametric memory — fail badly regardless of prompting. The grokked circuit, having paid the long price of extended training, possesses something the larger models reasoning from outside their weights do not. Parametric memory, properly grokked, is not a consolation prize. It is, on the right task, the better instrument.

## Part Seven — Grokking Is Not the Exception

It would be easy to file all of this under *toy curiosities* — a charming pathology of small networks on arithmetic, of no bearing on the large models one actually deploys. That filing would be a mistake, and correcting it is the business of this part. The deep claim grokking illustrates — that capabilities form in discrete events, often invisibly to the loss curve — recurs in the training of real models, and once you are looking for it, you find it in several places.

The cleanest large-model instance we met already, in Episode Three, though we may now see it in its developmental light. In the 2022 study of in-context learning and induction heads by Catherine Olsson and colleagues at Anthropic, the training loss curve of a transformer — smooth almost everywhere, as these curves are — shows a small but distinct *bump*, a brief interval in which the loss behaves anomalously before resuming its descent. That bump is not noise. It is the formation of the **induction heads**, the circuit responsible for the copy-and-continue mechanism underlying in-context learning, and the authors show that the model’s in-context learning ability *appears at the same moment*. A capability switches on, abruptly, at a specific point in training, and leaves a visible scar on the loss curve as it does so. This is grokking’s family resemblance writ on a real model: a discrete developmental event, a circuit crystallising, a new competence arriving not by slow accumulation but by something nearer to a phase transition.

This connects, in turn, to one of the more heated debates of recent years, which I shall referee briefly because the developmental view resolves it rather neatly. A 2022 paper by Jason Wei and colleagues catalogued what it called **emergent abilities** of large language models — capabilities that are absent in smaller models and present in larger ones, appearing *sharply* as scale increases rather than improving gradually, so that one cannot extrapolate their arrival from the trend below. A 2023 rejoinder by Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda, and Sanmi Koyejo argued that this emergence is, in many cases, a **mirage** — an artefact of the *metric*. Use a harsh all-or-nothing measure like exact-match accuracy, they pointed out, and a smoothly improving underlying capability will appear to leap from zero to one as it crosses a threshold; switch to a smoother, more forgiving metric and the leap softens into a gentle slope. The sharpness was, on this argument, in our ruler, not in the model.

The two positions are less opposed than the framing suggests, and grokking shows why. Recall what the restricted and excluded losses did: a smooth, continuous internal process — the clock assembling — presented as a sharp cliff in the test accuracy, purely because accuracy is a threshold readout of a continuously improving circuit. Schaeffer and colleagues are, in effect, making exactly this point about scaling, and they are right that much apparent emergence is threshold artefact. *And yet* the induction-head bump is not a metric artefact — it is a genuine discontinuity in the loss itself, a real event in the network’s development. Both things are true. Some sharpness is in the ruler; some is in the machine. The discipline the developmental view imposes is to stop arguing about the surface curve and go *look* — to ask, of any apparent emergence, whether an actual circuit is forming at that point, which is a question about mechanism that the metric debate cannot settle and internal instruments can.

The most ambitious synthesis of this picture is the **quantisation model of neural scaling**, a 2023 proposal by Eric Michaud, Ziming Liu, Uzay Girit, and Max Tegmark. Its conceit is that the smooth power-law curves of neural scaling — the reliable, almost suspiciously clean lines along which loss falls as models and data grow — are an *aggregate* phenomenon, the smooth sum of a great many *discrete* underlying events. The network, on this view, learns a sequence of distinct skills, which they call **quanta**, each acquired at a particular scale or point in training, each contributing a small drop to the loss. Any individual quantum is learned *abruptly* — it is there or it is not. But there are so many of them, acquired across such a range of scales, that summed together they trace a smooth line. The clean scaling law is, like the calm test-loss curve through grokking’s intermission, a tranquil surface over a great deal of discontinuous activity beneath. If that is right — and it remains a model, with its frayed edges — then *everything* a large model can do was acquired in a grokking-like event, and the smoothness we find so reassuring is precisely what hides the structure we should most like to see.

## Part Eight — Measuring a Mind as It Grows

If training is an embryology — a sequence of discrete births — then the natural ambition is to build an instrument that detects the births *as they occur*: a developmental science with a thermometer, that can point to a moment in training and say *here, a new structure formed; here is what it is*. The most serious attempt at such a science goes under the name **developmental interpretability**, pursued largely by the group around Daniel Murfet and Jesse Hoogland — the Timaeus organisation — and built on the body of mathematics we brushed against in Part Five — singular learning theory, due originally to Sumio Watanabe. It is the most theoretically demanding material in this course, and also the youngest and least settled, so I shall lay out the idea with care and the caveats without flinching.

Begin with the mathematics, because the whole edifice rests on one fact about neural networks that classical statistics gets wrong. The standard tools of statistical learning theory assume a model is **regular** — that, near a good setting of its parameters, the loss landscape looks like a tidy bowl, curving upward in every direction, so that each parameter is pinned down and the count of parameters measures the model’s complexity. Neural networks are not regular. They are **singular**: their loss landscapes are riddled with degeneracy. The minima are not isolated points at the bottom of bowls but extended, connected *varieties* — flat-bottomed valleys, ridges, intricate surfaces along which the loss does not change at all, because there are whole directions in parameter space you can move without affecting what the network computes. A network has many internal symmetries — neurons you could permute, weights you could rescale in compensating pairs — and each is a direction of flatness. The naive parameter count badly overstates the *effective* complexity, because a great many of those parameters are not doing independent work.

Watanabe’s singular learning theory is the apparatus for doing statistics correctly in this singular setting, and it furnishes the quantity the developmental programme leans on. In place of the parameter count it offers the learning coefficient — the very lambda that priced complexity in Part Five’s free energy, now given its proper pedigree: globally, a quantity from algebraic geometry called the real log canonical threshold; locally, around a particular point in parameter space, the **local learning coefficient**, or **LLC**, which one can actually estimate. The LLC is best understood as a measure of *effective dimensionality* — how many directions of parameter space are genuinely being used near a given solution, as opposed to lying flat and idle — though, as its proponents are careful to say, it is a richer object than mere dimension-counting, sensitive to the precise geometry of the degeneracy. The estimation, worked out by Edmund Lau, Susan Wei, and Daniel Murfet, proceeds by a stochastic sampling method — a tempered random walk through parameter space, watching how the loss responds — which makes the LLC, in principle, a number you can compute for a real network at a real checkpoint.

It is worth one paragraph to place this against the capacity measure an older schooling may reach for, the VC dimension, because the contrast is where the LLC’s character lives. VC dimension rates an entire hypothesis *class*, by its worst case, with no reference to any data distribution — it tells you what the architecture *could* shatter, in the hands of an adversary, on the least convenient data conceivable. The LLC rates *this solution*, on *this data* — not what the instrument could play in principle but what this player is doing with it now. Two networks of identical architecture, and therefore identical class capacity, can sit at wildly different LLCs, one sprawled across its parameters and the other using a sliver of them. And — the property everything downstream depends on — the LLC *moves during training*, since it is a fact about where the weights presently sit rather than about the architecture. A worst-case class constant could never register a birth; a solution-level, data-aware measure can, and that is the entire qualification for the post of developmental thermometer.

Here is why this is the right instrument for an embryology. The LLC is a measure of *structure* — of how much effective complexity the network has crystallised. As a network develops, as it forms a circuit and that circuit takes up residence in the weights, the local geometry of the loss landscape *changes*, and the LLC changes with it. So one can track the LLC across training and read it as a developmental signal: plateaus where structure is stable, and transitions where new structure forms. The bet of developmental interpretability is that the discrete births we have been circling — the induction-head bump, the grokking cleanup, the Michaud quanta — show up as detectable *phase transitions* in these geometric quantities, and that one can therefore segment training into stages not by eyeballing the loss but by a principled measurement of the model’s changing internal complexity.

And there are results, which keep the programme from being mere aspiration. In a 2024 study of the developmental landscape of in-context learning, Hoogland, Wang, Carroll, and colleagues showed that in-context learning emerges in *discrete developmental stages*, detectable as transitions in the loss landscape’s geometry — milestones one can locate in both parameter space and function space, separating qualitatively distinct phases of the network’s development, validated against behavioural and structural metrics. In a companion line of work on a refined version of the LLC, George Wang, Jesse Hoogland, Stan van Wingerden, Zach Furman, and Daniel Murfet applied the measure to *individual components* of a small attention-only transformer and watched its attention heads *differentiate* over training — start undifferentiated, then specialise into distinct functional roles, the way cells differentiate in a growing organism — and in the process turned up a previously unnoticed circuit, a so-called multigram circuit, which the refined geometric measure surfaced before anyone had thought to look for it. And in a study with the forbidding title of dynamical versus Bayesian phase transitions in a toy model of superposition, Zhongtian Chen, Edmund Lau, Jake Mendel, Susan Wei, and Daniel Murfet connected the theory back to the very toy model of superposition we studied in Episode Four, showing that the structures that form there — the geometric arrangements of features — appear and rearrange at specific points in training that the singular-learning apparatus identifies as genuine phase transitions, and distinguishing the *dynamical* transitions one sees in the course of training from the *Bayesian* ones the equilibrium theory predicts, which do not always coincide. And the thermometer has now been held to this episode’s own protagonist: the basin-selection study of Part Five doubles as the LLC’s field test on grokking itself, the coefficient’s trajectory registering the crossing from the dear basin to the cheap one as it occurred.

That the same geometric measure that *detects* a developmental transition can also be aimed at a *component* and reveal *which circuit formed* is the prize the programme is reaching for: not merely a thermometer that registers that something happened, but one that can be pointed at the something. Whether the prize is fully in hand is another matter, to which I now turn, because the honest reckoning is as important as the promise.

## Part Nine — The Honest Limits

I have given developmental interpretability a generous hearing, because it is reaching for the right thing. Candour now requires the other side of the ledger, and it is not short.

The first limit is the one already conceded about grokking and inherited by everything built on it: the cleanest results live on *toy tasks*. Modular addition, small attention-only transformers, deliberately constructed toy models of superposition — these are chosen precisely because they are simple enough to understand completely, and that very simplicity is what licenses doubt about whether the lessons transfer. Grokking in its pure, dramatic form is largely a small-network-on-clean-algorithmic-data affair; the singular-learning results, likewise, are demonstrated at the scale of thousands or low millions of parameters. The claim that real, large-model training is *also* a sequence of detectable phase transitions is *motivated* by these results and partly corroborated by things like the induction-head bump, but it is not established at scale. The bridge from the toy to the frontier model is, at present, a bridge of plausibility, not of demonstration.

The second limit is a methodological hazard I shall put bluntly: *everything is a phase transition if you measure it finely enough*. A sufficiently sensitive instrument, pointed at a sufficiently complicated process, will always find structure — plateaus and transitions, stages and milestones — and the human eye, ours especially, is exquisitely tuned to see narrative where there is only noise. The discipline of validation that we demanded of circuits in Episode Six applies here with full force, and is harder to satisfy. A claimed developmental stage is a hypothesis, and it earns belief only when the structure it posits can be *independently confirmed* — by intervention, by correlation with a behavioural change, by surfacing a circuit one can then verify by the methods of the earlier episodes. The interpretability illusions of Episode Six have a developmental cousin: the just-so story of stages, fitted after the fact to a curve, persuasive and quite possibly imaginary. The Timaeus group is, to its credit, generally scrupulous about this; the field around it will not always be, and a stage story unbacked by intervention deserves the same scepticism we would give a circuit diagram unbacked by ablation.

The third limit is technical and concerns the LLC itself. Its estimation is *delicate and dear*. The sampling procedure has knobs that must be tuned, it grows costly at scale, and the resulting number can be sensitive to choices that are not always easy to justify from first principles. A measurement that is finicky to take is a measurement to treat with care, particularly when conclusions about a model’s development are to be hung from small movements in it.

And the fourth limit is the deepest, and I shall not paper over it: singular learning theory in its rigorous form is a theory of *Bayesian* learning — of the posterior distribution over parameters that an idealised Bayesian reasoner would hold — and the networks we actually train are *not* trained by Bayesian inference. They are trained by stochastic gradient descent, a different process with its own dynamics, and the correspondence between the two is an active research question rather than a settled identity. The Chen and colleagues result that *dynamical* and *Bayesian* phase transitions need not coincide is, read one way, exactly this gap showing itself. The theory is beautiful and the geometry is real; whether the beautiful equilibrium theory licenses conclusions about the messy dynamical reality of SGD is precisely what remains to be shown.

I should add the one place where, limits notwithstanding, the ambition earns its keep most clearly, and it is the connection back to safety that I promised to keep brief. If a dangerous capability — situational awareness, a deceptive strategy, the kind of hidden objective we studied in Episode Seven — forms during training in a discrete event, as the developmental picture suggests capabilities generally do, then an instrument that flags that event *as it happens* would be worth a great deal: an early-warning system that catches the formation of a worrying circuit during training, rather than discovering it after deployment by the unhappy method of being surprised. That is the safety case for developmental interpretability, stated once and not belaboured. It is, today, an aspiration resting on toy-scale validation. Whether it becomes an instrument depends on whether the bridges I have just described get built. I would not bet against the attempt, and I would not yet rely on the result.

## Part Ten — The Engineer’s Dividend

Enough theory; you have systems to train, sir, and this episode owes you something usable at the bench. Here is what the developmental view changes about the working life of someone who fine-tunes and ships models.

The first dividend is a corrected instinct about *early stopping*. The reflex to halt when held-out performance plateaus is sound on large, redundant datasets and treacherous on small, structured ones — and the efficiency account tells you exactly when to be suspicious. The reason you rarely see clean grokking on a real training run is not that grokking is exotic; it is that you are almost always training *above* the critical dataset size, where the generalising solution is preferred from early on and the long intermission collapses. That is not grokking being absent — it is grokking happening so promptly that fitting and generalising fuse into a single descent. Knowing *why* you don’t see the intermission tells you when you might: shrink the data, sharpen the structure of the task, and the gap can in principle reopen, your plateau revealed as an intermission rather than a terminus. I offer that last as a working conjecture and not a law: whether a given plateau conceals a circuit forming in the dark or merely a run that has quietly died is exactly the question the aggregate loss is unfit to answer. The plateau is a hypothesis about the future, and a fragile one.

The second dividend concerns the knobs you already turn without quite knowing what they do. **Weight decay** and **initialisation scale** are not mere stabilisers to be set by folklore; they are the *levers on the lazy-to-rich axis*, which is to say they partly determine *whether and when your model learns features at all* rather than merely interpolating from its random starting basis. A large initialisation buys you a lazy network — quick to fit, poor to generalise, declining to move its features. If a fine-tune is fitting its training data and failing to generalise in the way the lazy regime predicts, the lever to reach for may not be more data or more steps but a change to the scale and regularisation that governs whether feature learning *engages*. These are not cosmetic settings. They select the regime.

The third dividend is the one I would tattoo on the inside of every practitioner’s wrist: *the loss curve is a lossy summary of what your model is doing*. A flat stretch is not necessarily a dead model; it may be, as in grokking’s intermission, a circuit forming vigorously beneath a surface that reports nothing. The remedy is the whole burden of this course — instrument the *internals*, not merely the scalar. Track quantities that look at *parts* of the computation, as the restricted and excluded losses did; if you can manage it, watch a complexity measure like the LLC across training. The aggregate loss is the patient’s temperature: useful, cheap, and nearly silent about the disease.

The fourth dividend connects this episode to the eighth, and to the fine-tuning questions I know occupy you. A fine-tune is, in miniature, a developmental episode of its own — a short additional training run that grows or modifies structure on top of a base model — and the lazy-to-rich lens is unusually illuminating about *why low-rank and small-magnitude fine-tunes behave as they do*. A LoRA-style update is, almost by construction, *lazy*: it is small, it is confined to a low-dimensional subspace, and it therefore tends to *modulate the features the base model already has* rather than growing genuinely new ones — which is precisely the empirical finding the crosscoder model-diffing work reported in Episode Eight, that fine-tuning mostly adjusts existing circuitry and stays near the base. That is a feature when the base already contains the machinery you need and you merely wish to redirect it; it is a *limit* when the task demands a feature the base never learned, for a lazy update is the wrong tool for growing one. Reach for a richer update — more parameters free to move, more training, the regime deliberately tipped toward feature learning — when, and only when, you have reason to believe the capability you want is not already latent in the base waiting to be steered. Knowing which case you are in is half the craft, and the lazy-rich distinction is how you reason about it.

The fifth and last is simply a pointer: the instruments described in Part Eight are increasingly *runnable*. The developmental-interpretability tooling for estimating local learning coefficients is open and improving, and the barrier to watching your own model’s complexity evolve across a training run is lower than it was a year ago and falling. The science is young and the measure is delicate — I have said so plainly — but it is no longer purely the property of theorists. If the loss curve’s silence has ever cost you, this is the direction in which the better instruments lie.

## Closing — What We Have Earned, and Where It Points

We set out from a misbehaving toy — a network that passed its examination and then, ludicrously, kept studying until it understood — and we have, I hope, turned that embarrassment into a thesis. Training is not the smooth slide down a hill that the loss curve depicts. It is a development: a sequence of discrete events in which structure forms, circuits crystallise, and capabilities switch on, often invisibly to the scalar we habitually watch.

Let me be exact about what we have actually earned, since a closing that overclaims would betray the spirit of the whole. We have *one* fully worked example — modular addition — in which we can name the learned algorithm to the gear, watch it assemble beneath a flat surface with instruments built for the purpose, and explain by two complementary theories both why memorisation comes first and why generalisation wins in the end; we have chased the notion of simplicity itself from the norm through description length to volume, and priced it with a free energy spoken in two terms whose exchange rate is set by the data and never by us; and we have seen the same memorise-then-generalise dynamic caught lately on a task that is no toy — implicit multi-hop reasoning over stored facts — with the generalising circuit traced as it forms and its one great failure pinned to the architecture’s want of cross-layer memory. We have *one* robust developmental event in a real model — the induction-head bump — that is plainly a genuine discontinuity and not a trick of the metric. We have a plausible and partially corroborated conjecture, the quantisation model, that *all* of a large model’s competences were acquired in such events, with the smooth scaling law being their tranquil sum. And we have a young, mathematically serious programme — developmental interpretability, on the foundation of singular learning theory — attempting to make the detection of these events rigorous, with real toy-scale results and four honest limits standing between it and the frontier-scale instrument it aspires to be.

That is a genuine advance and an unfinished one, which is the usual condition of this science and no cause for apology.

A word on where it points, and it points two ways, which together close a circle this supplement opened. This episode and its question — *how did the machine come to be?* — concern the axis of *training time*, the long process by which the weights were found. The instruments of Episodes Eight and Nine, and the steering of Episode Seven, concern the *finished* machine and the axis of its *layers* — how a fixed set of weights computes a single token as activation flows from input to output. There remains a third axis we have so far left untouched: the axis of *generation* — what happens across the *sequence* of tokens a model emits when it is permitted to reason aloud, to think step by step, to spend many forward passes on a single hard problem rather than one. That axis turns out to have its own surprising structure, and a rather provocative relationship to the layer axis we know — the question of whether reasoning is, in effect, a way of borrowing depth the architecture did not supply, paid for through a bottleneck the residual stream never imposed. But that is the next episode’s affair, and one likes to see the country before walking into it.

Until then, sir.

*— End of Episode Ten.*
