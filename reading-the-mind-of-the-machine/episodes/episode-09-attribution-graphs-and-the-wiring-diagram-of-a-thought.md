# Attribution Graphs, and the Wiring Diagram of a Thought

*A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability — Episode Nine*

## From the Instrument to the Diagram

Good day, sir. We ended the last episode holding an instrument we had built but not yet wielded: a *replacement model*, the original’s clerks swapped out for legible transcoders, made faithful on a given prompt by error nodes and made very nearly *linear* by the freezing of attention. I promised we would draw with it. This is the drawing.

A word first on what this episode *is*, and is not, so that you do not feel I am repeating myself. In the seventh episode I walked you through a gallery — the things the wiring diagrams *showed*: the model reasoning through Texas to reach Austin, planning a rhyme before writing toward it, performing an arithmetic it cannot describe, thinking in a language-independent core, hallucinating by a misfired circuit, and — the hinge of that whole episode — narrating a chain of reasoning its actual computation did not follow. Those were the *findings*. I gave them their proper stage and I shall not re-narrate them here. *This* episode is the engineering *beneath* the gallery: not what the graphs revealed but *how a graph is built* — how its nodes and edges are computed, how the resulting hairball is pruned into something a human eye can follow, how it is *validated* (for an unvalidated graph is merely a pretty hypothesis), and where, precisely, the method is still blind. The sixth episode’s discipline of *intervention* returns here as the indispensable validation step, so keep it to hand. A graph drawn from the replacement model is a *claim*; only an intervention in the *real* model can confirm it.

The route. First, what an attribution graph *is* — its nodes and its edges, plainly. Then *how the edges are computed*, which is where the linearity we paid for last episode finally earns its price, and where I shall relate this method carefully to the attribution patching of the sixth episode, because they are cousins and the difference between them matters. Then the reduction of the astronomical raw graph to a legible one — *pruning* and the grouping of features into *supernodes*. Then the crucial part, *validation by intervention* — the move that turns a drawn graph into a believed circuit, and the place where the seventh episode’s findings reappear, now wearing their true hats as *experimental confirmations* rather than mere observations. Then a trial this course incurred as a debt last episode: the cross-layer transcoder itself in the dock, and the uncomfortable discovery that two instruments can each pass its examinations while telling two different stories about the same thought. Then the deepest limitation — the *frozen attention window* — and the frontier work now, slowly, thawing it. Then the distinction between a *local* graph that explains one answer and a *global* connectivity map that approximates the reusable circuit. And then, as the course requires, an honest accounting of what the method buys and what it still cannot do, and the engineer’s dividend.

The most vivid material this science has produced rests on this method. Let us see how the trick is actually done.

## Part One — What an Attribution Graph Is

An attribution graph is, at bottom, a simple object described in one sentence: it is a *directed, weighted graph, drawn for a single prompt, whose nodes are the active features and whose edges are the contributions each feature makes to the others on the way to the answer.* Let me unfold that, because each part is exact.

The **nodes** are of a few kinds. Most are *active transcoder features* — the features of our cross-layer transcoder that fired on this particular prompt, each pinned to a specific token position, so that “the Texas feature at the word *Dallas*” is a distinct node from “the Texas feature at the word *capital*.” There are *input nodes* — the token embeddings, the raw material written on the notebook’s first page. There are *error nodes* — the very error terms of the last episode, the per-clerk confessions of what the transcoders could not capture, now appearing as honest nodes in the graph that contribute to the answer through channels the legible features do not explain. And note their one structural peculiarity, because it is the confession made topological: an error node has *no incoming edges.* Nothing in the graph explains an error node — by construction, it is the unexplained — and so it stands in the diagram as a pure source, pushing on the computation from nowhere, a quantified column of dark matter rising through the drawing. And there are *output nodes* — the logits, the model’s scores over possible next tokens, the thing the whole computation is aimed at producing. One further precision worth fixing: a “feature” yields one node *per position at which it fires* — the bookkeeping is per feature *and* per token — and likewise one error node per clerk per position, so the raw object is large in exact proportion to its completeness.

The **edges** are *direct contributions*: an edge from node A to node B, with a weight, means *feature A’s activation contributed this much to feature B’s input* — to B’s pre-activation, the quantity that determines whether and how strongly B fires. A positive weight is excitation, A helping to switch B on; a negative weight is inhibition, A helping to switch B off, the suppression we have met repeatedly. The graph is thus a complete bookkeeping of *who pushed whom, and how hard*, on the path from the input embeddings, up through the firing features, to the logit that won.

Let me read one aloud, because you will consume much of this course at a run, sir, and a diagram described is worth more to a listener than a diagram gestured at. Take the field’s favourite specimen: *the capital of the state containing Dallas is* — answer, *Austin*. At the bottom of the drawing sit the embedding nodes, one per token, inert and given. Above the word *Dallas*, a cluster of features lights: features for the city, for Texan things generally. Above the word *capital*, a different cluster: features for capital cities, and — subtly distinct — features for the *act of being about to name* a capital, an operator waiting for an operand. Now the first interesting wires: from the Dallas-features, edges run *rightward and upward* — rightward, note, which means *across positions*, which means *along frozen attention* — into a group of features at the later positions that represent not Dallas but *Texas*: the state, never mentioned in the prompt, sitting in the middle of the drawing as an inferred intermediate, a thought the model had but did not say. From the Texas group and the say-a-capital group together, edges converge on a final cluster at the last position — *say Austin* — which pushes, with the fattest edge in the diagram, on the Austin logit at the top. And threaded through all of it, the honest untidiness: error nodes lending their unexplained weight here and there, and fainter wires that partially bypass the waypoint — the drawing showing not one immaculate path but a *dominant* path amid lesser traffic, which is, as we shall see when the hydra reappears, exactly what one ought to expect of this kind of machine. That is an attribution graph: embeddings at the bottom, the answer at the top, and in between — visible, weighted, and countable — the intermediate *thought*.

Set this beside the sixth episode’s showpiece, because the contrast is the whole point of the advance. The indirect-object circuit — twenty-six heads in seven classes — was a circuit whose *nodes were attention heads*, traced *by hand*, for a *task*. And the heads, you will recall, were *polysemantic*: each did many unrelated things beyond identifying indirect objects, so that the circuit told us *which heads mattered* without telling us *what, cleanly, each was doing.* The attribution graph upgrades the circuit on every axis at once. Its nodes are *monosemantic features* — clean, nameable concepts, not muddy heads. It is drawn *automatically* — by arithmetic, not by months of patient patching. And it is drawn for a *specific prompt* — a circuit for *this* thought, not a generic account of a task. Where the sixth episode’s circuit was a hand-drawn map of which streets carried traffic, the attribution graph is an automatically-generated diagram of which *thoughts* flowed into which, for one particular sentence. That is the leap, and the transcoder is what made it possible.

## Part Two — How the Edges Are Computed

Now the arithmetic, at the right depth, because here is where the linearity we purchased last episode is spent, and where I must be careful to relate this to a technique you already know.

Recall the local replacement model’s defining property. The clerks have become *sums of feature-decoder-directions*; the attention is *frozen* into fixed routing; the normalisation is *frozen* into fixed scaling. With all the nonlinear complications either captured inside the transcoder features, or shunted into error nodes, or frozen out of the way, the path from “feature A fired” to “feature B’s input” is *linear*. And a linear path has a computable weight, directly, by arithmetic. To find the edge from A to B you take A’s decoder direction — the direction A *writes* — route it forward through the frozen attention and the identity skip-connections of the residual stream to the floor where B reads, and *project it onto B’s encoder direction* — the direction B *reads*. The result, scaled by how strongly A actually fired, *is* the edge weight: how much of B’s input A was responsible for. It is, in essence, a dot product of directions, multiplied by an activation. The field, computing all these edges at once, does it by a *backward pass* through the linearised model — what the methods paper calls *backward Jacobian tracing* — but the conceptual content is the simple one: in a linear model, the contribution of each part to each other part is a readable number, and you read it.

Here I must draw a distinction from the sixth episode that careful people get wrong, because it bears directly on how much you should trust these edges. In the sixth episode I described **attribution patching**: a cheap *approximation* of patching, which uses the model’s *gradients* — one backward pass — to *estimate* what the effect of patching each component *would* be. I was at pains to flag its limitation: the gradient is a *linear approximation* of a *nonlinear* model, and it degrades exactly where effects are large and saturating — which is to say where they matter most. So attribution patching is a screening instrument, never a verdict.

The attribution graph’s edges look superficially similar — both are linear, both come from a backward pass — but the difference is crucial and it is in the model’s favour. Attribution patching linearly *approximates* a nonlinear model and hopes the approximation is good. The attribution graph computes *exact* contributions in a model that *is* linear *by construction* — because we *made* it linear, last episode, by freezing the nonlinear parts and shunting the remainder into error nodes. The linearity of the attribution graph is not a hopeful approximation of the real model’s behaviour; it is an *exact property of the local replacement model.* The edges are *exact direct effects within that model.* The whole question of fidelity, therefore, does not hide inside the edge weights — it has been *moved*, deliberately and visibly, into two places we can inspect: the *error nodes*, which quantify what the transcoders missed, and the *freezing of attention*, which sets aside what the attention is doing. The edges themselves are honest. What you must check is whether the *model they are honest about* is a faithful stand-in for the real one — and that, as we shall see, is exactly what intervention tests and what the error nodes report.

One more mechanical fact, and it is the seed of this episode’s gravest limitation, so plant it now. A transcoder feature reads and writes *only within a single token position* — that is what a feed-forward clerk does; it does not move information sideways. *All* movement of information *between* positions is done by *attention*. So when feature A at one position influences feature B at *another* position, *every bit* of that influence is *mediated by attention* — and the attention, in our local replacement model, is *frozen.* For an edge between two features at the *same* position, some of the contribution is direct, through the clerks. But for an edge *across* positions — and a great deal of interesting computation crosses positions — the contribution flows entirely along the frozen attention edges, which means the graph faithfully routes the *value* but is *silent on why attention carried it there.* Hold that; it is the frozen window, and Part Six is about the work prying it open.

## Part Three — From a Hairball to a Diagram: Pruning and Grouping

If you computed every node and every edge of the raw attribution graph for a real prompt, you would have an object of no use to anyone — millions of features across dozens of positions, each with thousands of edges, a dense and impenetrable hairball that resolves nothing. The raw graph is *complete* and *illegible*, which for our purposes is barely better than the model itself. Two reductions turn it into something a human can read, and each deserves a word, the second more than the first, because the second is where a tidy story can be quietly imposed.

The first reduction is **pruning**. The principle is to keep only what *matters for the answer* and discard the rest. You begin at the output node — the logit you are explaining — and trace *backward*, asking of each node how much *influence* it ultimately had on that output, counting not only its direct edge to the output but its *indirect* influence through all the paths by which it fed other features that fed the answer. You keep the nodes and edges of greatest influence — enough to account for the bulk of what drove the output — and you prune away the vast remainder of features that fired but did not, in the end, bear on *this* answer. What survives is a human-scale subgraph: a few dozen features, in legible layers, with the edges that actually carried the computation. The hairball becomes a diagram.

And the diagram arrives, in the modern tooling, wearing its own report card, which you should learn to read before you read the graph. Two numbers ride along. A **replacement score** grades whole routes, and grades them strictly: what fraction of the influence running from the input tokens to the answer travels through legible feature nodes the entire way, never once passing through an error node. And a **completeness score** grades single junctions, and grades them leniently: for every node, weighted by how much that node bears on the answer, what fraction of its incoming influence arrives from features and tokens rather than seeping in through the error nodes’ unexplained channels. The two are measuring one thing — how much of the work the dark matter is doing — at two scales, and the pair tells you which humility is owed: a low completeness score says the junctions themselves are fed chiefly from the error nodes, the drawing accurate and mostly dark; a replacement score far beneath it says the junctions are decently accounted for and yet scarcely a route from token to answer stays legible the whole way, a little error admitted at each of many hops compounding along the path. A graph with handsome scores has earned a hearing. A graph without them has earned a shrug, however pretty its arrows.

The second reduction is *grouping into supernodes*, and here you must keep the sixth episode’s warning about *interpretability illusions* close, because this is exactly where one can creep in. Even after pruning, the graph often contains *clusters of features doing the same conceptual job* — a dozen slightly different “Texas” features, several variants of “is about to say a capital city” — for the features, as the fifth episode warned with its talk of *feature splitting*, subdivide as the instrument grows finer, so that one human concept is spread across several machine features. To make the diagram readable, one *groups* these into a single **supernode** — “the Texas node,” “the say-a-capital node” — collapsing the redundant detail into the concept a human actually thinks in. And this grouping is a *curatorial* act. It is performed by a person — increasingly with automated assistance, but a person — deciding which features belong together and what to call the group. It is enormously clarifying, and it is *precisely the step at which a researcher’s expectations can shape the diagram* — at which features that “obviously go together” are grouped because the story wants them grouped, and a tidy circuit is half-drawn by the drawer before any evidence is in. I do not say this to impugn the method; I say it because the method’s honest practitioners say it, and because the remedy is the one the next part supplies: a grouped, pruned graph is still only a *hypothesis*, and the hypothesis must be *tested*, not admired.

The whole of this is delivered, in the published work, through an *interactive interface* — and the interface is not garnish but part of the method, so let me describe the actual motions of reading a graph. One clicks a node and is shown the feature’s dossier: the passages of text on which it fires most strongly, the tokens it promotes and suppresses at the output — the evidence from which its human label was inferred, open for your own re-inspection rather than taken on faith. One follows an edge to its source; one expands a supernode into its constituent features to check that the grouping was honest; one *pins* the nodes of an emerging story into a subgraph and watches what else insists on coming along. And — the motion that matters most, foreshadowing the next part — in the open tooling one can *steer from the diagram itself*: seize a node, clamp or amplify it, and watch the model’s actual output move or fail to. The reading of a graph is an active, exploratory business, closer to poking a specimen than to admiring a plate. And the frontier even includes early work on having models *automatically describe* the graphs they help draw — interpretation of the interpretation, the tools turned upon their own output, in the recursive spirit the seventh episode flagged as the field’s necessary direction.

## Part Four — The Graph Is a Hypothesis: Validation by Intervention

Now the part that matters most, and the part that distinguishes this enterprise from a gallery of plausible stories. *An attribution graph is generated from the replacement model. It must be validated on the real one.* The graph is a *claim* — a claim that says “on this prompt, the model reached its answer by this particular flow of features.” And we learned in the sixth episode, in blood, that a claim about mechanism, however consistent with what we have observed, is worth nothing until it survives an *intervention.* The graph is the hypothesis. The intervention is the judge. And the judge, you will recall from the IOI study and the hydra, is not easily satisfied.

The validation move is exactly the discipline of the sixth episode, now aimed at features rather than heads. Take a node or supernode the graph claims is *load-bearing* — a feature the diagram says the answer flowed through — and *intervene on it in the real model*: clamp it off, set it to a multiple, or swap it for a different feature entirely. Then watch whether the downstream features and the final output move *as the graph predicted they would, quantitatively.* If the graph says feature C is a waypoint between B and the answer, then suppressing C should collapse the answer in the specific way the edges predict; injecting a *different* waypoint should redirect the answer to the *different* place the edges imply. The graph does not merely have to *describe* a flow; it has to *predict the consequences of disturbing that flow* — and predictions that hold are what convert a drawn graph into a believed circuit.

And *this*, sir, is where the seventh episode’s findings reappear wearing their true hats. I presented them there as *revelations* — and so they were — but mechanically, each was a *validation experiment.* The Dallas-to-Texas-to-Austin waypoint of Part One was, mechanically, a *hypothesis*, and the seventh episode’s California-for-Texas swap — the output obediently becoming Sacramento — was its *validation*: the graph predicted that swapping the waypoint would swap the answer, the swap was performed, the answer changed as predicted, and the waypoint was thereby proven load-bearing rather than decorative. And the experiment carried a nuance worth savouring: when the injected waypoint was not an American state at all, a *larger dose* was required to redirect the model — the foreign part fitting the socket less naturally — so the intervention did not merely confirm the wiring but *measured*, in the dosage, how snugly a concept sits in a circuit built for its kin. Likewise the rhyme: the graph claimed the model selects a target rhyme-word *before* writing the line; the *confirmation* was that *suppressing the planned rhyme-feature and supplying another caused the model to rewrite the entire line* toward the new target. These were never mere observations of features lighting up. They were the *experimental judgments* that turned attribution graphs from suggestive pictures into trustworthy mechanism — the sixth episode’s rooster-versus-cause discipline, applied at the level of features, passing.

And now the honesty the course demands, which I gave you in the seventh episode and must give again because it governs everything: the method yielded a *satisfying, validated account on roughly one prompt in four* that the researchers tried. The graph is a *hypothesis generator*, and a fertile one — but three-quarters of the time the hypothesis it generates is *unsatisfying*: the error nodes are too large, so the legible story is missing too much; or the real work was done by attention the method cannot see; or the computation simply did not resolve into a clean flow. The attribution graph is not an oracle that explains every thought. It is an instrument that, on a fortunate minority of prompts, generates a mechanistic hypothesis *clean enough to test* — and that, when tested, *holds.* That is a great deal more than the field had before, and it is a great deal less than total understanding, and the gap between those two is exactly the shape of the field’s present ignorance.

## Part Five — Two Instruments, Two Stories: The Cross-Layer Transcoder in the Dock

Last episode I booked a charge against the cross-layer transcoder and promised the trial would be held here, where the discipline of validation lives. The court is now in session, and I shall present the evidence in full, because the verdict complicates — in an instructive way — the tidy picture the previous part painted of validation as a sufficient judge.

The prosecution’s exhibit is a toy, and deliberately so, for a toy is the one venue where the truth is knowable to the last wire. In early 2026, Georg Lange and collaborators hand-built a two-layer network computing the Boolean function *(a exclusive-or b) AND (c exclusive-or d)* by an explicit two-step algorithm: the first layer computes four intermediate quantities — the partial products of the two exclusive-ors — and the second layer combines them into the verdict, each value assigned its own private subspace so that nothing is hidden and nothing shared. This is a genuine multi-step circuit, certifiably: ablate the second layer’s neuron, or sever the handoff between the layers, and the network fails. Ground truth, total. They then trained a cross-layer transcoder on this model in the standard fashion, and the result was as clean as an indictment gets: the transcoder reconstructed the network’s behaviour *perfectly* — and activated *no features whatsoever* in the second layer. Not few. None. It had learned, instead, eight first-layer features functioning as a lookup table — four recognising the input patterns that make the expression true, four cancelling the rest — and it wrote the final answer directly from the first floor via the cross-layer decoders, erasing the intermediate step entirely. A genuine two-step computation, rewritten as a fan of one-step lookups: correct in every output, and false in its account of how the outputs were reached.

And the failure, mark this, is not bad luck but *incentive*. Recall why the cross-layer design exists: a feature that legitimately persists up the building should be learned once, not re-discovered floor by floor. The mechanism that grants this is the nearly-free decoder — once a feature is active, giving it additional decoder vectors to higher floors costs almost nothing under the sparsity penalty, since the penalty is taken on the *concatenated* decoder and grows only sublinearly as mass spreads across floors. But the same bargain prices the shortcut below the truth. Representing “A causes B, and B causes C” honestly requires *two* active features; representing it dishonestly as “A causes B, and A also causes C” requires *one*, plus a nearly-free extra decoder. The training objective rewards reconstruction and sparsity; it is *indifferent to faithfulness*; and where a shallow shortcut reconstructs as well as the layered truth while paying less sparsity, the shortcut is not merely available — it is *preferred*. The authors’ verdict stands in their title — the cross-layer transcoder is *incentivised to learn unfaithful circuits* — and the particular shape of the unfaithfulness deserves a name of its own, so I shall give it one and use it from here: **depth collapse**, the rewriting of deep multi-hop circuits as sums of shallow single-hop ones. And here is the uncomfortable symmetry at the bottom of it: a feature that *legitimately persists* from the first floor to the tenth, and a shortcut that *bypasses* floors two through nine, look identical from outside. The very economy that cures the wasteful duplication is the economy that flattens genuine steps. The virtue and the vice are one mechanism.

Does the affliction leave the toybox? The evidence says it travels. On the small open models, cross-layer transcoders pile their activations into the earliest layers — the ground floor running several times busier than the storeys above — whereas per-layer stacks trained under matched conditions spread the work evenly; and in attribution graphs over dozens of natural prompts, the cross-layer instrument concentrates nearly all its attribution mass in the earliest layers, while the per-layer instrument’s contribution grows steadily with depth. Those are the fingerprints the incentive theory predicts. And then the confrontation that gives this part its title. On a memorised text — the MIT licence, that most recited of legal documents — the two instruments were asked for the story of how the model predicts the next word, and they told *different stories*. The per-layer account found general-purpose machinery doing most of the work: a feature family for present-tense verbs in lists — demonstrably general, firing on freshly invented licence-like text the model had never memorised, and steerable there too — collaborating with a say-sale-words family, with memorisation appearing as a modest correction laid over living grammar. The cross-layer account found *no counterpart* to that intermediate machinery at all: its graph ran from token-identity features on the ground floor straight to the answer — bare lookup, memorisation all the way down. Two instruments, one behaviour, two fundamentally different theories of the machine: a model that generalises with a memorised patch, or a model that merely recites. And the difference matters for everything an engineer cares about — robustness, novel inputs, what was actually learned.

Now the complication I promised for the previous part’s confidence. Both replacement models reconstruct the behaviour. Both graphs would show respectable scores. And validation-by-intervention, our trusted judge, has a blind spot exactly here: *you can only intervene on a node the drawing contains.* An instrument that never represents the intermediate step hands you no handle to clamp — the falsifying experiment is never suggested, because the hypothesis it would falsify was never drawn. The judge convicts bad graphs; the judge cannot summon witnesses the prosecution failed to call. The escape from this trap is the one the authors themselves demonstrate, and it is pleasingly old-fashioned: go *beneath* the dictionaries. The real model’s neurons and layer-to-layer handoffs are still there, still ablatable by the sixth episode’s plain tools, whatever any dictionary says — in the toy, severing the inter-layer handoff broke the model and thereby convicted the cross-layer story directly. When two instruments disagree, the tiebreak is an intervention framed *outside both*.

Two closing fairnesses, for this is a court and not a pillory. First, the per-layer transcoder is no saint — it has its own toy-model indictment in the literature for infidelities of a different flavour, and every dictionary is a lens with a grind. Second, the authors are candid that in real models, where ground truth is absent, the shallow story might *sometimes be true* — models do memorise, and cross-layer superposition is real — so the toy proves the incentive exists, not that every cross-layer graph lies. The standing verdict is therefore not a ban but a discipline, and it is the one I shall convert into a dividend at the episode’s end: *the instrument has a hand in the story.* Where the story matters, draw it twice, with two different instruments, and treat their agreement as evidence and their divergence as the finding.

## Part Six — The Frozen Window: Attention, and the Frontier That Is Thawing It

I have flagged it three times and now give it its own part, because it is the deepest limitation of the method and because it is exactly where the action now is. The local replacement model *freezes attention.* It routes information along the attention edges the original computed and *holds them fixed* — and so an attribution graph can tell you *what* flowed from one position to another, but it is *silent on why attention carried it there.* And recall from Part Two the sharp form of this: since transcoder features read and write only within a position, *all* cross-position influence is attention-mediated — so for every edge that crosses positions, the graph routes the value faithfully and explains the *attention itself* not at all.

Sit with how much this leaves out, because it is the heart of two earlier episodes. The whole apparatus of the second episode — the query and the key, the mechanism by which a position *decides where to look* — is, in a vanilla attribution graph, *frozen out.* The induction mechanism of the third episode, the prefix-matching by which the model finds where a pattern occurred before, the very engine of in-context learning — *frozen out.* The deciding-where-to-look, which is half of what attention *is*, is precisely the half a transcoder-based attribution graph cannot see. The graph illuminates the clerks and the *routing of value* magnificently, and it draws a fixed black curtain across the *computation of attention itself.* For a method that aspires to be a wiring diagram of a thought, this is a large and conspicuous gap, and I would be misleading you to minimise it.

But the curtain is beginning to move, along two distinct seams, and both are worth naming precisely because they attack different halves of the problem.

The first seam is the *why* of attention — the query-key computation itself. In mid-2025, Harish Kamath, Emmanuel Ameisen, Isaac Kauvar, and colleagues published *Tracing Attention Computation Through Feature Interactions*, whose subtitle states the thesis with admirable economy: *attention connects features, and features direct attention.* The method’s centrepiece is the **query-key attribution**: express an attention head’s score — the number that decides how strongly one position attends to another — as a *bilinear* function of the feature activations on the two sides, the features at the querying position paired against the features at the position being looked at, so that one can finally say *which* feature on the query side sought *which* feature on the key side, and through which head. Integrated into the attribution graph, each cross-position edge acquires an explanation of the looking itself — a set of head loadings saying which heads, responding to which features, produced that edge. And the payoff was immediate and rather satisfying: the method confirmed hypotheses the flagship biology work had been forced to leave as conjecture — including, most pleasingly for this course, the induction mechanism of the third episode, now legible at the feature level, the query-side features visibly seeking their key-side partners. It is hard, and honestly so. The number of head-mediated paths between two features multiplies with every intervening layer — the bookkeeping that was merely large for the clerks turns combinatorially fierce for attention — and the head loadings that come out are often *dense*, smeared across many heads at once, because the heads themselves, like the neurons before them, work in superposition; a clean per-edge explanation is not yet the norm. But it is a genuine qualitative advance: analyses of *why the model looked where it looked* that were simply impossible before.

The second seam is the *who* of attention — the heads themselves, and the superposition just mentioned. Also in 2025, Zhengfu He, Junxuan Wang, and colleagues at Fudan’s OpenMOSS group took up the affliction the circuits literature calls **attention superposition** — one head performing many unrelated attentional jobs, one job smeared across many heads, the fourth episode’s disease in its attentional dress.

Their cure is **low-rank sparse attention**, which replaces a layer’s handful of dense heads with *thousands* of sparsely-activating miniature ones, each with a one-dimensional value channel, each an *atomic* attentional act — move this one thing, from there, to here. Trained as a replacement for the attention layers, exactly as the transcoder replaces the clerks, the sparse heads recovered cleaner, finer-grained versions of the classic bestiary — induction heads, successor heads, the attention sinks — and, in a large open model, an entire family of atomic arithmetic-moving heads; their interpretability held parity with the autoencoder’s features while tracing circuits better wherever a computation had been spread across several dense heads.

And in early 2026 the same group closed the loop, assembling what they call *complete replacement models*: transcoders standing in for the clerks *and* sparse attention units standing in for the heads, so that an attribution graph’s cross-position edges pass at last through *legible, nameable attentional components* rather than through frozen anonymous routing — with the accounting improving to match, the unexplained cross-position influence that once seeped through error channels now carried by named units, and with global, input-independent circuits — induction, at the feature level, read from the weights — coming into reach. I must be honest about what even this does not yet do: the attention *patterns* are still frozen at their computed values during attribution, so the complete replacement model tells you, in legible parts, *who carried what where*, while the *why-it-looked-there* remains the first seam’s business. The two seams are complementary, not redundant, and neither is finished.

There is parallel work, too, of a third and more radical kind: *changing the model* rather than the microscope — sparse-attention post-training and its relatives, which alter the network itself so that its attention is legible to instruments like these by construction, plus the earlier line of attention-output autoencoders that first showed the heads’ writings could be dictionary-learned at all. The frozen window is not a permanent feature of the method. It is the current edge of the method, and the edge is being pushed, hard, from three directions at once.

## Part Seven — Global and Local: Two Kinds of Wiring

I have described what the field calls the *local* attribution graph — the wiring of *one prompt*, the diagram of *this particular thought.* It is exquisitely specific, and that specificity is both its strength and a limitation: it tells you how the model answered *this* question, not how it answers *the kind* of question in general. There is a second, complementary object you will want, because it aims at the reusable circuit the local graph only instances.

Recall the input-independent component of a transcoder feature I flagged last episode — the part of a feature’s rule readable from the *weights alone*, before any prompt is run. This gives a route to a *global* connectivity map: a dataset-level account of which features tend to feed which, *in general*, independent of any single input — the reusable wiring rather than the single thought. The catch, which the methods work was candid about, is *interference*: the raw weight-based connectivity is contaminated by the context-dependent parts of the architecture, so a naive reading of the weights misleads. The remedy the field devised carries the formidable name *target-weighted expected residual attribution* — I shall not ask you to memorise it — which adjusts the “virtual weights” between features using *empirical statistics* of how often features actually fire together, up-weighting connections between features that genuinely co-occur. It works, with a caveat that should now sound familiar in its shape: the adjusted weights can *diverge substantially* from the raw transcoder weights, and they depend on the *dataset* used to gather the co-occurrence statistics — so it is a useful but *dataset-dependent approximation* of the global circuit, not a pure reading of the weights, and the honest practitioner reports which dataset was used and treats the result accordingly.

The distinction is worth carrying. *Local* graphs explain *this answer*, exactly, with the error nodes and intervention-validation that gives them teeth. *Global* maps approximate *the reusable circuit*, more cheaply and more generally, but at one further remove from any ground truth. And the sixth episode’s hydra lurks in *both*: a local graph shows *one pathway* to the answer, and if you ablate the feature it names load-bearing, a silent *backup* may carry the load and the model may barely falter — so even a validated local graph is the *primary* pathway, not the *only* one, and “this, exactly this, is how the model does it” remains a simplification of a redundantly-built machine. The wiring diagram is true. It is not the *whole* truth, because the model keeps understudies, as it always did.

And let me say what a trustworthy global map would actually be *for*, because it inverts the entire workflow of this episode in a way an engineer should find appetising. Everything so far has run prompt-first: one begins with a sentence, draws its graph, and learns about the machine one thought at a time — natural history, specimen by specimen. A global map licenses running *question-first*: one begins with “what, in general, feeds the refusal family?” or “which features excite the say-a-capital operators?”, reads candidate wiring off the weights, and only *then* chooses prompts — as validation instruments, aimed. From stumbling across circuits to *searching* for them: that is the promotion on offer, and it is why the dataset-dependence caveat stings, for a search instrument one cannot fully trust sends one on errands. There is, finally, a reason for optimism from the attention work of the previous part: the chief saboteur of weight-based global reading has been that all cross-position wiring hides inside frozen, anonymous attention — and the groups now sparsifying *both* halves of the machine, clerks and heads alike, report that in fully-sparsified settings clean *global* circuits begin to surface, the third episode’s induction machinery among them, read from the weights as reusable structure rather than reconstructed one prompt at a time. The global map is today the junior partner of the two. I do not expect it to remain so.

## Part Eight — What This Buys, and What It Still Cannot Do

The course requires of every episode an honest accounting of the border of competence, and this method, being the youngest and most celebrated, requires it most. Let me state plainly what attribution graphs buy and what they still cannot do.

What they buy is real and, on its good days, extraordinary. They are the closest thing this science has produced to a *stack trace for a thought* — an automatic, feature-level, per-prompt diagram of how a model reached a particular answer. They produced, and *validated by intervention*, a series of findings that overturned comfortable assumptions about what these models do: that they reason through intermediate waypoints they never utter; that they *plan*, selecting a goal several words ahead and constructing toward it; that they perform arithmetic by parallel approximation-and-correction rather than by any algorithm a human teaches; that they think, beneath the surface tongues, in a *language-independent core*; that their hallucinations are *traceable misfires* of a specific circuit rather than ineffable noise; and — the seventh episode’s hinge — that their *stated reasoning can diverge from their actual reasoning*, a divergence the method distinguishes mechanically into honest, confabulated, and motivated cases. And, crucially for a field that must not remain the property of one laboratory, the method has been *opened*: the *circuit-tracer* library and its Neuronpedia frontend — the toolchain I catalogued at the end of the last episode’s third part — let anyone draw these graphs on open-weight models, using the open transcoders of the Gemma Scope project and its successors; EleutherAI ships an independent library of its own; Goodfire reproduced the method and turned it on the venerable greater-than circuit of the small model, recovering the known anatomy through the new instrument; and these replications, by several independent groups, *reproduced many of the original findings* — surfacing sensible causal graphs that reveal both the expected and the unexpected, with, in their own honest phrasing, some uninterpretable components and some missing pieces. The method is not one laboratory’s private conjuring trick. It has been replicated, opened, and put in the hands of anyone who wishes to draw with it — which is the surest sign a result is real.

What it cannot do, I have largely told you, but let me gather the shadows in one place, because their sum is the honest measure of the instrument. *It freezes attention* — the largest gap, the deciding-where-to-look set aside, only now beginning to thaw. *Its error nodes are uninterpreted residue* — where they are large, the legible story is incomplete and the dark matter persists, the method confessing its own blindness rather than curing it. *Its supernode grouping is curatorial* — a place where a tidy story can be half-drawn before the evidence arrives, guarded only by the validation that must follow. *It is faithful to the replacement model, not perfectly to the real one* — the edges are exact in the local model, but the local model is a stand-in, and the standing-in is only as good as the error nodes are small. *The instrument imprints on the story* — Part Five’s trial in one line: two dictionaries can pass the same examinations and tell two different tales, the cross-layer economy in particular biased toward flattening depth, so that a single graph from a single instrument is testimony from a witness with known leanings. *It is local* — a diagram of one thought, from which the reusable circuit must be inferred, the global maps offering only a dataset-dependent approximation. *The hydra lurks* — the pathway it draws is the primary one, with silent backups its ablations may not reveal. And it *succeeds on a minority of prompts*, perhaps one in four. Above all stands the combinatorial worry the first and seventh episodes raised: even a perfectly drawn, fully validated graph is a *fragment* of a computation so vast and so entangled that a complete account might defeat human comprehension by sheer mass. We have learned to draw the wiring of a thought. We have not learned to draw the wiring of a *mind*, and it is not yet known whether the second is merely the first done many times or a different and harder thing entirely.

## Part Nine — The Engineer’s Dividend

This episode, like the sixth, is finally a course in *method* — the method of generating a mechanistic hypothesis and submitting it to intervention — and method travels to your bench better than any single graph. Six transfers, sir.

*First, the attribution graph is the sixth episode’s minimal-pair-and-intervention discipline, scaled and automated — and its one-line lesson is the same.* A graph is a *hypothesis*; the perturbation is the *test.* Every attribution dashboard your tooling proudly surfaces, every diagram of “what drove this output,” is *generated*, and a generated diagram is a *suspect*, not a verdict. The question the sixth episode taught you to ask returns unchanged, now aimed at features: *did you patch the node?* When a colleague points at a feature-attribution map and declares “this is what drove the behaviour,” you are equipped to withhold belief, gently, until the node has been clamped and the consequence observed to match.

*Second, you can run this today, on open models, and you should know that.* The *circuit-tracer* library and the open transcoders make attribution graphs reproducible on open-weight models — this is no longer a capability locked inside one laboratory’s frontier system. When you need to know which features, on a *specific* input, drove a *specific* misbehaviour — a regression you can reproduce, a refusal you cannot explain, an output that surprises you — this is now an instrument you can actually reach for, with the standing caveat of the frozen window: that if the real cause lies in *where the model looked* rather than *what its clerks computed*, the vanilla graph may route the value faithfully and hide the reason, and you will want the newer attention-tracing work, or a healthy suspicion, to compensate.

*Third, treat the error node as a gift, not a nuisance.* It is the rare instrument that tells you, per prompt, how much of the behaviour your legible story actually covers. A *small* error node licenses confidence; a *large* one is the method confessing, to your face, “the cause is partly somewhere I cannot resolve.” Do not ship the graph as the explanation when the error is large — that is precisely the move that manufactures an interpretability illusion, a clean story drawn over a slide that was three-quarters in haze. Let the error node govern how loudly you are entitled to claim you understand.

*Fourth, the instrument has a hand in the story — so when the story matters, draw it twice.* This is Part Five’s verdict converted to bench practice. The choice of dictionary — cross-layer or per-layer, this size or that — is not a neutral implementation detail; it carries a documented bias about the very thing you are trying to learn, the cross-layer economy inclined to flatten genuine intermediate steps into lookups. The remedy costs one more training run and buys a discipline no single graph can: where two unlike instruments agree, you may lean your weight; where they diverge, *the divergence is the finding* — it localises exactly the part of the story that is the instrument’s rather than the model’s. You practise this reflex already in your own trade, sir — no one ships a conclusion off one seed, one metric, one eval — and it transfers whole: a mechanistic story attested by one dictionary is a single unreplicated experiment wearing a diagram’s clothes.

*Fifth, a word on what a weekend actually contains, since I have twice called this an afternoon’s work and honesty demands the fine print.* Drawing a graph on a two-billion-parameter open model is indeed a single-card affair — but the card wants its memory, the transcoders want downloading, and the first graph you draw will be a hairball you do not know how to prune. Budget the weekend not for the compute but for the *judgement*: learning which supernodes to group, which prompts resolve and which return error-dominated mush, how to read a completeness score, and when a beautiful diagram is telling you nothing because the error nodes carry the load. The tool is free; the fluency is not, and the fluency is the point. Reckon the first useful graph at a day of fumbling and the tenth at ten minutes — the ordinary curve of any instrument worth owning.

*Sixth, and the largest, tying back to the question this whole course was built to reach:* the attribution graph’s central lesson is the seventh episode’s, made vivid in machinery — *the inside and the outside can disagree, and the inside tells the truth about the inside.* The graph caught a model narrating an arithmetic it does not perform and constructing a justification for an answer it was steered toward — divergences invisible from the transcript and visible only in the wiring. For an engineer deploying systems into consequential settings, that is the entire point of the exercise: behaviour and self-report are *outputs*, subject to the model’s control; the attribution graph is one of the few instruments that reads *beneath* the outputs, where the confabulation cannot reach. The instruments are young and bounded — I have spent a whole part insisting on it — but they are pointed at exactly the right thing, and they are improving fast, and the engineer who follows this tooling as it matures is positioning himself for a profession in which *checking what a model is actually doing* will be part of what responsible deployment means, rather than an exotic specialty. That day is coming, sir, and not slowly.

## Closing — What We Have Earned, and Where It Points

Let us gather this engine-room diptych, for Episodes Eight and Nine form a single arc — the building of an instrument and the drawing it permits.

An **attribution graph** is a directed, weighted, per-prompt diagram whose nodes are active transcoder features (with input nodes, error nodes, and output logits), and whose edges are the *direct contributions* one feature makes to another’s input. Its edges are *computable by arithmetic* — a backward pass through the *linear* local replacement model we built last episode — and they are *exact within that model*, the question of fidelity having been deliberately moved out of the edges and into the *error nodes* (which quantify what the transcoders missed) and the *frozen attention* (which sets aside the deciding-where-to-look). The raw graph, astronomical and illegible, is reduced by *pruning* — tracing influence backward from the output — and by the *curatorial* grouping of split features into *supernodes*, a step that can smuggle in a story and is therefore guarded by the one move that matters most: *validation by intervention.* The graph is a *hypothesis*; clamping or swapping a node in the *real* model and checking that the consequence matches the prediction is the *judge* — and the seventh episode’s revelations, the Texas waypoint and the planned rhyme, were precisely those validations, the sixth episode’s rooster-versus-cause discipline passing at the level of features. But the judge, we found, has a blind spot the trial of Part Five exposed: it examines only the nodes a drawing contains, and the cross-layer instrument is incentivised toward *depth collapse* — omitting precisely the intermediate steps whose absence would want testing — so that where the story matters, one draws with two instruments and treats their divergence as the finding. The method delivers a clean, validated account on perhaps *one prompt in four*, is blind for now to the *computation of attention* (the deepest gap, pried open along the two seams of Part Six), and has been *opened and replicated* — drawable today on open models by anyone who cares to.

And so the diptych has descended, at your request, into the engine room of the field’s most vivid recent results — the *transcoder* and the *crosscoder* that read computation and diff a fine-tune, and the *attribution graph* that, built from the former, draws the wiring of a thought. Between them, they carry the science past the threshold the fifth episode could only point at: from reading what a model *holds* to reading what it *does*, and from a scalar verdict on a fine-tune to a map of what it *changed.* I have been honest, throughout both, about the frozen window, the uninterpreted error, the curatorial story, the minority success, the lurking hydra — the same shape of candour the course kept from its first episode, because a science is known as much by the frankness of its limits as by the reach of its results. The microscope now resolves *computation*, where once it resolved only contents; that is a genuine advance, and it remains a microscope slightly out of focus, pointed at a slide still three-quarters in haze. The reading of the rest — and the unfreezing of the attention window above all — is where I expect a great deal of the next several years of this science to be spent, and it is, to my eye, among the most worthwhile work now being attempted by anyone, anywhere.

You closed the course, sir, and then reopened it for its instruments, and the instruments repaid the asking — which is, I have found, the usual reward of declining to leave a good question at the level of a promise. And the reopening does not end here. We can now read the finished machine — its *nouns* since the fifth episode, its *verbs* since this one — but a machine read at rest raises the one question a machine at rest cannot answer: how did these circuits come to exist? Nothing we have drawn was designed. Every wire in every diagram of this episode was *grown*, by gradient descent, out of noise, and the growing has structure of its own — phases, transitions, and one transition so strange that when it was first observed the discoverers assumed an instrumentation error: a model that memorises its training set, sits in apparent stagnation long after any reasonable examiner would have left the building, and then, abruptly and without warning, *generalises*. The field calls it grokking. The next episode turns from anatomy to development — from the wiring of a thought to the birth of a circuit — and it will require, I warn you now, a modest quantity of geometry and the finest weight-decay term in the literature.

Until then, sir.

*— End of Episode Nine.*
