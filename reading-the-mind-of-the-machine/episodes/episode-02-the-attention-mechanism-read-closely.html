The Attention Mechanism, Read Closely

A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability — Episode Two

The Clerk We Have Not Yet Met

Good day, sir.

In our first episode I leaned, rather heavily, on a single picture: the residual stream as a shared notebook, passed up a long table of clerks, each clerk reading what is currently written and adding a contribution of his own. I asked you to hold that picture above all others, and I hope you have. And toward the end of that episode, I traced one circuit — the induction head — to show that these models really can be read; and to do it, I borrowed two words on credit. I spoke of an attention head forming a query — a question it asks — and of each position offering a key — a label advertising what it can answer — and I told you the loan would be repaid. You have, then, already seen these two machines at work: you watched a query meet a key and an attention land where it was wanted. What you have not yet seen is what they actually are, how they are computed, and why the head splits so cleanly into the two halves I promised. Today I pay the debt in full — and the dividend, as you will see, is most of the field.

Here is what we left unexamined. The clerks at our long table are not all of one kind. There are, in fact, two species of clerk, and they do entirely different work. One species processes each notebook entry in isolation — it looks only at the single position in front of it, thinks hard about that one entry, and writes its contribution. This is the feed-forward clerk, and it is, for our purposes today, the less interesting of the two, because it cannot look sideways. It cannot relate one position to another. It is a specialist working alone in a sealed room — commanding, I should note, roughly two-thirds of the model’s parameters, for the sealed rooms are where most of the payroll goes; the supplement devotes a full episode to what happens inside them.

The other species is the one that makes a transformer a transformer. It is the clerk who is permitted to look up and down the table — to read what was written at other positions, earlier in the text, and to bring information across from there to here. This is the attention clerk. And here is the fact that makes it the centre of the whole field: attention is the only mechanism in the entire architecture by which information at one position in the text can influence the computation at another. Every instance of “something said earlier in the passage affects what happens here” — every act of staying on topic, resolving a pronoun, completing a pattern, obeying an instruction given three paragraphs ago — must, without exception, be routed through an attention head. The feed-forward clerks cannot do it; they are sealed in their rooms. If information must travel between positions, it travels by attention or it does not travel at all.

That is why the most legible and most famous circuits in this science live in attention. It is where the movement of information happens, and movement is what a circuit, fundamentally, is. So today we open up the attention clerk and look at exactly how he works. And the central discovery — the one I want you to carry out of this episode the way you carried the notebook out of the last — is this: a single attention head, which appears to be one thing, is in truth two independent machines wearing one coat. There is a machine that decides where to look. And there is a quite separate machine that decides what to write down once it has looked. Where, and what. These two are not entangled. They can be studied apart. And once you see the head split cleanly down that seam, an enormous amount of the field falls into place.

A precise subject, sir. Let us be precise about it.

Part One — What Attention Is For, Before How It Works

Let me establish the purpose before the mechanism, because the mechanism is fiddly and you will tolerate the fiddliness far better if you already know what it is in aid of.

Consider a concrete need. The model is reading a sentence, and it arrives at the word “it.” To predict anything sensible about what follows, the model must work out what “it” refers to — a dog mentioned two sentences ago, perhaps, or a proposal, or a country. The information required to resolve “it” is not located at the position of the word “it.” It is located back where the dog, or the proposal, or the country, was first named. So the model has a transport problem. It needs to reach back to that earlier position, retrieve the relevant information, and bring it forward to the present position where the prediction is being made.

That is the whole job of attention: selective retrieval across positions. At each position, an attention head surveys the earlier positions in the text, decides which of them are relevant to the work at hand, and copies information from the relevant ones into the present position’s entry in the notebook. It is, if you like, a clerk who may walk back along the table, read what earlier clerks wrote, and carry a copy of the useful pages forward to his own desk.

Now, two features of this job will turn out to matter enormously, so let me name them at once.

The first: the head must choose where to look. It cannot copy from everywhere — that would be to drown the present position in the entire prior text. It must be selective, attending strongly to a few relevant positions and ignoring the rest. The machinery of choosing where to look is the first of our two machines.

The second: having chosen where to look, the head must decide what to take from the chosen position and how to transform it on the way over. It does not necessarily copy the earlier entry verbatim. It may extract some particular aspect of it. The machinery of what to take and how to write it is the second of our two machines.

Where to look. What to take. Hold those two phrases. The entire mechanical content of this episode is the discovery that these two questions are answered by two separate, independent pieces of the head — and that this separation, which the original engineers introduced for reasons of mere computational tidiness, is the master key to reading attention circuits.

Part Two — The Mechanism, Built Slowly

Now the fiddly part. I shall build it one piece at a time, and I shall use no symbols, only words, because the synthesiser reading this aloud would make a dog’s dinner of the symbols and because, in truth, the words are clearer.

We begin at a single position in the text — call it the present position, the one currently doing the looking. Written in the notebook at this position is the residual-stream vector: that long list of numbers, twelve thousand of them in a large model, which we met last time. From this one vector, the attention head computes three different things by three different transformations. Three lenses onto the same notebook entry.

The first lens produces what is called the query. The query is the present position’s question — a vector that encodes, in some learned code, “here is what I am looking for.” If the present position is the word “it,” the query might encode something like “I am a pronoun seeking a recently-mentioned animate noun.” The query is computed from the present position’s notebook entry, and from that alone.

The second lens produces the key. But — and this is the crucial asymmetry — the key is computed at every position, not just the present one. Each position in the text produces a key, which is a vector encoding “here is what I have to offer; here is the label under which I am filed.” The position where the dog was named produces a key encoding something like “I am a recently-mentioned animate noun.” The key is each position’s advertisement of what it contains.

Now the head must decide where the present position should look. And it does so by comparing the query against every key. For each earlier position, it measures how well that position’s key matches the present position’s query — how aligned they are, how much the advertisement answers the question. The measure of alignment is a particular arithmetic operation between two vectors — the dot product, the same operation we used last time to ask “how much of the stream points along this direction.” A large value means the key strongly answers the query; a small or negative value means it does not. (The raw scores are, in practice, scaled down by a fixed constant tied to the width of the lenses — bookkeeping that keeps them in the polite range the next step handles gracefully, and that the desk records exactly.) So the present position, with its single query, obtains a score for every earlier position: how relevant is each, to me, now.

These raw scores are then passed through a step called the softmax, and this step does something specific and consequential, so let us make it precise. The softmax takes the whole collection of scores and converts them into a set of weights that are all positive and that sum to exactly one. It is, in effect, the dividing-up of a single fixed unit of attention among all the candidate positions. A position with a high score gets a large share; a position with a low score gets a vanishing one; but the total quantity of attention distributed is always, immutably, one whole unit. The head has exactly one unit of attention to spend each time, and the softmax decides how to apportion it.

This fixed budget has a consequence worth pausing on — and you may find, sir, that you have already met its symptoms in your own machine room. A head that finds nothing worth attending to cannot simply attend to nothing; the budget must be spent somewhere. It is obliged to point its one unit somewhere, even when it has no real use for any position. That obligation is the origin of a curious institution we shall inspect properly later this very episode, when we take the census of heads — and of a serving trick, later still, that you may already be relying on. For now, simply hold that attention is a fixed unit, divided up.

So: we have, at the present position, a set of attention weights — a verdict on where to look, expressed as “spend this much of my attention here, this much there.” That verdict, that pattern of weights, is called the attention pattern. And I want you to notice precisely which ingredients went into computing it: the queries and the keys, and nothing else. The attention pattern — the decision of where to look — is a function of queries and keys alone. Mark that. It will be half of the master key.

We are not finished, because deciding where to look is not the same as actually retrieving anything. Now comes the third lens. At every position, the head computes a value — a third vector, again derived from that position’s notebook entry, encoding “here is the actual content I will hand over to anyone who attends to me.” The query was the question; the key was the advertisement; the value is the goods themselves.

The head now does the retrieval. It takes the value at each position, weights it by how much attention the present position decided to pay that position, and sums them all up. A position attended to strongly contributes most of its value; a position ignored contributes almost none. The result is a single vector: a blend of the values from across the text, dominated by the values at the positions the head chose to attend to. Finally, this blended vector is passed through one last transformation — the output projection — which formats it for writing, and the result is added into the present position’s notebook entry. Added, you note — not substituted. Exactly as the first episode insisted: every clerk adds his contribution to the shared stream.

And now notice, with equal care, which ingredients went into computing what got written: the values and the output projection. The content that the head deposits into the notebook is a function of values and the output formatting — not of queries and keys. The queries and keys decided where; the values and output projection decided what.

There is one more wrinkle I owe you, because it governs everything and it is mercifully simple. A position is only permitted to attend backward — to itself and to positions earlier in the text, never to positions later. This is called causal masking, and the reason is that the model is in the business of predicting the next token from the preceding ones; it would be cheating, and useless, to let a position peek at words that have not yet arrived. So every head looks only over its own shoulder, never ahead. A clerk may walk back along the table, never forward.

That is the full mechanism of one attention head. Three lenses — query, key, value — turning each notebook entry into a question, an advertisement, and a parcel of goods; a comparison of questions against advertisements to decide where to look; a weighted gathering of goods from the chosen places; and an addition of the result back into the notebook. Fiddly, as promised. But before we step back and admire the seam, I must confess to a hole in the account — one you may already have spotted.

Part Three — Knowing Where You Stand: Position, and the Rotary Trick

Here is the hole. Nothing in the mechanism I have just described knows where anything is. The query is computed from the content of the present entry; the keys are computed from the content of the earlier entries; the dot product compares content against content. If some prankster shuffled the words of the text into a random order, every query would meet every key exactly as before, and every score would come out identical. The mechanism, as stated, is blind to position — it knows what was said but has no idea where, no sense of near or far, of before or just-before. And yet I have spoken, breezily, of a head that attends “exactly one step back.” One step back along what? By the account so far, the clerk knows the contents of every page but not their order in the book — a deficiency that would tell, sir, in any line of work.

So positional information must be supplied, deliberately, from outside the attention arithmetic. The question of how turns out to matter a great deal, and the field’s answer has changed once already in the architecture’s short life.

The original arrangement was the obvious one: stamp each notebook entry, at the very bottom of the stack, with a vector encoding its absolute address — I am token four hundred and twelve — and let the entry carry the stamp upward, where queries and keys, being computed from the entry, would inherit a whiff of it. Some models learned these address stamps; others used a fixed mathematical pattern. It worked, after a fashion. But notice what the scheme actually offers: absolute addresses, when nearly everything language cares about is relative. Grammar does not want to know that a word is the four-hundred-and-twelfth; it wants to know that this word is one before that one, that the verb is a few back from its subject, that the quotation opened recently. Under absolute stamps, a head wishing to attend “one step back” must learn the relationship between address four hundred and twelve and address four hundred and eleven, and separately between thirteen and twelve, and so on for every address — the same simple relation, memorised over and over in different clothes. Worse, addresses beyond the lengths seen in training were simply foreign to the model — a fact that used to make long documents a genuine adventure.

The modern arrangement — now close to universal in the open-weights models you deal with professionally — is cleverer, and it is called the rotary positional embedding. The idea is this. Rather than stamping addresses onto the notebook entries, we interfere, gently and systematically, with the queries and keys themselves, at the moment just before they are compared. Specifically: each query and each key is rotated — turned, pairs of dimensions at a time, through an angle proportional to its position in the text. The further along the text a position sits, the further its query and key have been turned. And the different pairs of dimensions are turned at different speeds — some are second hands, sweeping briskly with every token; some are minute hands; some are hour hands, creeping around over thousands of tokens. Each position in the text thereby wears, distributed across its query and key, the face of a many-handed clock showing its place in the document.

Now the consequence, which is the entire point and a small piece of geometric elegance. When a rotated query meets a rotated key in the dot product, the two rotations partially cancel — and what survives depends only on the difference between the two angles. And since each angle is proportional to its position, the difference of angles is proportional to the difference of positions. The score between a query and a key therefore depends on their relative offset — three tokens apart, one token apart — and not a whit on where in the document the pair happens to sit. Hold two clock faces side by side: you can read the elapsed time between them without caring what hour either shows. The head that wishes to attend “exactly one step back” now needs to learn just one thing — the fixed angular signature of adjacency — and that single lesson is valid at position ten and position ten thousand alike. Relative structure, learned once, applied everywhere. That, mechanically, is how the previous-token head of our first episode knows its business.

Two footnotes, and the second touches your bench, sir. The first: notice that the rotation is applied to queries and keys only — the values pass untouched. Position, in this scheme, influences where a head looks and leaves no stamp on what it carries. The architecture has quietly filed position with the routing department rather than the cargo department — and you will see in a moment, when we come to the seam, that this is exactly the department where one would wish it filed.

The second footnote is the practical dividend. Because position lives in those rotation speeds, it can be renegotiated after training. A model trained on contexts of a certain length, asked to serve far longer ones, meets rotation angles it has never seen — the hands of the clock in positions no training example ever produced. But slow the hands — rescale the rotation speeds so that the old, familiar range of angles is stretched to cover the new length — and the model finds the geometry recognisable again. This trick, in its several refinements, is how practitioners have repeatedly stretched context windows well past their training lengths with only modest fine-tuning, and it is a pleasing early instance of the bargain this course keeps advertising: understand the mechanism, and the mechanism becomes adjustable. There are rival schemes — one simply taxes attention scores in proportion to distance, which has a certain fiscal directness — but rotation is the incumbent, and the incumbent is well dug in.

The hole is patched. The clerk knows the order of the pages. Now we may step back and look at the seam.

Part Four — The Seam: One Head, Two Machines

Here is the observation that founded the modern science of transformer circuits, and it is, once you have the mechanism in hand, almost embarrassingly simple. I shall state it and then earn it.

The observation: the attention pattern — the decision of where to look — and the content moved — the decision of what to write — are computed by entirely separate parts of the head, from entirely separate sets of parameters, and neither depends on the other. The head, which presents itself as a single integrated operation, in fact factors cleanly into two independent circuits. The field gives them rather ungainly names, and to spare the synthesiser I shall translate. The first I shall call the query-key circuit — the where-to-look machine. The second I shall call the output-value circuit — the what-to-write machine.

Let me earn the claim by tracing the dependencies, which we have already laid out and need only collect. The attention pattern came from queries and keys. The queries and keys came, by their two lenses, from the notebook entries. So the where-to-look machine is: read the notebook, decide which positions should attend to which. It produces a pattern of weights. It touches the values not at all; it does not know or care what content lives at any position. It is concerned purely with relevance — with the geometry of questions and advertisements.

The content moved came from values and the output projection. The values came, by their lens, from the notebook entries. So the what-to-write machine is: given that the present position attends to some earlier position, take that position’s content, transform it, and write it here. It produces the actual deposit into the notebook. And it touches the queries and keys not at all; it does not decide where to look. Hand it an attention pattern from outside, and it will faithfully move content according to that pattern, indifferent to how the pattern was chosen.

So the two machines are genuinely decoupled. The where-to-look machine settles the routing; the what-to-write machine settles the cargo. You could, in principle, take the routing from one head and the cargo-handling from another and bolt them together, and each piece would do its job without complaint. Where and what are orthogonal questions, answered by orthogonal hardware.

I shall add one refinement for the connoisseur, because it sharpens the picture and it changed how the field does its sums. Notice that the query lens and the key lens never act separately — every use to which either is ever put is the comparison of one against the other. The mathematics duly obliges: the two lenses collapse into a single combined object, one transformation that takes a pair of notebook entries and returns a relevance score directly. And likewise on the other side of the seam: the value lens and the output projection collapse into a single transformation that takes an attended entry and returns the deposit to be written. The four lenses, in other words, pair off into two products — and only the products are real. You could alter the query lens however you pleased, compensate precisely in the key lens, and the head’s behaviour would not change by a hair; so there is no fact of the matter about what a query “really is” apart from the keys it is destined to meet. It is rather like a lock and its key, sir: the cutting of either, taken alone, is an arbitrary convention; the engineering fact is the fit. When the field analyses a head, accordingly, it analyses the two combined machines — never the four lenses separately, which would be studying the bookkeeping rather than the books.

Now, why does this decomposition matter so enormously for interpretability? Two reasons, and the second is subtle and important.

The first reason is simply that it halves the problem. To understand a head, you no longer face one tangled object; you face two simpler ones, and you can interrogate each on its own. You can ask of the where-to-look machine: under what circumstances does this head attend from one position to another? — and answer it by studying that machine in isolation. And you can ask of the what-to-write machine: given that it attends somewhere, what does it copy, and how does it transform it? — and answer that in isolation too. Two clean questions instead of one muddy one.

The second reason concerns where in the notebook each machine reads and writes, and it returns us to a theme from the first episode. Recall that the residual stream has twelve thousand dimensions, but that the queries, keys, and values are much smaller — a hundred or so dimensions, typically. This is forced by the arithmetic: the lenses that produce queries, keys, and values are transformations that compress the twelve-thousand-dimensional notebook entry down into a few-hundred-dimensional query or key or value. They are, in the mathematician’s term, low-rank — they can only see, and only express, a small subspace of the full stream.

The consequence is lovely and worth dwelling on. Because the where-to-look machine reads through these compressing lenses, it does not see the whole notebook entry when it decides where to attend. It sees only a small slice of it — a few hundred directions out of twelve thousand. It is, in effect, reading only a particular column of the notebook to make its routing decision, blind to the rest. Likewise the what-to-write machine reads only a particular slice of the attended position’s entry, and writes only into a particular slice of the present position’s entry. Each head, in other words, reads from a few directions of the residual stream and writes to a few directions of it — and is otherwise entirely uninvolved with the vast remainder.

This is the deep reason that many heads can share one notebook without trampling each other. Each head has staked out its own small subspace to read from and its own small subspace to write to. They are like a great many clerks all annotating the same enormous ledger, but each confined to his own narrow column — one minding the dates, another the sums, another the marginal cross-references — so that all may work the same page at once without collision. We met, last episode, the idea that concepts share the residual stream by pointing in nearly-perpendicular directions. Here is its operational cousin: heads share the residual stream by reading and writing in nearly-separate subspaces. The notebook is roomy, and everyone has agreed, more or less, to keep to his own column.

Part Five — Many Heads, and the Curious Fact of No Privileged Direction

I have been speaking of a head, in the singular, but of course a real model has a great many. A large model may have something like a hundred heads at each of its ninety-odd layers — many thousands of heads in all. Let me say briefly how they coexist, because it sharpens the picture and because it sets up a point I shall need in two episodes’ time.

Within a single layer, the heads operate in parallel and independently. Each has its own three lenses — its own way of forming queries, keys, and values — and therefore its own where-to-look machine and its own what-to-write machine. Each surveys the text, makes its own routing decision, gathers its own cargo, and writes its own contribution into the notebook. And then — exactly as the first episode’s additive principle demands — all their contributions are summed into the residual stream. The layer’s total effect on the notebook is just the sum of what its many heads each independently decided to add. No head consults another within the layer; they are simultaneous and separate, and the stream simply collects them all.

This means that different heads, even in the very same layer, can be doing utterly different jobs — and, as we shall see in a moment, they do, with a specialisation that would gratify a guild.

But first, the point I promised, which concerns the geometry of the notebook and which I raised in passing last time. I claimed, last episode, that the model stores concepts as directions, and that these directions need not, and generally do not, line up with the coordinate axes — with the individual numbers in the list. I asked you to take that partly on faith, the Othello board notwithstanding. I can now give you the structural reason, and it is a small jewel of an argument.

The residual stream has what is called no privileged basis. Let me unpack the phrase. A “basis” is a choice of axes — a set of directions you decide to call the coordinate directions, against which everything else is measured. To say a space has no privileged basis is to say that no particular choice of axes is special — that you could rotate the entire coordinate system to any orientation you liked, and nothing about the model’s behaviour would change. The reason this holds for the residual stream is precisely the low-rank lenses we just discussed. Every head reads the stream only through its query, key, and value transformations, and writes to it only through its output projection. If you were to rotate the coordinates of the stream, you could equally rotate all those transformations to match, and the products — the actual numbers the model computes — would be identical. The rotation washes out. In the architecture as drawn on the blackboard, the model cannot tell, and does not care, which way the axes point.

And here is the consequence that justifies last episode’s claim. If no orientation of the axes is special, then there is no reason for a concept to align itself with any particular axis. A concept — the “French” direction, say — is free to point any which way, at any angle to the coordinate directions, because the coordinate directions are themselves arbitrary. So features are directions, full stop, and reading a feature off a single coordinate — the naive one-number-per-idea theory we demolished last time — is hopeless not by accident but by the deep symmetry of the architecture. The notebook has no preferred handwriting direction; the clerks write at whatever slant they please.

One honest asterisk, sir, before we bank the doctrine — and I give it to you because you of all listeners will collide with it at the bench. The beautiful symmetry is a property of the idealised architecture, and training does not entirely respect it. The normalisation layers between the blocks carry a small learned scale for each individual coordinate — parameters nailed to the axes — and the optimiser itself treats every coordinate separately when it updates the weights. Between them they leave fingerprints: trained models reliably develop a handful of outlier dimensions, individual coordinates of grotesquely outsized magnitude — the very rogue coordinates that make activation quantisation such a delicate business on your own bench. So state the doctrine with care. The architecture assigns the axes no meaning, and a concept has no reason whatever to align itself with them — that argument stands, and everything we built upon it stands. But the training process does leave the axes faintly marked, and the flat claim that nothing in a real residual stream knows where they point would be tidier than the truth. File the outlier dimensions where you keep the tuned lens: a reminder that the specimen on the bench is always a little less symmetrical than the one on the blackboard.

I flag, and set aside for Episode Four, that one part of the model is different — the interior of the feed-forward clerk, where a certain operation does break this symmetry and does single out the coordinate axes as special. That exception is the seed of a great deal of trouble, and it is the reason the word neuron still means something, faintly, in one corner of the model. But that is two episodes away. For now: the residual stream has no privileged direction, and therefore features point everywhere, and therefore we shall need cleverer instruments than “read off the numbers” to find them.

I did, however, promise you the specialisation of the heads, and it deserves better than a passing remark. The cataloguing of heads is one of the field’s oldest occupations, and the catalogue is worth an excursion of its own.

Part Six — A Field Guide to Heads

When the interpretability researcher confronts a new model, one of the first orders of business is a census: go head by head, and determine what each one does. The seam tells us how to conduct it. For each head, interrogate the two machines separately — show the where-to-look machine a great variety of texts and observe where its attention lands; inspect the what-to-write machine to see what cargo it carries when it gets there. The work has the flavour of Victorian natural history — long days in the field, a notebook of one’s own, the gradual emergence of species — and like Victorian natural history it has produced a recognisable fauna. A surprising number of heads turn out to have crisp, nameable jobs, and the same species recur, model after model, with the regularity that the first episode taught us to call universality. Let me give you the principal specimens.

The previous-token head you have already met: it attends, from every position, exactly one step back, and carries forward a summary of what it finds there. The rotary clock is what makes its trick mechanically possible; the induction circuit is what makes it famous. There are cousins at other fixed offsets, and heads that attend preferentially to the beginning of the current sentence or clause — the positional family generally, navigating by the clock more than by content.

The duplicate-token head is the natural-born detective of the family: it attends, from a token, back to earlier occurrences of the very same token. Its where-to-look machine asks, in effect, “where have I appeared before?” — the query naming the token’s own identity, the keys answering in kind. When a name like Fink-Nottle turns up a second time in a passage, it is a duplicate-token head that first registers the recurrence — a name of that sort, once encountered, not being readily mistaken for another. Remember this specimen; it will testify at length in our sixth episode.

The successor head is a more startling find. It attends to a token that belongs to some conventional ordered sequence — a day of the week, a month, a numeral, a letter of the alphabet — and what its what-to-write machine deposits is a push toward the next element of the sequence. Shown Monday, it promotes Tuesday; shown March, April; shown seven, eight. Monday is followed by Tuesday with a reliability one wishes attached to certain other engagements. The head amounts to a little learned increment operation — and the same specimen has been found across model families of quite different sizes and pedigrees, which is universality again: incrementation, it seems, is a tool every model decides it needs, and they all build it in recognisably the same place.

There are heads that attend from a pronoun back to candidate referents; heads that track quotation marks, attending to where the current quotation opened; heads that mind the subject of the sentence across its interruptions. There are the negative heads I mentioned in our first episode — the objectors — whose cargo opposes a prediction that other components are promoting; the best-studied of them attend to a token that has appeared before and write, precisely, a vote against repeating it, a learned check on the model’s own enthusiasm for parroting itself. And there are the name-mover heads, which fetch a person’s name from earlier in a passage and deposit it at the point of prediction — the star witnesses of the sixth episode’s courtroom drama, where we shall watch them, their backups, and their saboteurs all at work in a single traced circuit.

And then there is the institution I promised in Part Two. Survey real attention patterns at scale and you find something that looks, at first, like an epidemic of idleness: head after head spending the bulk of its attention, on the bulk of tokens, on the very first position in the text — a position that frequently holds nothing more nutritious than a ceremonial beginning-of-text marker. Naive inspection concludes that the model is fascinated by its own first word. The truth is our old friend the fixed budget. A head whose trigger is absent — the duplicate-token head in a passage with no duplicates, the quotation head in a passage with no quotations — must spend its unit of attention somewhere, and the model has learned to designate the first position as the place where unwanted attention is dumped: a wastepaper basket, institutionalised. The field calls it the attention sink. The head is not studying the first token; it is declining, in the only grammar available to it, to study anything. Many heads thus spend most of their existence dormant at the sink, springing to life only when their particular quarry appears in the text — a pattern less like an office of busy clerks than like a fire brigade: idle, by design, nearly always.

The catalogue should also come with a warning label, and I would be failing in my duties if I did not affix it. Attention patterns are the most photogenic objects in this entire science — bright heat-maps showing which words look at which — and they have accordingly become the most popular, and the most carelessly read. The temptation is to treat the pattern as the explanation: the model attended from here to there, and therefore that is why it did what it did. But you now know the seam, and the seam says otherwise. Attention is only the routing; whether the routing matters depends entirely on the cargo, and the cargo is the other machine’s department. A head may attend strongly to a position while its what-to-write machine carries essentially nothing of consequence from it — the clerk may stare at a page for minutes, sir, and write nothing; the staring is data, not verdict. And the sink makes matters worse, since the single largest destination of attention in the whole model is, by construction, meaningless. The corrective discipline is always the same: never read the where-machine without auditing the what-machine. Much of the early literature’s enthusiasm for attention maps as explanations did not survive this audit, and the field’s standards are the better for the funeral.

Part Seven — The Engineer’s Dividend: An Archive of Keys and Values

I promised in the first episode that understanding mechanisms pays engineering dividends, and attention supplies the clearest cases on record — several of which are sitting, at this moment, in your serving stack, sir. Permit me a brief excursion to the machine room. It will cost us nothing; the mechanism we have built this hour is exactly the mechanism that runs the bill.

Consider what happens when a model generates, one token at a time. To produce the next token, the newest position must form its queries and compare them against the keys of every position before it, then gather the corresponding values. But here is the saving grace, and it follows from causal masking: the keys and values of the past positions never change. A past position attends only backward; nothing the future does can revise its notebook entry, and so its keys and its values, once computed, are final. It would be madness to recompute them for every new token — and so nobody does. They are computed once and archived: every position’s keys and values, for every head, at every layer, held in memory for the rest of the conversation. The field calls this archive the key-value cache, and the act of generation is thereby reduced, at each step, to one new query interrogating a great stored library of old advertisements and old goods.

Now do the bookkeeping, because the bookkeeping is where the economics of your profession lives. The archive grows with every token: per token, per layer, per head, a key and a value. For a large model this comes to a meaningful fraction of a megabyte for every single token of context — multiply by a context of a hundred thousand tokens and the archive for one conversation runs to tens of gigabytes, a warehouse beginning to rival the model’s own weights. The celebrated cost of long contexts is, at bottom, the cost of warehousing advertisements and goods; and the speed of generation is governed less by arithmetic than by how fast that warehouse can be read. When you size a batch, sir, you are, whether the dashboard says so or not, allocating shelving.

Once the structure is seen, the optimisations almost propose themselves — and each is an interpretability observation wearing overalls. The first: notice that the diversity a layer needs lives mostly in its questions. Many heads asking many different questions is the whole point of multi-headed attention; but must every head also keep its own shelf of advertisements and goods? The field’s answer was no: let groups of query-heads share a single set of keys and values among them. This is grouped-query attention, standard in the current generation of open-weights models, and it shrinks the archive several-fold — commonly eight-fold — at modest cost in quality; the extreme version, every query-head in a layer sharing one communal shelf, goes by multi-query attention. For the cataloguer of heads there is small print worth reading: in such models the where-to-look machines remain individual, but the cargo is communal — the heads in a group may ask different questions, yet whatever position they attend to, they draw from the same shelf of goods — each retaining only its own way of formatting the shared cargo for deposit. The seam, you notice, is what makes the bargain even thinkable: routing and cargo are separate machines, so one may be economised without touching the other.

The second optimisation is my favourite, because its pedigree is pure interpretability. Recall the attention sink — the wastepaper basket at position one. When a conversation outruns the archive and something must be evicted, the obvious policy is to discard the oldest tokens first. Try it naively and the model degrades — not gracefully, but precipitously, into incoherence. The diagnosis comes straight from the census: you have thrown away the sink. Thousands of dormant heads, obliged by the softmax to spend their budget somewhere, have lost the somewhere — and their obligatory attention, denied its basket, smears across the remaining positions as noise in every layer at once. The remedy, once the mechanism is understood, is almost comically cheap: when evicting, always keep the first few tokens, forever, alongside a sliding window of recent ones, and discard from the middle. Do that, and models stream coherently over millions of tokens. The fix arrived in the very paper that named the sink — diagnosis and remedy published in one stroke — and it is the cleanest specimen I can offer you of this course’s standing bargain: a mechanism read is a mechanism exploited. And the bargain has since closed its loop at the factory itself: the newest generation of open models ships with the basket designed in — a dedicated sink token, or a small learned escape valve in each head’s softmax — the institution the field discovered by census now installed by the architects on purpose. The seam, meanwhile, has yielded a third exploitation: DeepSeek’s latent attention, which compresses the shelf itself — keys and values held as a single shared low-rank latent, unpacked only at the moment of use — shrinking the warehouse not by sharing shelves but by folding them. And the newest open models of the Gemma class economise along the other axis entirely, interleaving layers that attend only to a recent sliding window with the occasional full-span layer, so that most of the archive is simply never kept. The remainder of the menagerie — the quantising of the archive, the paging of it as an operating system pages memory — I leave to your professional literature, which I believe keeps you adequately supplied.

The excursion is over; back to the science. We have heads in the singular, and a census of their species. What we do not yet have is the thing that makes them an intelligence rather than a guild of savants: cooperation.

Part Eight — Composition: How Heads in Different Layers Conspire

Everything so far has concerned heads acting alone. But the real power — and the source of every interesting circuit in this science — comes from heads cooperating across layers. And the mechanism of their cooperation follows directly from the additive notebook, so directly that you could almost derive it yourself.

Here is the situation. A head in an early layer reads the notebook, does its work, and adds its contribution to the stream. That contribution now sits in the notebook. The stream flows upward. A head in a later layer then reads the notebook — and what it reads includes the earlier head’s contribution, because nothing erased it. So the later head’s queries, keys, and values are computed from a notebook entry that the earlier head helped to write. The later head is, in effect, reading the earlier head’s output as part of its input. This is called composition, and it is how isolated heads become multi-step algorithms.

Now recall that the later head has three lenses — query, key, value — and the earlier head’s contribution can flow into any of them. This gives three distinct flavours of composition, and the distinctions matter, because different circuits use different flavours.

The first flavour I shall call query composition. Here, the earlier head writes something into the notebook that the later head reads through its query lens — so the earlier head influences what the later head is looking for. The earlier head, you might say, poses the later head’s question. It reaches forward and adjusts the query, changing the target of the later head’s search.

The second flavour I shall call key composition. Here, the earlier head writes something that the later head reads through its key lens — so the earlier head influences which positions advertise themselves to the later head. The earlier head is relabelling the filing system, changing the advertisements that the later head’s query will be compared against. This one is subtle, and it is the heart of the most famous circuit in the field, so hold it with particular care: an early head can write, at some position, a note that changes how that position advertises itself to a head in the layer above.

The third flavour I shall call value composition. Here, the earlier head’s contribution flows into the later head’s value lens — so it changes what content the later head will carry when it attends to that position. The earlier head is altering the goods on the shelf, so that when the later head retrieves from that position, it retrieves something the earlier head placed there.

Where to look — through queries and keys — and what to carry — through values. Composition can reach into any of the three. And when it does, the two heads together compute something neither could compute alone. The field sometimes speaks, evocatively, of virtual heads — the idea that a pair of composed heads behaves like a single, more sophisticated head that does not physically exist anywhere in the model but emerges from the collaboration. Two clerks, each simple, who by leaving notes for one another in the shared ledger accomplish jointly a task that would baffle either singly.

I want you to feel the shape of what composition buys, even before we see a specific instance next episode, because it is the whole reason depth matters. A single head can only attend based on what is directly written at each position — the raw content of the notebook as it stands. But a composed pair can attend based on something computed — because the early head can first write a derived fact into the notebook, and the later head can then attend based on that. Depth, in other words, lets the model attend not merely to what is there but to what it has worked out. The notebook is not only read; it is progressively annotated, and later clerks may act on the annotations of earlier ones. That progressive annotation is the source of nearly everything sophisticated a transformer does.

Part Nine — The Ladder: What One Layer Can and Cannot Do

To make the value of composition concrete — to show you that depth is not a luxury but a hard necessity for certain computations — let me walk up a little ladder of toy models, each one layer taller than the last. This is a classic pedagogical device in the field, and it is illuminating precisely because at each rung we can say exactly what the model can and cannot express.

The bottom rung: a model with zero attention layers. Strip out attention entirely. Such a model can do nothing but map the current token, in isolation, to a guess about the next one — because with no attention, no information crosses between positions, and each position sees only itself. What it learns is therefore a bigram table: for each possible current word, the marginal likelihood of each possible next word. “The word ‘the’ is often followed by a noun.” A lookup table of pairs, and nothing more. It has no notion of context whatever; the words before the current one might as well not exist. A creature of pure local reflex.

The next rung: a model with one attention layer. Now information can cross between positions — once. A single head can attend from the present position back to one earlier position and bring something forward. What can such a model express? It can learn what are called skip-trigrams — patterns of the form “if a certain word appeared somewhere earlier, and the current word is such-and-such, then predict so-and-so.” The where-to-look machine selects which earlier word to attend to; the what-to-write machine copies or transforms it into a prediction. This is a real gain over bigrams: the model can now let a distant word influence the present guess. If the word “open” appeared earlier, the present word “the” might be steered toward “door.”

But here is the instructive part — the defect of the one-layer model, which exposes exactly what a single attention step cannot do. Because the where-to-look machine and the what-to-write machine are decoupled, and because each sees only its own endpoints, a single head can attend to an earlier word and copy something about that word — but it cannot condition what it copies on a second word. It can say “having attended to ‘open’, predict ‘door’.” What it cannot cleanly say is “having attended to ‘open’, predict whatever word followed ‘open’ last time” — because to do that, it would need to first know what followed ‘open’, which is a fact about a different position, a fact that no single attention step has had the chance to compute and write down. The one-layer head is stuck attending on the basis of raw tokens and copying on the basis of raw tokens. It cannot attend on the basis of a relationship between tokens, because computing that relationship would itself require an attention step, and it has spent its only one.

This shows up as a characteristic class of errors — the field calls them skip-trigram bugs — where the one-layer model, having learned “attend to a quotation mark and expect another quotation mark,” cannot distinguish which quotation mark should match which, and produces confidently wrong completions. The bug is not a failure of training; it is a mathematical limit of one attention layer. The head simply lacks the expressive power to represent the conditional structure, and no amount of training will conjure it.

The next rung: a model with two attention layers. And now, with composition newly possible, the limit lifts. The first layer’s head can compute a relationship — for instance, “the word at this position was preceded by such-and-such” — and write that relationship into the notebook. The second layer’s head can then attend on the basis of that written-down relationship, by key composition, doing what the one-layer model provably could not: attend not to a raw token but to a token characterised by its context. And the first useful thing this newfound power produces is precisely the operation the one-layer model failed at — “find where the current pattern occurred before, and predict whatever followed it then.” That operation has a name. It is the induction head. And it is the entire subject of our next episode.

I want the moral of the ladder to land cleanly, because it justifies the architecture of everything to come. The jump from one attention layer to two is not a quantitative jump — a bit more of the same. It is a qualitative one. One layer can copy a token it has seen. Two layers can look a token up by its context. That is the difference between a clerk who can fetch a page you point to and a clerk who can fetch the page that follows the page matching a description you give him — between retrieval and associative retrieval. And the whole leap is purchased by composition: by one head writing a note that another head acts upon. Depth, in a transformer, is the depth of annotation — the number of times the notebook can be marked up before a later clerk must read it and decide. Each additional layer is one more round of “work something out, write it down, and let the next clerk build on it.” That is what we buy when we stack.

Closing — What We Have Earned, and Where It Points

Let us gather the episode.

Attention is the one mechanism by which information moves between positions in the text — the feed-forward clerks are sealed in their rooms, and so every act of relating one part of a passage to another must route through an attention head. That alone makes attention the natural home of the field’s circuits, because a circuit is, at bottom, a pattern of movement.

A single head works by three lenses upon each notebook entry — the query, the question; the key, the advertisement; the value, the goods. It compares queries against keys to decide where to look, apportioning a single fixed unit of attention by the softmax; and it gathers values according to that verdict to decide what to write, adding the result into the shared notebook. Position enters the comparison by the rotary trick — queries and keys turned like the hands of a many-speed clock, so that what the dot product sees is relative offset, the same lesson valid anywhere in the document, and renegotiable, conveniently, after training.

And the master discovery — the seam down which the head splits — is that where to look and what to write are computed by two independent machines: the query-key circuit, concerned only with relevance and routing, and the output-value circuit, concerned only with cargo. Decoupled — indeed, each a single combined object, a lock-and-key fit in which only the pairing is real — and therefore separately legible. Each, moreover, reads and writes only a small subspace of the twelve-thousand-dimensional notebook — which is how thousands of heads share one stream without collision, and which, together with the stream’s lack of any privileged direction, is why features are directions free to point anywhere.

The census conducted along that seam yields a recurring fauna — previous-token heads, duplicate-token heads, successor heads incrementing their way through the days of the week, negative heads voting against the model’s own enthusiasms, name-movers awaiting their trial in Episode Six — together with the attention sink, the institutional wastepaper basket where dormant heads park their obligatory budget; and with the standing caution that an attention pattern is routing, not explanation, until the cargo has been audited. The same mechanics, read with an engineer’s eye, run the economics of serving: the key-value cache, the archive whose shelving you allocate whenever you size a batch; grouped-query attention, which economises the goods while preserving the questions; and the sink-aware eviction that lets a model stream forever — mechanism read, mechanism exploited.

Finally, heads cooperate across layers by composition — an early head writing a note that a later head reads through its query, its key, or its value. Composition is what turns isolated heads into multi-step algorithms; it is the reason depth matters; and its first and most celebrated fruit is the induction head, the operation that a single attention layer provably cannot perform and that two layers can.

We have, in short, traded the airy notebook for its actual machinery. We know now, mechanically, who writes in the notebook, how they decide where to read, what they carry, what it costs to warehouse their goods — and we have found the one structural fact, where and what are separable, that lets a human being read an attention circuit at all.

In our next episode, we cash in the ladder’s promise. We shall take the induction head — which the first episode showed you as a conjuring trick and which this episode showed you to be possible only with two layers and composition — and we shall trace it to its last wire. And then I shall show you something that genuinely startled the people who discovered it: that a model’s ability to learn within a single prompt, with no change to its weights at all, does not develop gradually during training. It switches on, almost all at once, in a single sharp moment — and that moment is the moment these very heads are born. I shall have it ready.

Until then, sir.
