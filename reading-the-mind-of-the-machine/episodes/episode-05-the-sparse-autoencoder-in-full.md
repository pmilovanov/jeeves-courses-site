# The Sparse Autoencoder, in Full

*A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability -- Episode Five*

## The Commissioning of an Imperfect Instrument

Good day, sir.

We come to the instrument itself. In four episodes we have established the problem with some care: a model represents its meanings as *features*, which are *directions* in the residual stream; there are far more of these features than the stream has dimensions to hold straight; and so they are folded together in *superposition*, overlapping at slight angles, and tangled even into the individual neurons, which are in consequence *polysemantic* -- each one firing on a baffling assortment of unrelated concepts. We cannot read the model by reading its dimensions, for the features do not align to them. We cannot read it by reading its neurons, for each neuron is a tangle. We need an instrument that takes this folded, tangled representation and hands us back the clean, single-meaning features the model is actually using. Today we build it.

But I want to set the tone of the building carefully, because it is not the tone you might expect. The sparse autoencoder is the most celebrated instrument in this science -- the thing that, in the last two years, took interpretability from a promising idea to a field producing real results at the scale of frontier models. There is a temptation to present it as a triumph: behold, the microscope, and through it the mind of the machine laid bare. I shall not present it so, and not because it does not work -- it works, often astonishingly -- but because a microscope you do not understand the distortions of is not a scientific instrument; it is a generator of confident errors. The good valet, presenting his master with a new instrument, does not conceal its flaws to flatter the purchase. He discloses every one of them in good light, so that his master may trust the readings precisely as far as they deserve and not one inch further.

So this episode is a commissioning, conducted honestly. We shall build the microscope -- and I shall show you it has a real and beautiful pedigree, borrowed from neuroscience. Then we shall hold it to the light and I shall show you the smudges: the features it systematically *underestimates*, the features it silently *kills*, the way the very same feature *splits into several* when you build a bigger instrument -- so that the innocent question “how many features does this model have?” turns out to have no clean answer. Then I shall show you how the field has, in the last two years, *polished out* some of these smudges with cleverer designs. And then, the deepest difficulty of all, the one no polishing removes: that we have *no ground truth* -- no independent list of the model’s true features against which to check whether the microscope is showing us what is really there or what it has merely invented. After all of that, fairly, I shall show you what the instrument has *achieved* at the scale of real models, which is genuinely remarkable.

And then -- because this course was assembled later than the triumphant first reports, and I will not have you leave with yesterday’s confidence -- I shall give you the *reckoning* that arrived in twenty twenty-five and rumbles on still: a season of sharp empirical papers that asked whether this celebrated instrument actually *beats a simple ruler* at the jobs it was sold for, found, embarrassingly often, that it did not, and forced the field into a humbler and more interesting account of what the thing is genuinely *for*. An honest reckoning has both columns, and the second column has grown since the instrument’s first unveiling.

Let us begin where the idea was born, which was not in the study of language models at all.

## Part One -- The Pedigree: Dictionary Learning and the Visual Cortex

The technique we are about to build is, in its mathematical bones, an old one, and it comes to us from two older fields -- signal processing and neuroscience -- under the name **dictionary learning**, or equivalently **sparse coding**. Understanding its origin is not antiquarianism; it tells us *what the instrument was designed to do* and therefore what we may reasonably ask of it.

Here is the founding idea, and it is elegant. Suppose you have a great many signals of some kind -- let us follow the historical case and say small patches of natural images, little square fragments of photographs of the everyday world. You wish to find a *dictionary*: a collection of elementary patterns -- call them *atoms* -- such that *any* patch from your collection can be reconstructed as a combination of just a *few* atoms from the dictionary. Not a combination of *all* of them -- a combination of a *sparse* handful. The dictionary should be such that every natural image patch is *some small selection of atoms, added together*. And you do not specify the atoms in advance; you *learn* them, by searching for the dictionary that lets you reconstruct all your patches well while using as few atoms per patch as possible.

In the middle nineteen-nineties, two researchers -- the work is associated with the names Olshausen and Field -- did exactly this with natural image patches, and the result is one of the small classics of computational neuroscience. When you force a system to reconstruct natural images sparsely -- using few atoms per patch -- the atoms it learns are not arbitrary. They come out as *oriented edge detectors*: little localised patterns of light and dark at various orientations and scales, each atom responding to an edge or a bar at a particular angle in a particular place. And the reason this electrified the field is that *these are very nearly the receptive fields of the neurons in the primary visual cortex* -- the first cortical area that processes vision in mammals, whose neurons had been painstakingly characterised decades earlier and were *known* to be exactly such oriented edge detectors. The sparse-coding system, given nothing but natural images and the instruction “reconstruct these using few atoms,” had *rediscovered the representation the brain actually uses*. The conclusion drawn -- and it is the load-bearing idea for everything we do today -- is that the brain may represent the visual world by *sparse dictionary coding*: a large dictionary of features, of which only a few are active for any given input.

Now you can see the bridge to our problem, and it is almost exact. We *believe*, from the superposition story, that a language model represents its meanings as a *large dictionary of features*, of which only a *sparse* few are active on any given token. That is precisely the structure sparse coding was invented to recover. So the proposal -- and it is the founding proposal of the feature-extraction programme, the younger of modern interpretability’s two great limbs -- is: *apply dictionary learning to the model’s activations*. Take the residual-stream vectors the model produces, treat them as our “signals,” and learn a dictionary of feature-atoms such that each activation is a sparse combination of a few atoms. If the model really does use a sparse feature dictionary, this procedure should *recover* it -- should hand us back the clean features that superposition folded together. The instrument that performs this learning, in the particular form the field has settled on, is the **sparse autoencoder**.

## Part Two -- The Architecture, Built Slowly

Let me build the sparse autoencoder one piece at a time, in words. It has two halves, and they are mirror images: an *encoder* that takes an activation apart into features, and a *decoder* that puts it back together from them.

Begin with the **encoder**. It receives one residual-stream vector -- one entry from the notebook, twelve thousand numbers in a large model, folded and tangled. Its job is to *un-fold* that into a list of *feature activations* -- a long list, with one entry per feature in our dictionary, saying how strongly each feature is present. And here is the essential design choice that gives the instrument its power: *the list of features is much longer than the list we started with*. We started with twelve thousand dimensions; we ask the encoder to produce a feature-list of perhaps *hundreds of thousands*, or in the largest cases *tens of millions*, of entries. This is deliberate and it is the whole point. The model folded *many* features into *few* dimensions; to un-fold them we must give ourselves *more* slots than the model had dimensions -- enough slots to hold the folded features *separately*, one per slot, with room to spare. The autoencoder’s hidden layer is *wide* precisely so that it can spread out what the model crammed together. The field calls this *overcompleteness* -- more dictionary atoms than input dimensions -- and it is the feature, not the bug.

The encoder does its work in two steps. First, a *linear* transformation maps the twelve-thousand-dimensional activation up into the very wide feature space -- and this transformation is, in effect, *measuring how much the activation points along each feature direction*. Each row of it is one feature’s *detector*: it asks “how much of this activation lies along my direction?” Second -- and this is what makes the code *sparse* -- a *nonlinearity* is applied that *zeroes out* the vast majority of these feature activations, keeping only the few that are strongly present and setting the rest to nothing. In the simplest design this nonlinearity is our old friend the rectified-linear-unit, “keep positives, clamp negatives to zero” -- and the tuning that makes it selective is a learned *bias* per feature: a negative offset that holds each detector below zero until the evidence for its feature is strong, so the clamp silences everything the bias kept underwater, leaving only a sparse handful of features active. So the encoder’s output is a *sparse feature code*: a list of hundreds of thousands of slots, of which only a few dozen are non-zero on any given token, each non-zero entry saying “this feature is present, this strongly.”

Now the **decoder**, the mirror half. It takes that sparse feature code and *reconstructs the original activation* from it, by a single linear transformation back down to twelve thousand dimensions. And the structure of this transformation is where the *features themselves live*, so attend closely. The decoder works by *addition*: for each feature that is active in the code, it adds in that feature’s *direction*, scaled by how strongly the feature is present. Each *column* of the decoder is therefore one feature’s *direction in the residual stream* -- the actual vector that this feature contributes when it fires. These decoder columns *are* the dictionary atoms. They are the things we have been calling “the French direction,” “the Golden Gate Bridge direction” -- recovered, at last, as explicit vectors we can examine. The reconstruction is just the sum of the active features’ directions, each weighted by its activation: exactly the “sparse combination of a few atoms” that dictionary learning seeks.

So a *feature*, in this instrument, is a *pair*: an encoder detector that recognises when the feature is present, and a decoder direction that says what the feature contributes when it fires. The encoder reads the feature out; the decoder writes it back. And the whole apparatus -- encode into a wide sparse code, decode back to the original -- is trained to do one thing: make the reconstruction *match the original activation as closely as possible*, while keeping the code *sparse*. Which brings us to the objective, and to the first smudge on the lens.

## Part Three -- The Objective, and the Tension Inside It

The sparse autoencoder is trained to satisfy *two* demands at once, and the two demands *pull against each other*, and that tension is the source of nearly every distortion we shall catalogue. So let me state both demands and then show you the war between them.

The first demand is **fidelity**: the reconstruction must match the original activation. We measure how far off the reconstruction is -- the *reconstruction error* -- and we push to make it small. A perfect autoencoder would reproduce every activation exactly. This demand, alone, would be trivially satisfied by simply keeping *all* the features active and copying the input through -- so it must be opposed, or it yields nothing interpretable.

The second demand is **sparsity**: the feature code must use as *few* active features as possible. We add to the training objective a *penalty* on the number -- or, more precisely, on the total *magnitude* -- of active features, pushing the model to explain each activation with the fewest, faintest features it can. The standard form of this penalty is the *sum of the magnitudes* of all the feature activations -- the field calls it the *ell-one penalty* -- and minimising it pressures most features toward zero, leaving only the few that are truly needed. This demand, alone, would be trivially satisfied by keeping *no* features active at all -- a perfectly sparse code of all zeros, which reconstructs nothing. So it too must be opposed.

Now see the war. Fidelity wants *more* features active, more strongly, so as to reconstruct the activation faithfully. Sparsity wants *fewer* features active, more faintly, so as to keep the code clean. Every feature the autoencoder switches on improves the reconstruction a little and costs a little sparsity penalty; every feature it switches off saves penalty but worsens the reconstruction. The training settles at a *compromise* between the two -- and crucially, *there is no single correct compromise*. You can dial the sparsity penalty stronger, and get a sparser code that reconstructs less faithfully; or weaker, and get a denser code that reconstructs more faithfully. Every setting of the dial gives a different autoencoder, sitting at a different point on a trade-off curve -- the field borrows the economist’s term and calls it a *Pareto frontier* -- between fidelity and sparsity. There is a *whole family* of valid instruments, not one.

And here is the first hint of a problem that will deepen into the central difficulty of the episode: *the dictionary is not unique*. Different penalty settings give different dictionaries; and even at a *fixed* penalty setting, different training runs -- different random starts -- can give *different* dictionaries that reconstruct *equally well*. We are not reading off *the* features of the model as though there were a single fact of the matter. We are reading off *a* set of features that happens to reconstruct the activations sparsely -- and other sets would have done so too. Hold that. It is the smudge that does not wipe off.

## Part Four -- The Smudges on the Lens

Let me now show you the distortions directly -- the systematic ways the instrument misrepresents what it is looking at. There are four I want you to know, and I shall be candid about each, because each one has, in the field’s short history, misled someone.

The first smudge is **shrinkage**, and it is a *systematic bias* baked into the sparsity penalty itself. Recall that the penalty is on the *magnitude* of the feature activations -- the stronger a feature fires, the more penalty it incurs. But this means the autoencoder is punished not only for switching on *unnecessary* features but for letting *necessary* features fire at their *true strength*. To reduce the penalty, it *systematically under-reports* how strongly each feature is present -- it shrinks every activation toward zero, trading a little fidelity for a lot of penalty relief. So the strengths the instrument reports are *biased low, consistently*. When the autoencoder tells you a feature is firing at a certain strength, the true strength is *higher*; the instrument is built to underestimate. This is not random noise that averages out; it is a directional thumb on the scale, present in every reading. An instrument that always reads low is usable only if you know that it always reads low -- which is exactly why I am telling you.

The second smudge is **dead features**. During training, some features in our enormous dictionary *never find a job*. They are initialised, the training proceeds, and they simply never activate on any input -- or activate so rarely that they contribute nothing. They are *dead*: slots in the dictionary that hold no feature, detectors that detect nothing. In a large autoencoder a substantial fraction of the dictionary can die this way, which is wasteful -- you built ten million slots and a quarter of them are corpses -- and which complicates the bookkeeping, because the *nominal* size of your dictionary overstates the number of features you actually recovered. The field has developed tricks to *resurrect* dead features during training, nudging the idle ones toward useful work, but their existence is a standing reminder that the instrument’s structure and its *contents* are not the same thing.

The third smudge is the deep one, and it is called **feature splitting**, and it does something philosophically uncomfortable: it makes the *number of features* depend on the *size of your instrument*. Here is the phenomenon. Build a *small* autoencoder -- a modest dictionary -- and you will find a feature that fires on, say, *birds*: a single “bird” feature, clean enough, activating on text about birds of all kinds. Now build a *larger* autoencoder on the *same model* -- a bigger dictionary, more slots -- and that single bird feature *splits*: where you had one “bird” feature, you now have *several* -- a feature for *seabirds*, a feature for *birds of prey*, a feature for *songbirds*, each a finer-grained specialist. Build a larger instrument still, and these split *again* -- the seabird feature fracturing into gulls and albatrosses and the rest. The features are *not* a fixed list waiting to be found; they form a *hierarchy*, coarse-to-fine, and *which level of the hierarchy you resolve depends on how big a dictionary you trained*. A bigger microscope does not just find *more* features; it finds *finer* ones, by subdividing the coarse ones you already had.

Sit with what this does to our innocent question, “how many features does this model have?” The honest answer is that the question is *malformed* -- it is, to borrow a comparison I shall permit myself just this once, sir, rather like asking how many *colours* there are in a rainbow. You may say seven, if you wish to teach children; or you may resolve finer and find that between any two named colours there lies a continuum of intermediate shades, subdividing as finely as your instrument allows. The rainbow does not *have* a number of colours; it has a continuous structure that *you* carve at a resolution of your choosing. So too the model’s features: there is a hierarchical structure of meaning, and the sparse autoencoder carves it at whatever grain its size dictates. There is no privileged number, because there is no privileged grain. This is not a defect to be fixed; it is a *fact about the thing being measured* -- but it is a fact that any honest user of the instrument must hold, lest he report “the model has four million features” as though he had counted something real rather than chosen a resolution.

There is a fourth and subtler smudge I shall name only briefly, called **feature absorption**, in which one feature quietly *absorbs* part of another -- a specific feature learning to carry, on the cases it covers, the direction a more general feature owns, so that the general feature mysteriously *fails* to fire when it ought to, its job having been silently annexed. The canonical specimen: a feature for *begins with the letter S* that mysteriously declines to fire on the word “short” -- because a dedicated feature for that very word has absorbed the case, spelling duty and all, so the general feature’s silence is evidence not of absence but of annexation. It is a reminder that the features the instrument recovers are not always cleanly separated even when they look it; the dictionary can develop awkward internal overlaps that take real care to detect. I mention it so you know the catalogue of distortions is not closed at three.

## Part Five -- Polishing the Lens: The Modern Designs

Now the encouraging part of the honest reckoning: in the last two years the field has *polished out* several of these smudges with cleverer designs, and the common thread among the good ones is worth stating up front, because once you see it the whole recent literature organises itself. *The trouble with shrinkage comes from using one quantity -- the feature’s magnitude -- to do two different jobs at once: deciding* whether *a feature is on, and deciding* how strongly. *The ell-one penalty punishes magnitude, which is the right way to encourage* fewness *but the wrong way to set* strength. *So the cleverer designs all, in their various ways,* separate the decision of which features are active from the estimate of how strongly they are active. Decouple selection from magnitude, and shrinkage falls away. Three designs do this, and I shall give you each.

The first is the **gated** sparse autoencoder. Its idea is to split the encoder into two channels that share their detection work but answer different questions. One channel decides, for each feature, *whether it is on or off* -- a gate, a yes-or-no. The other channel decides, *for the features the gate has switched on*, *how strongly* they fire. And the crucial move is *where the sparsity penalty is applied*: it is applied *only to the gate* -- only to the on-or-off decision -- and *not* to the magnitude. So the instrument is pressured to keep *few features active* -- the gate is penalised for opening -- but is *not* pressured to shrink the strengths of the features it does activate, because the magnitude channel bears no penalty. Shrinkage, which was entirely an artefact of penalising magnitude, *disappears*. The gated design was a marked step forward for exactly this reason: it removed a known, systematic bias by refusing to make one quantity do two jobs.

The second design is the **top-kay** sparse autoencoder, and it is the most direct of the three: it dispenses with the magnitude penalty *altogether* and instead *simply keeps the k most strongly active features and zeroes the rest* -- where *k* is a number you choose, say thirty-two or sixty-four. There is no penalty to shrink the magnitudes, so *no shrinkage*; and sparsity is controlled *exactly* -- you get precisely *k* active features, not “however many the penalty happened to leave,” so the fidelity-sparsity trade-off is pinned down cleanly rather than dialled in by trial and error. The cost, and it is a real one, is that you must *choose k in advance*, and the right number of active features genuinely varies from token to token -- some activations are simple and need few features, some are rich and need many -- so a fixed *k* is a compromise of its own. But for the price of choosing one number, you buy away the shrinkage bias and the fiddly penalty-tuning at once. It has become, for many purposes, the workhorse.

The third design is the **jump-threshold** autoencoder -- the name in the literature is the *jump rectified-linear-unit*, but I shall spare the synthesiser and call it the jump-threshold. Its idea is a *learned threshold* per feature: each feature has a cutoff, learned during training, below which it is held at *zero* -- switched off entirely -- and above which it fires at *its full, true value*, with no shrinkage. The “jump” is the discontinuity at the threshold: the feature leaps from nothing to its genuine strength as the input crosses the cutoff, rather than ramping up gradually from zero the way the plain rectified-linear-unit does. This, like the others, separates the on-or-off decision (governed by the threshold) from the strength (reported faithfully above it), and it achieves a fidelity-sparsity trade-off near the best known. Of the three, it is in some respects the most refined, threading the needle between the gated design’s two-channel machinery and the top-kay design’s fixed budget.

I give you all three not to burden you with a taxonomy but because they share that single illuminating principle -- *separate selection from magnitude* -- and because knowing them lets you read the field’s recent output without bewilderment. When you see a paper touting a new autoencoder variant, ask first: *how does it decide which features are on, and how does it estimate their strength, and has it kept those two jobs apart?* That question cuts to the heart of nearly every design. And one further polish, arrived after these three and aimed at a different smudge altogether: the **matryoshka** design trains dictionaries *nested* inside one another, coarse within fine like the dolls, so a single instrument carries several grains of the feature hierarchy at once -- and the splitting of the previous part becomes a structure one *chooses a level of*, rather than an artefact one suffers at whatever grain the dictionary size happened to dictate.

## Part Six -- Evaluation, and the Problem That Has No Ground Truth

We have built the instrument and polished its lens. Now the question that ought to make any careful person uneasy: *how do we know it is showing us anything real?* How do we *evaluate* a sparse autoencoder -- judge whether its features are the model’s true features or fictions the instrument has conjured? The field has three families of measure, and then beneath them a problem that none of the three can reach, and I must lay out both honestly.

The first measure is **reconstruction fidelity**, but assessed in a clever and consequential way rather than by raw error. The trick is to *put the reconstruction back into the model* and see whether the model still works. Recall that the autoencoder takes a real activation and produces a reconstruction of it. So: run the model normally up to some layer, *replace* the real activation with the autoencoder’s reconstruction, let the model continue, and measure *how much worse the model predicts* with the reconstruction substituted for the real thing. If the autoencoder has captured what matters, the model should run *almost as well* on the reconstruction as on the original -- the degradation in its loss should be small. The field calls the complement of this degradation the *loss recovered* -- the fraction of the model’s competence that survives the substitution. It is a far more meaningful measure than reconstruction error, because it asks not “does the reconstruction look like the original?” but “does the reconstruction *do the same job* in the model?” -- which is what we actually care about.

The second measure is **sparsity** itself -- how few features are active per token on average -- because an instrument that achieves good fidelity only by switching on hundreds of features per token has not really *disentangled* anything; it has merely re-described the activation in a different dense basis. We want fidelity *and* genuine sparsity together, which returns us to the Pareto frontier: a good autoencoder pushes that frontier outward, achieving more fidelity at a given sparsity than its rivals.

The third measure is **interpretability** -- do the features actually *mean* something a human can articulate? -- and here the field has built an ingenious, if slightly vertiginous, machinery: it uses *language models to interpret the features of language models*. For a given feature, you collect the inputs that make it fire most strongly, hand them to a capable model, and ask it to *propose an explanation* -- “this feature fires on mentions of legal contracts,” say. Then, to *check* the explanation, you give it to *another* model and ask it to *predict*, from the explanation alone, when the feature should fire -- and you score how well those predictions match the feature’s actual firing. A feature whose explanation lets you predict its behaviour is genuinely interpretable; one whose proposed explanation fails to predict it is not. This automated explain-and-score loop lets the field assess the interpretability of *millions* of features without a human reading each one -- and it is a preview of the *automated interpretability* theme that will close our series.

And now, beneath all three measures, the problem that none of them solves, and that I have been promising since the outset. *We have no ground truth.* There exists no independent, authoritative list of the model’s *true* features against which we could check the autoencoder’s dictionary and pronounce it correct or incorrect. Every measure I have just described is a *proxy*. Reconstruction fidelity tells you the autoencoder *captures* the activation, not that it has carved it at the model’s *true* joints. Interpretability tells you the features are *human-articulable*, not that they are the *model’s* features -- a feature can be perfectly interpretable to us and yet *not* be a unit the model itself computes with. And recall the non-uniqueness from Part Three: *two different dictionaries can both reconstruct the activations well, both yield interpretable features, and yet disagree* about what the features *are*. Without ground truth we have no way to declare one of them right and the other wrong. We are in the position of an astronomer who has built a fine telescope and sees *structure* in the sky -- but cannot step outside the cosmos to verify that the structure is *there* and not an artefact of the glass. Every check we can run is a check *for consistency*, never a check *against truth*, because the truth is precisely what we lack independent access to. This is the deepest limitation of the instrument, and no cleverer design removes it; it is a limitation not of our engineering but of our *epistemic situation*. I shall return to it, gravely, in the seventh episode -- the course proper’s finale -- because it is the same gap that makes the safety question hard.

## Part Seven -- What the Instrument Has Achieved

I have shown you the smudges at length, because that is the valet’s duty. Let me now, in fairness, show you what this imperfect instrument has *accomplished*, because it is genuinely remarkable and the honest reckoning has both columns.

When the sparse autoencoder was scaled up -- trained on the activations of a *production-grade* frontier model, with a dictionary of millions of features and a great deal of computation -- it worked, and it worked at a scale and quality that surprised even its proponents. The landmark demonstration trained dictionaries on a deployed mid-sized frontier model and pushed the largest to some *thirty-four million* features; and it recovered features that are, by the interpretability measures, genuinely meaningful -- and, strikingly, *abstract* and *general* in a way that earlier neuron-level work never achieved. Features that fire on a concept *regardless of the language it is expressed in* -- the same feature for a concept whether the text is English or Russian or Chinese -- *multilingual* features, capturing meaning above the level of any particular tongue. Features that respond to a concept whether it arrives as *text or as an image* -- *multimodal* features, in models that handle both. Features for concrete entities, for abstract relations, for tones and registers and rhetorical moves. The instrument, for all its distortions, was clearly resolving *real and abstract structure* in the model’s representations.

I must, even here in the favourable column, enter one figure that the triumphant write-ups tended to set in smaller type. Even those large dictionaries did *not* account for the whole of what the model was doing: a substantial fraction of the activation -- by some reckonings well upward of a fifth, and more at realistic sparsities -- was left unexplained, captured by no feature, consigned to a residual the field has taken to calling, with a candour I admire, the **dark matter** of the model. The microscope resolved a great deal of real structure; it also left a wide grey margin in which structure may lurk that these instruments, as built, simply do not see. Hold the thirty-four million in one hand and the dark matter in the other; both are true, and an honest reckoning carries both.

And -- this is the part that matters most for where we are going -- among the millions of features were ones with *direct bearing on safety*. Features that fire on *deception* and on the model representing something it is being untruthful about. Features for *sycophancy* -- for flattering the user against the evidence. Features for various *biases*, for *dangerous* technical content, for the model *withholding* information, for *secrecy*. The instrument was surfacing, as explicit and examinable directions, exactly the kinds of internal states we should most want to monitor in a system we intend to trust. That is a striking thing -- that the abstract apparatus we have spent five episodes building should deliver, as ordinary output, *handles on the very properties that safety turns upon*.

And these handles are not merely *observational* -- they are *causal levers*, which returns us to the most famous demonstration of all, now properly contextualised. You will recall, from the first episode, the *Golden Gate Bridge* feature -- the one which, when *clamped* to a high value, caused the model to steer every conversation toward that bridge, to identify *as* the bridge, to bring it up unbidden. We can now say exactly what that was: the sparse autoencoder recovered a feature whose decoder direction is “Golden Gate Bridge,” and the experimenters *forced that feature’s activation high* and let it write its direction into the residual stream on every token. The model, reading its own notebook, found “Golden Gate Bridge” insistently present and behaved accordingly. The point of the demonstration was never the bridge; it was the *proof of causality* -- that the features the instrument recovers are not passive correlates but *active components* the model *uses*, such that *intervening* on them *changes behaviour*. And if you can do that with the bridge, you can do it with the *safety-relevant* features -- pull the “deception” feature and watch the model’s truthfulness shift, pull the “sycophancy” feature and watch its flattery change. The clamp is a *control*, and the safety features are *what it controls*. That is the doorway to the seventh episode, and we shall walk through it there.

## Part Eight -- The Reckoning: Does the Microscope Beat a Ruler?

I have now shown you both columns as the instrument’s proponents would draw them: grievous smudges, honestly disclosed, and against them a genuine and remarkable haul of abstract, causal, safety-relevant features. For a year or two that was where the matter rested, and the mood in the field was buoyant. Then a colder season arrived, and I would be derelict -- the word, sir, is not too strong -- to deliver you a commissioning that stopped before it.

The cold season took the form of a simple, unsentimental question, of exactly the kind that periodically rescues a field from its own enthusiasm. It is this: *for the jobs we actually want the instrument to do, does it beat a cheaper tool?* An instrument may be beautiful, principled, possessed of a distinguished neuroscientific pedigree, and still be the wrong thing to reach for if a ruler costing tuppence does the job as well. So the sceptics -- and I use the word with respect, for scepticism is the immune system of a science -- set up the contest properly, with named tasks and honest baselines, and let the autoencoder run against the ruler.

Consider first the task of **probing**: detecting whether some specific concept -- a known property, say *this text is about medicine*, or *this code has a security flaw* -- is present in the model’s activations. The autoencoder offers an obvious approach: find the feature for the concept, watch whether it fires. But there is a cheaper tool, and we met it as far back as the Othello board in our first episode: a plain *linear probe*, a single direction fitted by ordinary supervised learning directly to the activations, no dictionary required. A careful study put the two head to head across a broad battery of concepts -- and the verdict, delivered without malice but without mercy either, was that the autoencoder features, for this purpose, *failed to beat the plain probe*. Across the battery the cheap ruler matched or bested the elaborate microscope; even the circumstances that at first looked like the autoencoder’s redoubt -- very little labelled data, or labels of a corrupt and unreliable sort -- dissolved, once the baselines were carefully tuned, into no advantage at all. The elaborate apparatus bought nothing the tuppenny tool did not already supply.

Consider next **steering** -- the control application, the one descended from the Golden Gate clamp, of nudging a model’s behaviour by intervening on a concept. A steering benchmark was assembled, the autoencoder set against simple alternatives -- among them a humble *difference-of-means* vector, computed by subtracting the average activation on texts lacking a trait from the average on texts bearing it, a recipe a competent undergraduate could implement before lunch -- and against small fine-tuned adapters. And again the celebrated instrument did not, in that first benchmarking, distinguish itself: the simple steering vectors held their own and frequently won. The clamp that turned a model into the Golden Gate Bridge was a real and causal demonstration, you understand -- I withdraw none of it -- but *real and causal* is a lower bar than *better than the obvious cheap alternative*, and on the second bar the early results were humbling.

And there was a third blow, the most philosophically pointed of the three, and the one I most want you to carry. Recall the deepest smudge from our evaluation -- that we have *no ground truth*, only proxies, chief among them *interpretability*, the test of whether a feature’s firing can be predicted from the explanation a model has proposed for it. Researchers asked an impertinent question of that proxy: they trained sparse autoencoders not on a real, trained model but on a *randomly initialised* one -- a model with the architecture but with its weights left as the dice fell, a network that has *learned nothing*, that means nothing, that is so much numerical noise wearing a transformer’s coat. By rights such a model has no features to find. And yet the autoencoder, pointed at this stuffed shirt, dutifully produced a dictionary of *interpretable-looking* features, passing the autointerpretability check at a rate not nearly so far below the real model’s as one’s peace of mind would require. Sit with that, sir. Our principal proxy for *real* will certify the features of a model that is *demonstrably empty*. It follows, with uncomfortable rigour, that passing the interpretability check is *not* strong evidence a feature is a genuine unit of the model’s computation -- the check measures the ingenuity of the explainer and the human appetite for pattern fully as much as it measures anything in the model. The astronomer’s telescope, we now learn, shows constellations even when pointed at a blank wall, because the constellation was always partly in the eye.

Now -- and this is why I call it a reckoning rather than a refutation -- the story did not end in the autoencoder’s disgrace, and a fair valet reports the recovery as scrupulously as the fall. As the dust settled through the latest work, a more discriminating picture emerged, and it turns on a distinction worth stating crisply, because it is the most useful single thing to take from the whole controversy. *The autoencoder is the wrong tool for acting on concepts you already know, and may be the right tool for discovering concepts you do not.* When you have a concept in hand -- a label, a trait, a behaviour you can name and collect examples of -- you can fit a probe or a steering vector to *that concept directly*, supervised and sharp, and the unsupervised dictionary, which spent its effort learning thousands of concepts you did not ask about, will rarely beat the bespoke tool aimed at the one you did. But the autoencoder’s distinctive trick -- the thing no probe and no difference-of-means can do -- is to enumerate the model’s concepts *without being told what to look for*, and so to surface the ones you would never have thought to probe for: the unknown unknowns, the surprising internal category, the feature for a kind of deception nobody had named. Later work duly showed that with more careful feature selection and labelling the instrument *can* be brought up to the level of the strong supervised baselines on the very steering tasks where it first stumbled -- so the autoencoder’s earlier failures were partly failures of *how it was used* rather than of the thing itself. The reckoning’s most humbling line item stands regardless: the method that beat every instrument on those benches, dictionaries and bespoke vectors alike, was *prompting*, which is to say asking the model nicely. The honest summary, as the field stands: the autoencoder was oversold as a universal instrument, has been repriced as a *specialised* one, and its proper office is *discovery* -- reconnaissance of an unfamiliar model’s conceptual territory -- after which cheaper, sharper tools are often the right thing to deploy on whatever the reconnaissance turned up. The repricing was institutional as well as intellectual, I should add: by the middle of twenty twenty-five the major laboratories’ interpretability teams had publicly shifted their weight from the scaling of dictionaries toward probes, model biology, and the computation-reading instruments of the supplement -- the crown moving houses exactly as an honest observer of the benchmarks would have predicted.

## Part Nine -- The Engineer’s Dividend

What should you, an engineer, take from the reckoning? Three things. First, a general and bracing principle: *in interpretability, always demand the baseline.* When a tool, however elegant, is proposed for a task, the question is never “does it work?” but “does it beat the cheap thing?”, and a field that forgets to ask it will spend years admiring instruments that a ruler outperforms. Second, the specific operational rule: reach for the autoencoder to *map* a model you do not understand, and for probes and steering vectors to *act* on properties you have already named -- right tool, right office. And third, the reckoning is itself the strongest possible advertisement for the discipline of our *next* episode. Every failure above was, at bottom, a failure to distinguish *a feature that correlates with something* from *a feature the model actually uses to do something* -- and the only cure for that confusion is to stop observing and start *intervening*: to reach into the running model, change the component, and watch the consequence. The autoencoder, for all its smudges, only ever gives us *correlation at scale*. Causation must be earned by experiment. Which is precisely where we go next.

## Part Ten -- Two Hand-Offs: The Instruments Built on This Pattern

Two developments before we close, and I shall give each precisely the space a hand-off deserves, for the supplement owns them both in full. The first: everything this episode built reads the notebook’s *contents* -- what concepts sit in the stream -- and not the *computation* the clerks perform upon them, which Part Seven of the last episode warned us is sometimes the whole story. The instrument that aims at the computation is the **transcoder** -- the autoencoder’s pattern, retargeted: trained not to reproduce an activation but to imitate, in sparse legible features, what a feed-forward clerk *does* to it. Assemble transcoders in place of clerks and you have a *replacement model* whose wires can be traced; trace them for a particular prompt and you have an *attribution graph* -- a wiring diagram of a thought. The eighth episode builds the transcoder properly, and the ninth draws and validates the diagrams; I mark the instruments now so that you will recognise their pedigree when they arrive.

The second is nearer your own bench: the same pattern stretched *across two models* is the **crosscoder**, one shared dictionary spanning a base model and its fine-tune, showing which features the tuning introduced, amplified, or suppressed -- the diff of a fine-tune, made visible in the model’s own vocabulary. For an engineer who must diagnose why a fine-tune regressed here and improved there, it is the most direct point of contact between this science and your daily work, and the eighth episode gives it the full hour it deserves, sharp edges and all.

## Closing -- What We Have Earned, and Where It Points

Let us gather the episode, which has been a long one, as the instrument deserved.

The **sparse autoencoder** is *dictionary learning* applied to a model’s activations -- sparse coding’s old trick, which once rediscovered the visual cortex’s edge detectors, now aimed at the notebook. Encoder up into a *wide, sparse* code; decoder back down by adding the active features’ *directions* -- and those decoder columns are the features we have sought for five episodes, recovered as explicit vectors. Trained on **fidelity** against **sparsity**, the instrument is a *family* along a trade-off frontier with *no unique dictionary*, and its smudges are systematic: **shrinkage** (repaired by the gated, top-kay, and jump-threshold designs, united by *separate selection from magnitude*), **dead features**, **feature splitting** (the rainbow’s colours -- a resolution chosen, not a number counted, and the matryoshka design makes the choosing explicit), and **feature absorption**. Evaluation runs on **loss recovered**, sparsity, and the automated interpretability loop -- all proxies, for *we have no ground truth*, and every check is for consistency, never against truth.

What the instrument achieved is real -- tens of millions of abstract, multilingual, multimodal features from a frontier model, safety-relevant handles among them, causal levers as the Golden Gate clamp proved, with a wide margin of **dark matter** unexplained. And the *reckoning* repriced it honestly: beaten by plain probes at detecting named concepts and by tuppenny vectors (and by *prompting*, above all) at steering; its interpretability proxy certifying features in a randomly initialised model; recovered, in the settling, as the *discovery* instrument -- reconnaissance, after which sharper tools act. Engrave the lesson: *always demand the baseline.* And mark the two hand-offs -- the **transcoder** and its attribution graphs, the **crosscoder** and its fine-tune diffs -- for the supplement builds both.

But I must close on the warning that has run beneath the whole episode, because it is the hinge into the next: we now have a *dictionary of features* -- we can *see* them, *name* them, even *clamp* them -- and yet a feature *correlated* with a behaviour is not yet *proven* to *cause* it; the rooster’s crow accompanies the dawn without raising the sun. To move from *the features are present* to *the features do the work*, we must *intervene* -- perform experiments rather than observations, which is the one thing the autoencoder cannot do for us.

That discipline of intervention -- activation patching, path patching, the careful causal interrogation of a model that, unlike any brain, we can *pause and alter at will* -- is the whole subject of our next episode. We shall trace a complete circuit, found by intervention, from first wire to last. And we shall meet an inconvenient discovery that humbles the whole enterprise: that when you disable a component you have proven “critical,” the model often *barely falters* -- because hidden backups silently take over its work. The microscope showed us the parts. The next episode asks whether we have truly understood the *machine*. I shall have it ready.

Until then, sir.
