# Circuits and the Discipline of Intervention

*A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability — Episode Six*

## A Confession, and an Acquisition

Good day, sir.

I must begin with a confession, and it concerns the manner of everything I have told you so far. Across five episodes I have been, in a particular sense, a *storyteller*. I have shown you a feature firing and said “this is the French feature.” I have shown you two heads and said “this one writes the previous token, this one copies it forward.” I have narrated the model’s inner life with considerable confidence — *this part does this, that part does that* — and you have, I hope, found the narration illuminating. But I have been trading, throughout, on a kind of license. Almost everything I have shown you has been *observational*: I looked at what the model does, noticed a part that *lights up* when some behaviour occurs, and *told you a story* in which that part causes the behaviour. And the uncomfortable truth, which I left you with at the close of the last episode and now make the whole subject of this one, is that *a part lighting up during a behaviour does not prove that the part causes the behaviour*.

I used a country proverb to mark the gap: the rooster crows at dawn, faithfully, every morning — and crowing causes nothing; the sun would rise in silence. A component may *accompany* a behaviour as reliably as the rooster accompanies the dawn and yet be a bystander, or a consequence, or a correlate of the true cause rather than the cause itself. Observation cannot tell these apart. To know that a part *does the work* — to establish *mechanism* rather than *correlation* — there is exactly one reliable instrument, and it is the instrument of every mature experimental science: you must *intervene*. You must reach in, *change* the part, hold everything else fixed, and *watch what happens to the outcome*. If changing the part changes the behaviour, the part is in the causal chain. If changing it changes nothing, the part was a rooster.

And here, sir, we possess an advantage so enormous that it is easy to overlook, and I want to dwell on it for a moment because it is the thing that makes this entire science *possible* in a way that neuroscience is not. The neuroscientist who wishes to know whether some cluster of neurons causes some behaviour faces a brutal practical wall: brains are not easily paused, not easily read out in full, not easily altered one piece at a time and run again from the identical starting state. But a language model is *open to us completely*. We can run it forward and *freeze it* at any point. We can *read every number* in every layer, exactly, without disturbance. We can *change* any activation to any value we like, *re-run from that point*, and observe the consequence with perfect precision and perfect repeatability — and then *undo* the change and try another. We can run the very same input a thousand times with a thousand different surgical alterations and compare. This is a god’s-eye laboratory, a degree of experimental control no student of biological cognition has ever remotely enjoyed. The whole discipline of this episode is the art of *using* that control well — of designing clean interventions that isolate cause from correlation. We shall acquire that art, apply it to trace one complete circuit from first wire to last, and then confront the discovery that, even with this god’s-eye control, the machine resists clean understanding in a way I find genuinely humbling.

Let us acquire the basic instrument first.

## Part One — Why Observation Is Not Enough

Let me make the inadequacy of observation concrete, because it is tempting to think the gap is merely theoretical and that in practice a part that lights up surely *is* the cause.

Consider that the residual stream, our shared notebook, accumulates contributions from *every* component, and that these contributions flow downstream and influence one another in tangled ways. Suppose you observe that some head is highly active whenever the model performs a certain task. There are several quite different explanations, and observation cannot distinguish them. Perhaps the head *performs* the task — the causal story. Or perhaps the head is *responding to* the same input feature that triggers the task, doing something unrelated, lighting up *alongside* the real machinery rather than constituting it. Or perhaps the head’s activity is a *downstream consequence* of the task having already been performed elsewhere — an echo, not an engine. Or perhaps the head is part of the machinery but a *minor* part, while the real work is done by components that, for whatever reason, do not light up so visibly. Each of these produces the same observation — *the head is active during the task* — and they have entirely different implications for understanding the model.

Only intervention separates them. *Change* the head’s output — set it to something it would not normally be — *while keeping the input fixed*, and see whether the task still gets performed. If the task collapses, the head was doing real work. If the task proceeds untouched, the head was a bystander, whatever its enthusiasm. There is no substitute for this. You cannot reason your way from correlation to cause; you can only *test*. And so the central methodological commitment of serious circuit analysis is to treat every observational story — every “this feature is the X feature,” every “this head does Y” — as a *hypothesis to be tested by intervention*, never as a finding in itself. The microscope of the last episode *generates* hypotheses. The interventions of this episode *test* them. A science needs both, and the second is the one that earns the word *causal*.

## Part Two — Activation Patching: The Fundamental Move

The fundamental interventional technique has several names in the literature — *activation patching*, *causal tracing*, *interchange intervention* — and I shall call it activation patching. The idea is beautiful in its simplicity, and once you have it, a great deal of the field’s method is just variations upon it.

Here is the setup. You take *two* inputs that differ in some targeted way. Call the first the *clean* input — one on which the model does the behaviour you are studying — and the second the *corrupted* input — one that is *almost identical* but altered just enough that the model *does not* do the behaviour, or does a different one. The art is in the *almost*: the two inputs should differ *only* in the specific respect whose machinery you wish to isolate, so that any difference in the model’s processing can be attributed to *that* respect and nothing else. To make it concrete with the example we shall trace in full shortly: the *clean* input might be “When Mary and John went to the shop, John gave the bag to,” on which the model correctly favours Mary; the *corrupted* input swaps a single name — “When Mary and John went to the shop, *Mary* gave the bag to” — which flips the correct answer to John. One token changed; everything else — length, grammar, vocabulary, sentence shape — held identical, so that whatever in the model’s innards now differs must concern *precisely* the computation of which name is the indirect object, and nothing incidental. That is the art of the *almost*, and a sloppy pair — one that changed two things at once — would smear the very effect one is trying to isolate.

Now you run the model on *both* inputs and *record every internal activation* in each — you cache the complete inner state of the clean run and the complete inner state of the corrupted run. And here is the move: you run the model *again*, and at *one chosen component* — one head, one layer, one position — you *replace* the activation from one run with the activation from the other, leaving everything else as it was, and you let the model finish. Then you *measure how much the output moved*. You have, in effect, *transplanted a single organ* from one body into another and asked whether the recipient now behaves like the donor. If transplanting that one component *carries the behaviour across* — if patching the clean activation into the corrupted run *restores* the clean behaviour — then that component *carries the information* responsible for the behaviour. If transplanting it does nothing, that component is not where the relevant information lives. Component by component, you map *where in the model the behaviour-determining information resides*.

There are two directions to run this, and they answer subtly different questions, so the field names and uses both. The first direction is *noising*: you start from the *clean* run and patch in a *corrupted* activation — you *break* the working model with one transplanted component and ask *what breaks it*. If patching corruption into a single component *destroys* the clean behaviour, that component was *necessary* — its clean value was load-bearing. The second direction is *denoising*: you start from the *corrupted* run and patch in a *clean* activation — you *repair* the broken model with one transplanted component and ask *what repairs it*. If patching cleanliness into a single component *restores* the behaviour, that component was *sufficient* to carry the relevant information into the broken context. Necessity and sufficiency — what breaks it, what fixes it — and a careful circuit analysis runs *both*, because they can disagree in instructive ways, and a component that looks necessary by one and not sufficient by the other is telling you something about how the work is distributed. The discipline is to use both lenses and attend to where they diverge.

## Part Three — Path Patching and Direct Attribution: From Set to Wiring

Activation patching, as I have described it, finds *which components* matter — it gives you a *set* of important parts. But a circuit is not a *set* of parts; it is a *wiring diagram* — a set of parts *connected in a particular way*, this head feeding that head, this one’s output becoming that one’s input. To recover the *wiring* and not merely the *parts*, we need a refinement, and it is called **path patching**.

The idea sharpens activation patching by being precise about *what flows where*. Recall from the second episode that a later component reads the residual stream *as written by all the earlier components* — so any earlier component can influence a later one *directly*, by writing something the later one reads, *and* indirectly, through chains of intermediaries. Plain activation patching, transplanting a component wholesale, mixes all these influences together. Path patching instead patches *only the contribution that flows along one specific path* — from component A to component B in particular — *while holding all the other paths at their normal values*. You ask, precisely: *does A influence the output by way of B, specifically?* — and by doing this for many pairs you recover not just *which* components matter but *how they are connected* — which feeds which, in what order, along what route. You graduate from a *list of suspects* to a *diagram of the conspiracy*. This is the technique that lets the field draw those wiring diagrams of named heads with arrows between them, and it is what we shall use in the showpiece shortly.

There is a companion technique worth your knowing, cheaper than patching and exploiting a fact we established early. Recall that the residual stream is *additive* — every component *adds* its contribution — and that the model’s final prediction is read off the stream by an essentially *linear* operation. It follows that the final prediction can be *decomposed into a sum of contributions, one per component* — you can ask, of each head and each layer, *how much did its contribution push the final answer toward the observed prediction?* This is **direct logit attribution**: you read the final prediction apart into per-component shares, *directly*, by exploiting the additive linear structure, with no patching required. It is fast and clean — but I must flag its one essential limitation, because it is a trap for the unwary. Direct logit attribution captures only the *direct* effect of each component on the output — the part that the component writes *straight* into the final prediction. It *misses* the *indirect* effects — the influence a component has by *changing what a later component then does*, which is most of the interesting computation. A head that does its work by *feeding another head* will look *unimportant* to direct logit attribution, because its effect is laundered through the intermediary. So direct logit attribution is an excellent *first pass* — cheap, immediate, good for finding the components that write *directly* to the answer, such as the final copying heads — but it must be *complemented* by patching to find the upstream machinery whose effects are indirect. Use it to find the last wires; use patching to trace back from them.

## Part Four — The Subtle Art of Ablation

Before the showpiece, one matter that sounds like a technicality and is in fact a place where careful people have drawn wrong conclusions: *how, exactly, do you “turn off” a component* when you want to test whether it is necessary? The act of *ablation* — disabling a part — seems straightforward, but the *method* of disabling matters enormously, and the naive method is actively misleading.

The naive method is *zero-ablation*: set the component’s output to *zero*, as though it had been deleted. It is intuitive — “remove the part, see if the machine still works” — but it has a serious flaw. Setting an activation to *zero* typically produces an internal state *the model has never encountered in its life* — a state *off the distribution* of anything that arises in normal operation. The model’s downstream components, fed this alien input, may behave *erratically* for reasons that have nothing to do with the function of the ablated part — they are simply being asked to process gibberish. So the damage you observe from zero-ablation may *overstate* the component’s importance, because some of the damage is just the shock of an impossible input rather than the loss of the part’s true contribution.

The better methods keep the model *on-distribution*. *Mean-ablation* replaces the component’s output with its *average value across a dataset* — a bland, typical value the model *has* seen, stripping out the component’s *input-specific* contribution while leaving it in a state the model finds ordinary. *Resample-ablation* replaces the output with its value *from a different input* — a real activation, just from the wrong context — which likewise keeps things on-distribution while removing the *correct* information. These give cleaner readings of a component’s *actual* functional contribution, because they ask “what is lost when this part stops doing *its specific job*?” rather than “what happens when this part emits nonsense?” The choice among zero-, mean-, and resample-ablation can *change which components look important*, and a careful analysis states which it used and why. I raise it not to drown you in method but because it is exactly the sort of detail that separates a *rigorous* causal claim from a *plausible-sounding* one — and because the showpiece we now turn to was built on patching done with this kind of care.

## Part Five — The Showpiece: How a Model Finds the Indirect Object

Now let me reward the method with a result — the most thoroughly traced circuit in the field’s short history, worked out in exhaustive detail in a landmark twenty twenty-two study with the agreeably candid title *Interpretability in the Wild*, and a genuine triumph of the interventional approach. It remains, some years on, the reference example of what a complete causal dissection looks like, and I shall give it to you not in the gestural form one usually meets but with its actual dimensions, because the dimensions are the point: the circuit comprises *twenty-six attention heads, grouped into seven classes*, identified within a small model — GPT-2 small, the field’s laboratory fruit fly, twelve layers of twelve heads, a hundred and forty-four in all — so that you may, and should, reproduce every step of what follows on your own bench. Rather under a fifth of the machine, that is, conscripted into one small grammatical task and caught in the act. The task is itself small, and I shall use the study’s own kind of example.

Consider the sentence: *“When Mary and John went to the shop, John gave the bag to — whom?”* You, sir, complete it without effort: *Mary*. The blank wants the name that is *not* the one already doing the giving — the *indirect object*, the recipient. Both Mary and John were named; John has just been named *again* as the giver; and so the answer is the *other* name, Mary. The field calls this task **indirect object identification** — identifying the recipient — and the question is: by what internal algorithm does a model arrive at *Mary*? Observation alone would never settle it. The study settled it by patching, component by component, and recovered a multi-stage algorithm of remarkable specificity. Let me walk the stages, because each was *found and verified by intervention*, and because the cast of characters is instructive.

A word first on how one *measures* the answer, because the whole investigation depends on having a number to watch, and the choice of number is itself a small lesson in method. One does not, as a naive approach might, simply ask whether the model’s single most likely next token is “Mary.” That throws away almost everything. Instead one watches the **logit difference**: the gap between the score the model assigns to the correct name, Mary, and the score it assigns to the incorrect one, John — a graded quantity that registers not merely *whether* the model is right but *how strongly* it favours the right answer over the tempting wrong one. In the model studied, that gap sat at about three and a half — which, the scores being logarithms, means the model rated Mary *thirty-odd times* likelier than John. A single graded number, sensitive in exactly the place the task lives: it moves when the circuit’s discrimination strengthens or weakens, and it is what every patch in what follows was scored against. Choosing the right thing to measure is half the experimental art, sir; a blunt metric would have rendered the finer machinery invisible.

The algorithm has, in essence, three jobs to do: *notice that one name is repeated*, *decide that the repeated name is therefore not the answer*, and *copy the other name to the output*. And the study found a distinct family of heads for each job, connected in exactly that order.

The first family are the **duplicate-token heads**, and you have met their species before — they were a specimen in the second episode’s field guide, the natural-born detectives. Their job here is to *notice the repetition* — to detect that the name “John,” appearing the second time, has *appeared before* in the sentence. They sit at the second “John,” attend back to the first, and signal: *this name is a duplicate, and here is where its twin lives*. They are abetted by a second route to the same fact: a **previous-token head** writes, at the position just after the first “John,” a note recording its predecessor, and an **induction head** — the very circuit of our third episode, prefix-match and all — then performs the “have I seen this before” matching by the two-layer mechanism we traced to the wire. Two of the seven classes, that is, are simply the induction machinery from earlier in the course, reporting for duty in a new circuit — a gratifying instance of the *universality* this series keeps promising: the parts are reused. Either way, this is the *detection* stage: the circuit establishes that one of the two names is repeated and which one it is.

The second family are the **subject-inhibition heads** — the study calls them *S-inhibition heads*, the S standing for the repeated subject, and I shall call them inhibition heads. They *take* the duplicate signal and do something clever with it, and the precise form of the cleverness is worth having exactly, because it is subtler than “suppress John” and it exposes a mechanism the field did not expect. They sit at the final position, attend back to that second “John,” and write a note that does not act on the prediction at all — instead it acts on the *next* family of heads, biasing *their* query so that, when they go looking among the names, they are steered *away* from the repeated subject. The inhibition heads do not themselves choose or forbid any answer; they *adjust the question the copying heads will ask*. This is query composition, the flavour from our second episode, deployed for inhibition.

And here the study turned up a genuinely delightful detail, one I flagged would come: the note the inhibition heads pass is not the *content* of the name John, nor even merely its identity, but in part a *pointer* — information about the *position* the unwanted name occupies, a coordinate rather than a copy. The heads communicate by sharing the *location* of a piece of information instead of the information itself — passing a reference, as a programmer would say, rather than a value. That a model should spontaneously evolve pointer-passing, that most basic device of human-written software, with no one having designed it in, is one of those findings that repays the whole laborious method in a single stroke. This is the *decision* stage, and notice its logical shape: the circuit does not directly find the right answer; it *eliminates* the wrong one, leaving the right one by exclusion. A pleasingly economical bit of reasoning, and one no one would have guessed from the outside.

The third family are the **name-mover heads**, and they do the *copying* — the output work. A name-mover head attends to the names in the sentence and *copies the attended name to the output*, boosting its probability as the next token. But — and here is where the stages connect — the inhibition heads have already written their note saying *not John*. So when the name-mover heads decide which name to attend to and copy, they are *steered away from John* by the inhibition signal, and they attend to *Mary* instead. They copy Mary. The blank is filled. The detection stage found the duplicate; the decision stage inhibited it; the copying stage, thus steered, moved the *other* name to the output. Three stages, three families of heads, wired in sequence, *each verified by patching* — patch the duplicate-token heads and the downstream inhibition collapses; patch the inhibition heads and the name-movers cease to discriminate; patch the name-movers and the answer is not copied. The wiring was not narrated from a hopeful glance at what lit up; it was *established by intervention at every junction*.

And there are two further families, each of which connects this circuit back to themes we have already met, which is why I have saved this showpiece for here rather than introducing it earlier. The fourth family are the **negative name-mover heads** — and they do the *opposite* of the name-movers: they attend to a name and *push its probability down*, *reducing* the likelihood of the answer they attend to. At first this seems perverse — why would the circuit contain heads working *against* the very answer it is computing? The interpretation, and it is a lovely piece of reverse-engineering in its own right, is that they perform a kind of *calibration* or *hedging*. Consider the model’s predicament: the name-mover heads copy whatever name they attend to, and they are confident creatures. But sentences are treacherous — sometimes the structure shifts, a pronoun appears where a name was expected, a new person is introduced, and the “obvious” name turns out wrong. A model that bet the farm on its name-movers every time would be brutally penalised on those occasions, for the loss it is trained against punishes confident error far more than honest hedging. The negative name-mover heads are the model’s learned hedge: they attend to the very name the positive heads are promoting and shave its probability back down a notch, so the model never commits *quite* so hard that an unexpected continuation ruins it. Later analysis pinned this down handsomely — these heads measurably *improve the model’s calibration*, trading a sliver of confidence on the easy cases for protection against the hard ones. It is a tiny internal actuary, pricing the risk of overconfidence, and no one designed it: it was worth a fraction of a nat of loss — the natural-log unit the loss is kept in — so gradient descent built it. And you will recognise them, sir: these are the *suppression heads* I mentioned in the very first episode, the ones that *lower* probabilities rather than raise them — and they are cousins, too, of the safety-relevant *withholding* and *secrecy* features we found in the fifth. The negative name-mover head is suppression *caught in the act within a known circuit*, doing visible calibration work. The fifth family I shall hold for its own section, because it is the inconvenient discovery that the whole episode has been building toward — the **backup name-mover heads**.

That, then, is the indirect-object circuit in its principal outline: duplicate-token, previous-token, and induction heads detect the repeat; inhibition heads pass the pointer that biases the query; name-mover heads copy the survivor; negative name-mover heads calibrate the confidence — a multi-stage, intervention-verified algorithm for a real linguistic task, the field’s proof that a model’s computation *can* be traced to the wire. And the cast, it has since emerged, was not hired for this one production: later work kept finding the same head families — movers, suppressors, duplicate detectors — reprising their roles in other tasks and other models, universality paying out at the level of whole circuits rather than single parts.

I must, though, enter one honest qualification before I let its lustre tempt us into overconfidence, because the study’s own authors did, to their great credit, and because it foreshadows precisely the trouble of the next two sections. When they tested how much of the model’s behaviour their recovered circuit actually *reproduced* — running the circuit in isolation and scoring it — it accounted for most of the work but not all; and when later scrutiny applied a stricter test still — the discipline the field came to call *causal scrubbing*, which demands that the *right information flow along the right paths*, rather than merely that the right heads be present — the explanation looked somewhat less complete than the headline faithfulness number first suggested. This is not a mark against the work — it is the finest circuit dissection we have — but a mark of how *hard* completeness is: even the reference example, the one everyone cites, leaves a remainder. Hold that remainder in mind. What comes next will explain a good part of where it hides, and it must come, because it is true.

## Part Six — The Inconvenient Discovery: Backups and the Hydra

Here is the fifth family, and the humbling discovery they embody. When the study did the obvious confirmatory experiment — *ablate the name-mover heads*, the ones that do the actual copying, and confirm that the model can no longer find the answer — something unexpected happened. The model *barely faltered*. Disable the heads you have just *proven* do the copying, the heads without which the answer cannot be produced, and the model *still produces the answer*, only a little less confidently. The “critical” component was removed and the machine *kept working*.

The explanation is the fifth family: the **backup name-mover heads**. These are heads that, in normal operation, sit *quiet* — they do little; they are not part of the working circuit you traced. But when the primary name-mover heads are *disabled*, the backups *wake up* and *take over the copying*, stepping into the role the primaries vacated, compensating for the loss almost completely. The model contains *redundant* machinery — understudies waiting in the wings — and when you ablate the lead, the understudy goes on, and the show continues. The field calls this phenomenon **self-repair**, or, with a vividness I rather admire, the **hydra effect** — for the mythical beast that grew two heads wherever one was struck off. Cut away the component doing the work, and the work *regrows* elsewhere.

I want you to feel the full weight of how *inconvenient* this is, because it is not a minor caveat; it strikes at the foundations of the method. Our entire interventional logic rested on a clean inference: *if disabling a component destroys the behaviour, the component was necessary; if disabling it changes nothing, the component was a bystander.* The hydra effect *breaks the second half of that inference*. A component can be *genuinely doing the work* — really, truly performing the computation in normal operation — and *yet*, when you ablate it, *the behaviour survives*, because a backup silently compensates. So *ablation can understate importance*: a part that looks dispensable by the ablation test may be doing real work that is merely *masked* by redundancy. Necessity becomes *slippery* — you can no longer read “ablation does nothing” as “this part does not matter,” because the part may matter and be backed up. The god’s-eye laboratory, it turns out, can be *fooled by the model’s own resilience*.

And it cuts deeper still, into the very notion of *the* circuit. We spoke of finding *the* indirect-object circuit — the wiring diagram, as though it were unique, the one path by which the model does the task. But the existence of backups means *there is no single path*. There is a *primary* circuit and a *shadow* circuit ready to take over, and very likely shadows behind the shadows — a *distribution* of overlapping, partially-redundant mechanisms, any of which can carry the load if the others fail. The clean diagram with its tidy arrows is a *simplification* — true as far as it goes, a faithful account of the *primary* pathway, but not the *whole* story, because the whole story includes the understudies and the understudies’ understudies. Frontier models, it appears, are *robust* in a way that actively *resists clean dissection*: they distribute their competence redundantly, so that no single neat circuit is ever the entire truth, and the very property that makes them reliable — their graceful degradation under damage — is the property that frustrates our wish to say *this, exactly this, is how it works*. The machine is harder to understand *because* it is well-built.

## Part Seven — When Is a Circuit Explanation Finished? Faithfulness and Its Illusions

The hydra effect forces a question that the field has had to take seriously: *what would it even mean to have a complete, correct account of a circuit?* If there are backups and shadows, when may you declare an explanation *done*? The field has articulated criteria, and they are worth your knowing, because they are what keep this enterprise a *science* rather than a gallery of plausible stories.

Three criteria. **Faithfulness**: does the circuit you have identified actually *reproduce the model’s behaviour* — if you run *only* your proposed circuit, isolating it from the rest, does it do what the full model does? An unfaithful circuit is a story that does not actually compute the result. **Completeness**: have you found *all* the components that matter — including, the hydra warns us, the backups and the negative heads and the parts whose contributions are easy to miss? An incomplete circuit omits load-bearing machinery. **Minimality**: is every component in your circuit *actually necessary* — or have you swept in bystanders, roosters that crow but do no work? A non-minimal circuit includes parts that do not earn their place. A good explanation is *faithful, complete, and minimal* — it reproduces the behaviour, leaves nothing important out, and includes nothing superfluous. Stating and *testing* against these criteria is what distinguishes a rigorous circuit analysis from a just-so story.

And the reason such rigour is not optional — the reason a field could not simply trust its narratively-satisfying explanations — is the standing danger of what are called **interpretability illusions**. An interpretability illusion is a story that *fits all your data and is nonetheless wrong*. The history of the field has real examples, and they are sobering. A direction that appears to *cleanly* encode some concept on the dataset you examined turns out to encode *something different* on another dataset — the interpretation was an artefact of your particular sample, not a property of the model. A feature that seems to *cause* a behaviour turns out to be tracking a *correlate* of the true cause, riding alongside the real machinery. A circuit that is *faithful* on the easy, clean examples you tested it on *breaks* on harder cases the model also handles — your account captured a fair-weather approximation, not the robust mechanism. In every illusion, the story was *consistent with the evidence in hand* and *false* — which is precisely why consistency with evidence is not enough, and why the interventional discipline, the both-directions patching, the careful ablation, the faithfulness-completeness-minimality checking, exists. These are the methodological antibodies against fooling oneself, and in a field where the object of study cannot speak and the stories are seductive, fooling oneself is the occupational hazard. The rigour is not pedantry. It is the immune system.

I should add, lest the episode read as pure caution, that this rigour *scales*. **Attribution patching** uses a single backward pass of gradients to estimate, cheaply and all at once, what patching each of thousands of components would do — a screening instrument whose honest caveats I shall bank for the dividend in a moment — and **automated circuit discovery** sets algorithms to search and prune the computational graph toward the responsible subgraph, automating what the indirect-object study did by months of hand labour; the field has since taken to benchmarking the discovery algorithms themselves, which is what maturing looks like. Both point toward the *automated interpretability* that the seventh episode will weigh.

## Part Eight — The Engineer’s Dividend

This episode has been a course in experimental method, and method, sir, travels well — the discipline of intervention has more to say to your daily work than any single circuit. Four transfers to the bench.

*First, the whole episode is a standing rebuke to a habit endemic in applied machine learning: explanation by what lights up.* Your observability stack, your attention visualisers, your feature dashboards, the attribution your evaluation tooling proudly surfaces — every one of them is *observational*, and every one is therefore a generator of roosters. The component that activates when your model misbehaves is a *suspect*, not a culprit, and the only thing that promotes it from one to the other is an *intervention*: change it, hold the rest fixed, re-run, and see whether the misbehaviour moves. When a colleague says “the model attends to the system prompt here, so that’s what’s driving the refusal,” you are now equipped to ask the one question that matters — *did you patch it?* — and to distrust the claim, gently, until they have.

*Second, the clean experimental design of activation patching is directly portable to debugging.* The essence was: construct two inputs differing in *exactly one* respect, so that any change in behaviour is attributable to that respect and nothing else. That is precisely the discipline that turns a flailing investigation of a production regression into a tractable one. When a fine-tuned model fails on some input, the instinct is to stare at the failing case; the method says instead to *manufacture its minimal pair* — the nearest input that *succeeds* — and let the difference between them localise the cause. The art is all in the *almost identical*, as it was in the laboratory.

*Third, attribution patching is a serving-friendly idea you may already have the machinery for.* Recall its trick: rather than the ruinous expense of patching every component in turn, use a single backward pass of *gradients* to estimate, all at once, what patching each component *would* do — screen broadly and cheaply, then confirm the few survivors rigorously. Anyone who has profiled a large model to learn which components carry which behaviours, or who has reached for gradient-based attribution to decide what may safely be pruned or quantised, is working a neighbouring seam. The interpretability framing buys you the crucial caveat the raw technique omits: the gradient estimate is a *linear approximation*, it degrades exactly where effects are large and saturating — which is to say where they matter most — and so it is a screening instrument, never a verdict. Cheap to screen, dear to confirm; never ship the screen as the confirmation.

*Fourth, and most useful: the hydra effect should change how you read your own ablation experiments.* You will, in the ordinary course of your work, disable things — drop a retrieval component, remove a tool, ablate an adapter, strip a section of a prompt — and observe that the system’s quality *barely moves*, and conclude that the thing was not pulling its weight. The self-repair discovery says: *not so fast.* A component can be doing real work and have its removal silently compensated by redundant machinery downstream, so that “we took it out and nothing broke” is genuinely ambiguous between “it was useless” and “it was useful and something covered for it.” The two have opposite implications for what you should do next, and only a more careful experiment — ablating the suspected backups *as well*, or measuring the compensation directly — can tell them apart. In a frontier model, robustness and redundancy are features; to the engineer trying to attribute a behaviour, they are a fog, and knowing the fog is there is the difference between a sound inference and a confident error.

## Closing — What We Have Earned, and Where It Points

Let us gather the episode.

We confessed the limit of everything prior — observation cannot tell a cause from a rooster — and took up the god’s-eye laboratory’s discipline: **activation patching** (clean and corrupted runs, one organ transplanted, the output watched; *noising* for necessity, *denoising* for sufficiency), **path patching** (from a set of suspects to a wiring diagram), **direct logit attribution** (fast, and blind to indirect effects), and the subtle art of ablation, where *zero*, *mean*, and *resample* give different verdicts because zero knocks the model off-distribution. The method’s reward was the **indirect-object circuit** traced to the wire in GPT-2 small: detection by duplicate-token and induction machinery reused from earlier episodes, decision by inhibition heads passing a *pointer*, copying by name-movers, calibration by their negative twins — every junction verified by intervention, and even so an honest remainder left over. Then the humbling: **backup heads** and the **hydra effect** — ablate the proven component and understudies take over — so ablation can understate importance, necessity is slippery, and *the* circuit is really a distribution of overlapping mechanisms; the machine resists dissection *because* it is well-built. Against **interpretability illusions** we armed ourselves with *faithfulness, completeness, minimality* and the stricter scrubbing; and the dividend carried the method to your bench — patch before you blame, manufacture the minimal pair, screen with gradients but never ship the screen, and read your own ablations with the hydra in mind.

We can now *read* the model’s features and *trace* its circuits, with rigour and with due humility about what eludes us. Which brings us, at last, to the questions this whole course has been walking toward — the questions of *action* and of *trust*. If we can read and trace, can we also *steer* — reach in and *change* what the model does, deliberately, by the very interventions we have learned to use as probes? And, far more gravely: can these instruments tell us whether a model *is what it appears to be* — whether, beneath behaviour that looks aligned and truthful, the model is in fact aligned and truthful, or merely *behaving so while observed*? That second question — raised in our very first episode and deferred ever since — is the question of the age, and it is unanswerable from the outside, by watching behaviour alone. Internal methods are the *only* serious instrument for looking *in*.
