Reading the Mind of the Machine

A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability — Episode One

The Grown, Not Built, Object

Good day, sir.

I should like to open this course with a confession, offered on behalf of an entire industry. It is this: nobody fully knows how these models work. The fact does not appear in the promotional literature, for reasons one can readily supply, but it is the founding fact of the science before us, and I would sooner you had it at the outset, between ourselves. I notice the periodicals have lately taken to declaring this science a breakthrough technology — an honour it will no doubt survive — but they declare it for the right reason, which is that the confession is true and somebody ought to be doing something about it.

I do not mean the confession in the cheap sense a journalist means it, all portent and no content. I mean something precise and considerably more awkward. We know, exactly, how a large language model is built — we shall spend whole episodes on nothing else. We know the architecture of every layer. We know the training procedure to the last detail. Every equation can be written on a whiteboard by a competent graduate student. And yet, when the finished model sits before us and produces a particular sentence, we cannot, in general, say why it produced that sentence and not some other. We possess the complete blueprint of the factory and not the faintest idea what the factory is thinking.

The reason for this predicament is the premise of the whole field, so it repays a moment’s attention. A conventional piece of software is written. Some human being decided, line by line, what it should do, and if you wish to know why it behaves as it does, you read the lines; the author’s intentions are right there in the text, available for inspection and, when the thing misbehaves, for blame. A neural network is not written. It is grown. We specify an architecture — a shape, an empty trellis of billions of numbers — and we specify a training objective, and then we pour a substantial fraction of all human writing through it for months, and the numbers settle. The final settlement of those billions of numbers — the thing we call the weights — is the program. And no one wrote it. It was found, by an optimisation process, in a space far too large for any mind to survey, selected only by the blind criterion of predicting the next word a little better. The model is not a designed artefact. It is a discovered one — nearer to a biological specimen than to a bridge, something to be dissected rather than consulted.

Mechanistic interpretability is the science of that dissection. It is the attempt to take a trained model, reach inside it, and recover the algorithms it has learned — not its behaviour observed from without, which is mere psychology, but the actual computational machinery within. To take the grown thing and turn it, in part, back into something that can be read.

Why should you care, sir, as a working engineer with production systems to run and no intention of publishing a paper? Three reasons, which the course will spend itself justifying, but which I shall state plainly now.

The first is safety, and it is the one that matters most, so I shall return to it at the very end of this course and let everything between build toward it. The short form: a model entrusted with consequential work needs a better guarantee than “it has behaved well so far.” We should very much like to know whether a model is, for instance, being deceptive — whether it has internally represented one thing and told us another. That cannot be established by watching it from the outside, for reasons I will make precise in the final episode. It can only be established by looking in.

The second is capability, plainly self-interested. A great many of the practical advances of recent years — in how these models are served, fine-tuned, compressed — came from someone understanding a mechanism well enough to exploit it. Interpretability is not the opposite of engineering. It sits upstream of it, the way knowing what is actually wrong with the engine sits upstream of fixing the car. The mechanic who only listens to the noise and the mechanic who has the thing open on the bench are doing different work, and charging differently for it.

The third is that the questions are, simply, among the most interesting now available to a curious person. Inside these models there are structures nobody designed, doing jobs nobody specified, and we are only beginning to read them. If that does not quicken the pulse a little, this may be a long seven episodes.

Here is the route. We begin with the one structural idea everything hangs from — the residual stream, the model’s shared channel of communication — and with the first, crudest instruments for reading it mid-thought. We then ask what is actually written in that channel, and meet the field’s central working assumption: that the model’s concepts are directions in a high-dimensional space. Assumptions being cheap, I will then lay out the evidence — including a small model, trained on nothing but the moves of a board game, that was caught secretly keeping the board, which remains the cleanest demonstration on record that these systems build models of their worlds rather than merely parroting their training data. From there we confront the strangest fact about how the knowledge is stored: a phenomenon called superposition, which is at once the reason interpretability is hard and the reason it is possible at all. We meet the instrument that unpacks it — the sparse autoencoder — and then the second great object of the science, circuits, the little algorithms that compute with concepts, together with the older anatomy of image models from which the word was borrowed. I will trace one circuit all the way to the wire, so that the claim “these things can be understood” arrives backed by a worked example rather than by my assurance. And we close on an honest accounting of what the field cannot yet do, because a science is known as much by the shape of its ignorance as by its results.

That is the panorama. The six episodes that follow descend into it — attention read closely, the origin of in-context learning, superposition made rigorous, the instrument that reads features at scale, the discipline of intervention by which we run experiments on these systems, and finally the steering of behaviour and the question of trust. But the panorama first; one likes to see the country before walking into it.

A wide subject, sir. In we go.

Part One — The Residual Stream as a Shared Notebook

Everything in this episode hangs from one structural idea, and if I fail to make it vivid in the next few minutes, nothing afterward will hold. So let us not rush it.

Recall the rough shape of a transformer — the architecture under every large language model now in service. It is a tall stack of identical layers, ninety-six of them in a large model, and a piece of text, once chopped into tokens — the word-pieces of the model’s fixed vocabulary, some tens of thousands of them, each assigned a learned starting vector — passes upward through that stack from bottom to top. At the summit, the model commits to its prediction of the next token.

The natural picture of this is a bucket brigade. Layer one does some work and hands its result to layer two; layer two works on that and hands the result to layer three; and so on to the top, each layer receiving its predecessor’s output, transforming it, passing it along. The information is relayed, hand to hand, and at each handover the previous result is consumed and replaced.

That picture is wrong, and the precise manner of its wrongness is the foundation of the entire field, so I want to replace it carefully rather than merely correct it.

What actually happens is this. Running up the full height of the model, from the first layer to the last, there is a single persistent object. At each token position it is a vector — a long list of numbers, perhaps twelve thousand of them in a large model. This object is the residual stream. And the layers do not relay it from one to the next. The layers read from it and write to it.

Let me be exact, because the exactness is the point. A layer does not replace the residual stream with its output. A layer computes a small adjustment — a contribution — and adds that contribution onto the stream. The stream flows past the layer largely undisturbed; the layer reaches in, reads the current state, computes something, and adds its contribution back. Then the stream, now slightly amended, flows on to the next layer, which does the same. Addition, not replacement. The distinction will turn out to carry a great deal of weight.

The image I want you to hold for the rest of the episode is a shared notebook carried up a long table of clerks. The notebook opens with the raw input written on its first page. Each clerk in turn reads everything currently written, thinks, and adds a note of his own. No clerk erases the notebook and starts fresh; no clerk is even permitted to. Every clerk only ever adds. By the time the notebook reaches the last clerk at the head of the table, it holds the accumulated annotations of every clerk below, and the final clerk reads the whole accumulation and announces the prediction.

This architectural choice — layers adding to a shared stream rather than replacing it — has a name. It is the residual connection, or skip connection, and it was introduced years ago for a thoroughly unglamorous reason: it made very deep networks trainable at all, by giving the training signal a clean path from top to bottom that did not have to claw its way back through every intervening layer. The engineers who introduced it were solving a numerical nuisance. They did not, I think, quite realise they were also settling how machine cognition would file its thoughts for the next decade. Men have stumbled into consequential things while reaching for smaller ones before; this was a larger instance than most.

Here is why it matters so much for our purposes. Because every layer adds to a common stream, the residual stream is a shared communication channel. A layer near the bottom can write a piece of information into it, and a layer near the top — eighty layers later — can read that very same piece of information, intact, because nothing in between was obliged to disturb it. The layers do not converse by passing messages down a line, each message degrading at every relay like a whisper around a dinner table. They converse by leaving notes in a common book that any later layer may consult directly. You will know from your own working life, sir, that the two arrangements have entirely different failure modes.

This gives us our first real foothold. If the residual stream is a notebook of accumulated notes, then interpretability becomes, in principle, the act of reading the notebook. We may halt the model at any layer, at any token position, lift out the residual-stream vector at that point, and ask: what has been written here so far? What does the model, at this stage of its thinking, currently hold to be true?

And we can be bolder still. There is a technique called the logit lens, and it does something I find genuinely lovely. We take the residual stream at some intermediate layer — halfway up the stack — and apply to it the very operation the model would normally apply only at the summit: the unembedding — a fixed matrix that scores every entry in the vocabulary against the stream, converting it into a prediction. In effect we interrupt the model mid-thought and demand: if you had to guess the next token right now, at layer forty-eight, with only half your thinking done — what would you say?

And it answers. And the answer instructs. For an easy, predictable token, the model’s guess is already correct and settled a fraction of the way up the stack, and the many layers above scarcely touch it — there is nothing left to do, and they sensibly do nothing. For a genuinely hard token, the guess goes on shifting, layer after layer, sometimes lurching from one candidate to another, right up to the final layers. The notebook lets us watch the model make up its mind — to see a thought converge in real time, or fail to. We are, in a small way, reading intermediate cognition off the page.

I must enter one caution here, since you, sir, are precisely the sort of person liable to try this at home. The logit lens, applied naively, works delightfully on some model families and produces fluent nonsense on others. The reason is not that the reticent models lack intermediate thoughts. It is that the notebook’s handwriting drifts as it ascends — each region of the stack develops small notational conventions of its own — and the summit’s reading apparatus is fitted to the summit’s hand. Ask it to read a middle layer’s script and it transliterates gamely and wrongly. The remedy, called the tuned lens, is to train a small translation for each layer: a learned adjustment that renders that layer’s script into the one the final readout expects. Do this, and the legible picture returns — the same gradual convergence of a prediction, now visible in models that had seemed to refuse the trick. The moral deserves filing where you can find it again: even our simplest instrument is itself a trained object, and every reading is a joint product of the specimen and the lens. We shall meet this moral repeatedly, wearing progressively more expensive clothing.

So: the residual stream is a shared notebook; layers read from it and add to it; and because of that additive, shared structure, the model’s intermediate thoughts are legible objects we can lift out and inspect. Hold that above all else. Everything to come is built on it.

But I have been quietly dishonest, in the way one is dishonest to a guest by tidying the drawing room and shutting the door on the box-room. I said the residual-stream vector is a list of twelve thousand numbers, and I have spoken as though we may simply read it. Twelve thousand numbers is not a sentence. It is not English. It is an enormous, opaque column of decimals. To say “we lift out the residual stream and inspect it” invites the obvious and devastating question: inspect it how? In what script is the notebook written?

That question is the whole of the next two parts. The answer is stranger than a reasonable person would guess.

Part Two — What Is Written in the Notebook: Features and Directions

We have a notebook. Now we must learn its handwriting, and we begin with the most elementary question imaginable: what is the unit of meaning? When the model wishes to record “this text is in French,” or “we are inside a quotation,” or “the current token is a verb” — how does it commit that to a list of twelve thousand numbers?

The natural first guess is what we might call the one-number-per-idea theory. Perhaps the four-hundred-and-seventeenth number in the list is the “French” number, large when the text is French and small when it is not; each idea assigned its own dedicated slot, like a labelled dial on a control panel. The technical name for this hope is a privileged basis — the notion that the individual coordinates, the individual numbers, are themselves the meaningful units.

This guess is almost entirely wrong, and understanding why is the gateway to everything. (I will enter one caveat now and redeem it much later: there turns out to be a single corner of the model where something like this hope is partly vindicated — where the individual numbers do carry meaning. We shall find it in the fourth episode, and it is the exception that proves how thoroughly the rule holds everywhere else. Set it aside for now.)

The actual unit of meaning is not a single number. It is a direction. The idea will repay slow building, so let us build it slowly.

Picture, not twelve thousand dimensions, which no human can, but three. A vector in three dimensions is an arrow from the origin to some point in space. The arrow has components — its extent along each of the three axes — but the arrow itself, as a geometric thing, the direction in which it points, belongs to no single axis. It is a blend of all three. You can rotate your coordinate axes to any orientation you like and the arrow goes on pointing the same physical way; the components change, the arrow does not. The direction is the real thing; the components are bookkeeping.

The claim of the field — and I shall present the evidence for it presently, rather than asking you to take a valet’s word — is that the model represents a concept as a direction in the residual-stream space. There is some particular direction, some particular arrow assembled from a combination of many of the twelve thousand numbers, that means “French.” When the model wishes to note “this is French,” it adds a vector pointing along that direction into the stream. To check whether the text is French, a later layer measures how much of the stream points along that direction. The concept lives not in a number but in an alignment.

This is the linear representation hypothesis, the central working assumption of the field: that human-interpretable concepts correspond to directions in the model’s activation space, and that the model combines them by addition. “French” is a direction. “Formal register” is another direction. A passage that is both French and formal is represented, near enough, by the sum of the two — the arrows added tip to tail, as one adds forces in a mechanics problem. Concepts compose like vectors.

The idea has a respectable ancestry, and you will very likely recall it. A dozen years ago, the first word-embedding models — word2vec and its relations — exhibited a celebrated parlour trick: take the vector for king, subtract the vector for man, add the vector for woman, and the nearest word to the resulting vector was queen. The trick worked because those small models had, without anyone asking, encoded concepts as directions — there was a direction for royalty and a direction for, roughly, maleness, and a word sat at the sum of the directions of what it meant. The linear representation hypothesis is the claim that this was never a parlour trick at all, but a first sighting of the general organising principle: that the residual stream of a frontier model is word2vec’s modest arithmetic, grown to civilisational scale.

Now I can give the field’s central noun its name. A feature is one of these meaningful directions — an interpretable direction in activation space standing for some property of the input. “This text is French” is a feature. “We are inside a pair of quotation marks” is a feature. “The sentiment here is regretful” is a feature. “The author is enthusiastic on the subject of newts” is, presumably, a feature, though one imagines it fires less often than its devotees would wish. Features are the nouns of the model’s inner language — the vocabulary of concepts from which its thoughts are assembled.

And here, at once, we can see why the one-number-per-idea theory was doomed, and the argument is so clean it is almost a pleasure to state. It is a counting argument. A large model’s residual stream has, say, twelve thousand dimensions. If each concept demanded its own dedicated dimension, its own private number, the model could hold at most twelve thousand distinct concepts. Twelve thousand, and not one more.

But consider what a frontier model demonstrably knows. Tens of thousands of public figures. The characteristic vocabulary of hundreds of academic fields. Thousands of programming constructs, idioms in dozens of human languages, the plots of countless books, the rules of countless games, the smell of a particular decade’s prose. The count of distinct concepts a large model has internalised is not twelve thousand. By any honest estimate it runs to the millions, very possibly the tens of millions.

So observe the corner the model is backed into. It must represent tens of millions of concepts. It has twelve thousand dimensions to do it in. The naive scheme is not merely wasteful; it is arithmetically impossible — the concepts outnumber the dimensions by perhaps a thousand to one. It is the demand that a librarian shelve a million volumes in a bookcase built for a thousand, and do it so that any volume can still be found.

How the model manages that feat is the subject of the fourth part of this episode, and it is the strangest material we shall cover today. But before the strangeness, I owe you the evidence I promised — the reason the field believes in features-as-directions at all, beyond the convenience of believing it. For that we leave language aside entirely, and go to the games room.

Part Three — The Board in the Machine

I have given you a hypothesis and called it the field’s central working assumption, and you would be entirely within your rights, sir, to ask what is holding it up. The claim that a model’s concepts are directions in a vector space has, after all, a slightly convenient ring — it is the sort of thing one might come to believe because one’s research programme would be very agreeable if it were true. And there is a sterner scepticism in circulation besides, which holds that nothing of the kind is happening at all: that a language model is an enormous table of surface statistics — a parrot of unusual diligence — repeating arrangements of words it has seen, with no representation of the world the words describe.

The difficulty in settling the dispute on a frontier model is that the world of general text is unbounded and largely unknowable; we cannot check the model’s internal representation of “the world” against the world, because nobody has the world written down. What one wants is a laboratory case: a model whose entire universe is small, closed, and known to us exactly, so that its inner representations can be checked against ground truth. The field found its laboratory in the board game Othello — played, you will recall, on an eight-by-eight grid, the discs flipping their allegiance when flanked, a property that will acquire a certain resonance presently.

The experiment, due to Kenneth Li and colleagues, ran as follows. A small transformer was trained on transcripts of Othello games and nothing else — sequences of moves, each move a token naming a square. The model was given no rules, no picture of a board, not so much as a hint that its tokens named positions on a grid. Its task was the usual one: predict the next token. And it learned the task uncomfortably well — asked to continue a game, it produced a legal move on better than ninety-nine point nine per cent of occasions. Now, legality in Othello is no light bookkeeping. Whether a move is legal depends on the full present configuration of the board, which depends on the entire history of captures and flips — which the model was never shown and was never told existed.

So the question poses itself sharply: by what means? Two hypotheses. The parrot hypothesis: the model has memorised a vast catalogue of statistical regularities over move sequences, with no notion of a board anywhere in it. The world-model hypothesis: somewhere in the residual stream, the model is keeping the board — maintaining, move by move, a representation of the state of all sixty-four squares — and consulting it.

Here the field reached for an instrument we shall use throughout the course, so let me introduce it properly. A probe is a small auxiliary classifier, trained by us, that takes the model’s internal activations as input and tries to predict some property we care about. If a probe can read the property out of the activations reliably, the information is in there. The researchers trained probes to look at the residual stream mid-game and answer, for each of the sixty-four squares, what currently occupies it.

The probes read the board. Square by square, with high accuracy, the state of a game the model had only ever experienced as a stream of move-tokens was sitting there in the activations. The model, never having been told there was a board, had concluded that there must be one — the board being, evidently, the cheapest hypothesis that accounts for the moves. One could wish that style of inference were more widely distributed.

But a careful person — and the researchers were careful — will note that reading is not yet proof of use. The board representation might be decoration: present in the activations, consulted by no one. So they intervened. Mid-game, they reached into the activations and edited them — altered the internal representation so that the board the model believed in differed from the board its move history implied; a disc flipped, in the model’s mind only. Then they let it continue. And the model played onward in accordance with the edited board — producing moves legal for the position it now believed in, including moves that were illegal in the game it had actually been fed. The board in the machine is not a commentary. It is the working state. The model consults it as a player consults the table.

Now the twist, sir, which is the portion of the story I most commend to your attention. In the original work, the probes that read the board well were nonlinear — small neural networks in their own right, capable of decoding rather convoluted encodings. Simple linear probes — the kind that merely measure the stream’s extent along a direction, which is what the linear representation hypothesis says ought to suffice — read the board poorly. This looked, for a moment, like genuine trouble for the hypothesis: here was a clean, verified internal representation, and it appeared not to be made of directions.

A researcher named Neel Nanda found the resolution, and it is instructive far beyond board games. The original probes had asked each square the natural human question: are you black, or are you white? But consider the model’s situation. It plays both sides — it predicts the next move for whomever is to move — and so from where it sits, the colour of a disc is a uselessly unstable property, flipping in significance with every turn. The serviceable bookkeeping, for a creature in that position, is not black against white but mine against theirs, relative to the player about to move. Re-ask the question in that vocabulary — is this square mine, or theirs? — and the board turns out to be linearly legible after all: plain directions, read by the plainest probes, exactly as the hypothesis ordered. The features were there the whole time. The failure had been ours — we had interrogated the model in our vocabulary instead of its own.

Three morals, in ascending order of importance.

First: world models can condense out of next-token prediction alone. No rules were given, no board was shown; the structure emerged because representing the world is the most economical way to predict the text the world generates. So when you hear it remarked that these systems “merely predict the next token,” you may agree without reservation, sir — in the spirit in which one agrees that an astronomer merely looks at lights in the sky.

Second: the linear representation hypothesis passed the sternest test then available, and passed it with a codicil. The concepts were directions — but directions drawn in the model’s ontology, not in ours. Mine-and-theirs, not black-and-white. This cuts both ways for the practitioner: when a probe comes back empty-handed, there are always two readings — either nothing is there, or you have asked in the wrong vocabulary — and a good deal of grief in this field traces to forgetting the second.

Third, and I shall permit myself the philosophy, since the occasion justifies it: Spinoza held that the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things. The little Othello model would appear to take the philosopher’s side — subject to the amendment, which he did not foresee, that the ideas may be filed under different headings than the things. Interpretability, you will find, is very largely the business of learning the headings.

We may now return, reassured, to the corner we left the model backed into: millions of concepts, each a direction, and only twelve thousand dimensions to point them in. The resolution is the strangest material in this episode.

Part Four — Superposition: A Thousand Ideas in a Hundred Dimensions

Superposition is the load-bearing wall of this entire subject, and on first hearing it can feel like a swindle. So let me build it from an intuition you already own, and then make it precise enough that the swindle turns out to be sound accounting.

Take a small case. Suppose you have a space of just two dimensions — a flat sheet of paper — and you wish to lay down concepts as directions on it. If you insist the directions be perpendicular — truly independent, non-interfering — you can fit exactly two. One up, one across. That is all a plane affords under a rule of strict perpendicularity.

Now relax the rule. Suppose the directions need not be perpendicular, only distinguishable — pointing in noticeably different directions, even if no two stand at a clean right angle. How many distinguishable directions fit on a sheet of paper? Not two. A great many — arrange them like the hours on a clock face, twelve of them, each plainly distinct from its neighbours, and not a right angle required among them.

That relaxation — from “perfectly perpendicular” to “merely distinguishable” — is the whole secret. And here is the fact that lifts it from a parlour trick to a deep principle. In two dimensions the gain is modest: a dozen directions instead of two. But the geometry of high-dimensional space is extravagantly, almost indecently, more generous. In a space of many dimensions, the number of directions you can pack so that any two are nearly perpendicular is not merely larger than the dimension count. It is exponentially larger. Twelve thousand dimensions hold not twelve thousand nearly-perpendicular directions but a number so vast it makes the count of concepts any model could ever want look like a rounding error.

The result has a respectable pedigree — it is in the neighbourhood of the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, and more broadly of the concentration-of-measure phenomena that make high-dimensional geometry behave so counterintuitively — but you do not need the theorem; you need the intuition, which is this: high-dimensional space is almost inconceivably roomy, provided you accept directions that are merely nearly-independent rather than perfectly so. It is the most useful single fact about high dimensions, and it runs hard against the picture our three-dimensional imaginations hand us, which is exactly why the field had to learn to trust the arithmetic over the imagination.

Superposition is the name for the model exploiting this. The model stores far more features than it has dimensions by giving each feature its own direction and accepting that the directions cannot all be perpendicular — that they must overlap, slightly, each treading faintly on its neighbours’ toes.

Now — nothing in this life is free, and you will already have the right objection ready. If the feature directions overlap, even slightly, they interfere. When the model writes the “French” feature into the notebook, a faint smudge of it lands along the “formal-register” direction too, because the two are not quite perpendicular. Reading the notebook is therefore always a slightly noisy affair; every feature read is faintly contaminated by every other feature sharing some of its direction.

So why does the model get away with it? Because of a second fact, which fits the first with a neatness I find quietly satisfying — the kind of fit that suggests one is looking at something true rather than something merely clever. The saving fact is sparsity. At any given moment, for any given token, the overwhelming majority of the model’s millions of features are simply not active. They are zero. Consider this very sentence: the feature for “this is legal boilerplate” is silent; the feature for “we are inside Python code” is silent; the feature for “the subject is marine biology” is silent. Of the millions of concepts the model could represent, only a tiny handful bear on any particular token, and the rest contribute precisely nothing.

And that is what renders superposition safe. The interference between overlapping features would indeed be ruinous if all the features spoke at once — the smudges would pile into an illegible grey. But because only a handful are ever active together, the chance that two currently active features collide badly is small. The interference is real, but it is diluted into harmlessness by sparsity. The model is, in effect, overbooking — selling more conceptual seats than the cabin holds, on the actuarially sound observation that its passengers almost never all travel at once.

So we may now state the central picture of how a model stores what it knows, in full and slowly, because everything downstream rests on it. The model represents millions of concepts as directions in a space of merely thousands of dimensions. It can do this because high-dimensional space affords an exponential abundance of nearly-perpendicular directions. The price is faint interference between overlapping features. And that price is made affordable because, at any one moment, only a sparse handful of features are active, so the interference rarely compounds into anything that matters.

This one idea reconciles the two facts about interpretability that would otherwise flatly contradict each other. It explains why interpretability is hard: the model’s concepts are not filed tidily one per dimension; they are smeared across the dimensions in overlapping combinations, so you cannot read a feature by reading off a number. And it explains, in the very same breath, why interpretability is possible at all: there is a clean structure in there — the features, the directions — only encoded, folded, compressed. The information has not been destroyed. It has been packed. And whatever has been packed by a regular procedure can, in principle, be unpacked by another.

The instrument that unpacks it is the subject of the next part — though I shall treat it here only in outline, since a later episode is devoted to building it properly, breaking it, and being honest about its distortions. For the moment, the outline.

Part Five — The Sparse Autoencoder: An Instrument for Unfolding

We have a problem of a very particular shape. The model has taken millions of clean, interpretable features and folded them, by superposition, into a few thousand dimensions of overlapping, interfering activation. We should like to unfold them — to take the smeared, packed residual-stream vector and recover the list of crisp, human-meaningful features that went into it.

For several years this was the central frustration of the field. We knew, from the counting argument, that the features were in there. We could not reliably get them out. The occasional feature could be found by luck or by patient manual labour, but reading the model’s vocabulary systematically, at scale, lay beyond reach — rather like knowing a manuscript is written in a real language while lacking any means to read more than the odd recurring word.

The breakthrough is recent, and it genuinely reanimated the field. It is an instrument called the sparse autoencoder. Said aloud, the field abbreviates it to a string of letters; I shall mostly just call it the autoencoder and spare us both.

Here is the idea, and once seen it has the satisfying inevitability of the right key turning in a lock that had resisted every wrong one. An autoencoder, in general, is a device with a deliberately frustrating task: you give it an input, and you ask it to reproduce that input as its output. Trivial so far — it could merely copy. But you force the information, in passing through, to squeeze through some constraint, so that mere copying is impossible and the device must instead learn an efficient re-encoding — must discover the input’s underlying structure in order to rebuild it through the constraint.

The sparse autoencoder uses a particular and counterintuitive constraint. We take the residual-stream vector of the model under study — our packed, folded, twelve-thousand-number object. We train an autoencoder to reproduce it. But the constraint we impose is not one of size; it is the reverse. We pass the vector through a much wider middle layer — not twelve thousand slots but perhaps five hundred thousand, or a million. A space far larger than the original.

And then — here is the lock-pick — we impose a ferocious penalty on that wide middle layer for having too many of its slots active at once. We demand that, for any given input, the wide layer represent it using only a tiny handful of its million slots — a few dozen, perhaps — with all the rest held at zero. We force it to be sparse.

Now set what we have demanded against the picture from the last part. We have asked for a re-encoding that is, first, very wide — up to millions of slots — and second, very sparse — only a few dozen active per input. Those are precisely the two properties of the model’s true feature vocabulary: millions of features, only a sparse handful active per token. So in forcing the autoencoder to find a wide, sparse re-encoding, we are forcing it to rediscover the very vocabulary the model folded away. Its wide middle layer is compelled, by its constraints, to spend one slot per genuine feature. We have built a machine whose only route to success is to unfold the superposition.

And — this is the part that lifted the field’s collective spirits — it works. Train such an autoencoder on a real model, inspect the slots of its wide middle layer, and you find them, to a remarkable and checkable degree, individually interpretable. One slot fires on, and near enough only on, mentions of the Golden Gate Bridge. Another on text in the subjunctive. Another on a specific genus of bug in C code. Another on a particular note of sycophantic flattery. The autoencoder has gone down into the model’s folded, illegible activation and come back up with a dictionary — a long list of discrete, nameable concepts, each in its own clean slot, no longer smeared, no longer in superposition.

The most vivid public demonstration of this involved exactly that Golden Gate feature, and I mention it because it made an abstract idea suddenly concrete for a great many people who had no patience for the theory. Researchers, having located the single slot for the Golden Gate Bridge, did not merely observe it. They reached in and clamped it on — forced that one feature strongly active regardless of the input — and the model became, there is no better word, obsessed. Asked about nearly anything, it found its way to the bridge. Asked to describe itself, it declared itself to be the bridge, fog and traffic and all. One has encountered similar conviction in gentlemen after a strong lunch, sir, though rarely sustained with such perfect consistency. This was faintly comic and meant to be, but under the comedy sat a serious proof. It showed the feature was no passive correlate, no coincidence of observation, but a causal lever: the model genuinely used that direction as its internal representation of the concept, and turning the lever turned the behaviour. The dictionary the autoencoder recovered was not a story we imposed from outside. It was the model’s own vocabulary, read back to it — and it answered to its own words.

I shall not pretend the autoencoder is a finished instrument; an entire later episode is given over to its limits, which are real and in places humbling, and to a recent and rather bracing dispute about how much the instrument can be trusted at all. But mark the change of situation. Before it, reading a model’s features was artisanal — slow, manual, one lucky find at a time. After it, the thing became something nearer industrial: train the autoencoder, receive a dictionary of millions of features, more or less automatically. That is a genuine threshold, and the field crossed it only lately. One calibrating note from further down the road, so that your enthusiasm may age as the field’s own has: the instrument’s reign as the instrument proved brief — the supplement will watch its crown pass to successors built on the same pattern but aimed at the model’s computation rather than its contents. So admire the threshold, and hold the admiration loosely until the fifth episode’s reckoning.

We have, then, the nouns — the features, and a machine to extract them. But a vocabulary is not a language. To understand the model’s thinking, and not merely its concepts, we need the verbs — we need to know not only what the model represents but how it computes one thing from another. We need circuits.

Part Six — Circuits: The Verbs of the Machine

A feature is a noun — a concept the model can hold. But a model does not merely hold concepts; it transforms them. It reads features present in the input and computes new features from them — takes “the previous word was ‘Mr’” and computes “the next word is probably a surname.” That computation — the learned little algorithm wiring input features to output features — is what the field calls a circuit. If features are the vocabulary, circuits are the grammar — the rules by which features are assembled into inference. Stated in full, mechanistic interpretability is the project of recovering both: the dictionary and the grammar.

A word, first, about the word, because circuit is borrowed, and the loan ought to be declared. This field did not begin with language models. It began, in the late twenty-tens, with image classifiers, and with a research programme — associated with Chris Olah and his colleagues — that proposed to treat a trained vision network the way an anatomist treats a specimen: identify the organs, name them, and trace how they connect. The dissections performed on one venerable network, an image classifier called InceptionV1, remain among the most complete ever carried out, and they set the standards of evidence the whole field still aspires to.

The anatomists found, for instance, a family of curve detectors: a set of neurons sitting together in one layer, each responding to curved line segments at a particular orientation — one for curves opening upward, one for upward-and-to-the-left, and so on, stepping around the full compass. And the identification was not a guess from suggestive examples; it was established the way one would wish, by independent lines of evidence converging on the same verdict. Optimise an image from scratch to excite the neuron maximally, and the optimiser draws you a curve at the favoured orientation. Collect the natural photographs that excite it most, and they are full of such curves. Rotate a synthetic curve smoothly through a full turn, and the members of the family hand the response from one to the next, in order, each peaking as the curve passes through its preferred angle. And then the flourish, which I confess I find rather stylish: the researchers sat down and wrote the weights by hand — composed, from first principles, a curve detector of their own design — and the hand-made organ behaved like the grown one. When you can build the part from the blueprint you claim to have recovered, sir, the claim is in respectable health.

Higher in the network, the parts assemble. There is a unit that detects cars, and it was found to read, from the layer below, a wheel detector and a window detector — consulting the wheel detector toward the bottom of its field of view and the window detector toward the top, which is, on the whole, where wheels and windows keep. That arrangement — identified parts in one layer, combined by the next through weights that respect the structure of the thing being detected — was the first object the field called a circuit. And one further observation gave the programme its confidence: the same features, and some of the same circuits, kept turning up in different networks, of different architectures, trained by different hands on different data. Curve detectors, it appears, are not an idiosyncrasy of one network’s upbringing but a natural solution — something the task itself favours, rediscovered by gradient descent wherever gradient descent is set the task. The field calls this universality, and holds it, with appropriate tentativeness, as grounds for hope: if networks converge on common structure, then anatomy learned on one specimen transfers to the next.

So the credo of the circuits programme was established in vision: features are the parts, circuits are their wiring, and both are recoverable by patient dissection. Then the transformer arrived, the centre of gravity moved to language, and the question became whether the credo would survive the move. The demonstration that it did — the language circuit traced most nearly to the wire — is the field’s showpiece, and tracing it is the thing that converts “these models can be understood” from a hope into a result. It is called the induction head, and the function it implements is the model’s ability to continue a pattern it has already seen in the same text.

Here is the behaviour to be explained. Suppose, earlier in a passage, the text contained the name “Honoria Glossop.” And now, later, it says “Honoria” again. The model predicts that the next token is “Glossop” — with, indeed, the particular confidence of one who has met the lady. It has noticed a repetition and is completing it. A humble skill, on its face — but pause on how generally useful it is. It underwrites staying on topic, using a name consistently, holding to a format, picking up a rule from an example offered moments earlier within the prompt itself. The whole capacity to learn from the prompt — what the field calls in-context learning, and what makes these models useful in practice — rests substantially on this one mechanism. So you will want to know how it is built. The third episode will earn this in rigorous detail; here I give you the working version, complete enough to be true.

It is built, the researchers found, from two attention heads in two different layers, working in sequence. Neither can do the job alone; the circuit is the pair, and the way they cooperate has a certain economy.

The first is a previous-token head, and its whole trade is annotation: at every position it glances one step back and records, in that position’s notebook entry, what the token before it was — so the first “Glossop” carries, planted in its margin, the note the token before me was Honoria. The second — the induction head proper, in a layer above — hunts. Standing at the second “Honoria,” it searches the earlier positions for one whose annotation reads preceded by Honoria, finds the first “Glossop” so marked, reads what that token is, and copies it forward as the prediction. The pattern completes. The hunting is done with two pieces of machinery I shall name now and explain later: each position asks its question with a query and advertises what it can answer with a key, and attention flows to where question meets advertisement. Hold the two words loosely — they are on loan, and the next episode pays for them in full. And I leave the mechanism, deliberately, at this altitude: the full trace, wire by wire, with the interventions that prove the account true rather than merely tidy, is the third episode’s showpiece, and it is worth arriving there still hungry.

The arrangement repays a moment’s appreciation, sir. Two clerks of no individual distinction — one of whom can do nothing but glance a single step back, the other of whom can do nothing but hunt for a matching label — and between them, through the shared notebook, they implement a genuinely general algorithm: find where the present situation occurred before, and predict whatever followed it then. Nobody designed it. Nobody wrote it. It was grown — it emerged in training because it earned its keep — and it was found, years afterward, by dissection: components identified, wiring traced, the mechanism confirmed not by storytelling but by intervention, by reaching in and disturbing each part to verify it does what the account claims. That is what a solved circuit looks like, and it is the existence proof for the whole enterprise — the demonstration that the grown thing really can be turned, in places, fully back into the read thing.

I shall name one further mechanism, briefly, because it complicates the picture in an instructive direction and because it returns in a later episode. The model contains not only circuits that assert but circuits that object. There are heads — variously called suppression or negative heads — whose job is to attend to some feature and write a contribution that opposes it, damping down a prediction that another part of the model was about to make too confidently. They are part of how the model hedges and calibrates, how it restrains its own enthusiasm. It is worth absorbing the shape of this early: a forward pass is not a tidy production line of agreement. There is an internal politics to it — proposals and objections, motions and counter-motions — and the answer the model gives is the resolution of the quarrel, not the verdict of a single clerk.

So we now have both objects the field is after. Features — the nouns, the concepts, extracted by the sparse autoencoder. And circuits — the verbs, the small grown algorithms, traced by careful dissection and confirmed by intervention, in a tradition that runs from curve detectors to induction heads. With both, the full ambition can be stated at last: to produce, for a given model, something like a complete annotated wiring diagram — every feature labelled, every circuit traced — such that the grown, discovered, illegible object becomes, at last, read.

That is the ambition. Honesty now obliges me to tell you how far from it the field actually stands.

Part Seven — What This Science Cannot Yet Do

I have told you an encouraging story — the notebook legible, the features extractable, a board caught in the act of being kept, one circuit traced clean to the wire. It is a true story and the progress is real. But a candid account of a young science must include the border of its competence, and I would be a poor guide if I walked you to the edge of the map and let you believe it was the edge of the world. There are four limitations worth carrying, and none is minor.

The first is scale. The induction-head circuit I just paraded as a triumph was traced in small models, and largely by hand. A frontier model is vastly larger, its circuits correspondingly more numerous and more tangled. How many circuits a large model contains is not known, but the number is large, and we possess a complete, confirmed account of only a modest handful. The honest position: we have an excellent, validated methodology for understanding circuits, and we have applied it exhaustively to a small fraction of any real model. We hold a beautiful map of a few streets in a vast and mostly uncharted city.

The second: the autoencoder is an approximation, and a lossy one. It is a fine instrument, but it does not recover the model’s features perfectly. When it rebuilds an activation from its dictionary, the reconstruction is imperfect, and the lost remainder may hold real features the instrument simply failed to see. Worse, the dictionary is not unique: train the autoencoder a little differently and you get a somewhat different dictionary. Some recovered “features” are genuine; others are artefacts — a true feature split across two slots, or two unrelated concepts wrongly fused into one. We have an instrument; we do not yet fully trust its readings. It is a microscope known to be slightly out of focus — a more uncomfortable possession than a microscope known to be in focus, and a more honest one than pretending.

The third, and the most conceptually unsettling: the linear representation hypothesis may not hold everywhere. Our entire account rested on features being directions combined by addition, and I showed you it surviving its sternest test on the Othello board. For a great many features it appears genuinely true — itself a substantive empirical finding, not a thing we were entitled to assume. But it is not guaranteed of everything. There is accumulating evidence that some of what a model represents is not a simple direction — that some concepts live in curved or multi-dimensional structures for which “direction” is the wrong word entirely; we shall meet a specimen or two in the fourth episode. Where the linear hypothesis fails, our instruments, built atop it, may be quietly mischaracterising what they report. The foundation is strong. It is not known to be universal, and the difference matters.

The fourth: a labelled part is not an understood whole, and this is the deepest of the four. Grant us, generously, a complete dictionary of every feature and a complete catalogue of every circuit. Would we then understand the model? Not necessarily. Understanding a system whole requires understanding the interactions of its parts, and a frontier model has so many parts interacting in so many combinations that a complete parts-list might still defeat human comprehension by sheer combinatorial mass. There is a real and unresolved worry that a full mechanistic account of a large model would itself be too large for any mind to hold — that we would have merely translated the model from one incomprehensible form into another, better-organised but no more comprehensible. Whether interpretability ends in genuine human understanding, or only in a more neatly catalogued bewilderment, is not yet known. It is, to my eye, the most important open question in the field, and the one I would watch most closely.

I lay out the four not to deflate the episode but because you asked to understand this subject, and to understand a science is to know the shape of its ignorance alongside its results. I would not have you leave with the tidy and false impression that the mind of the machine has been read. It has not. What has happened is more modest and, I think, more genuinely impressive for being modest: a method has been found, and a small number of mechanisms have been traced completely and confirmed. The first complete circuit was the field’s Rosetta Stone: proof the script can be read. A great deal of the tablet remains untranslated, and some of it may be written in a hand we have not yet learned to recognise.

Closing — Why a Working Engineer Should Care

Let us gather the episode.

A large language model is a grown object, not a written one — its program discovered by optimisation, not authored by any hand — and that is the root of its opacity. Inside it runs the residual stream: a shared notebook carried up through the layers, into which every layer adds a contribution and from which every layer may read. That additive, shared structure is what makes the model’s intermediate thoughts legible at all — by the logit lens crudely, by the tuned lens with a little learned translation.

Written in that notebook are features — concepts as directions, word2vec’s old arithmetic grown to scale, confirmed on the Othello board in the model’s own vocabulary of mine-and-theirs. Because concepts outnumber dimensions a thousandfold, the model packs them by superposition — nearly-perpendicular directions, faint interference, made safe by sparsity — and the sparse autoencoder is the instrument that unfolds the packing, as circuits are the grown little algorithms that compute with what it unfolds. The ambition is the complete wiring diagram, dictionary and grammar both; the borders — scale, the instrument’s smudges, the linear hypothesis’s limits, and the chance that a complete account might still defeat comprehension — are real, and I have not hidden them.

Now — why should this matter to you, sir, with systems to run and no plan to publish? The practical case is stronger than it first looks.

It matters because the abstractions of this field are quietly becoming the abstractions of applied engineering. When you reason about why a fine-tuned model has regressed on some capability, features and circuits are the precise language for the thought. When you puzzle over why a quantised model degraded in one narrow respect and not others, you are reasoning — named or not — about which feature directions survived the loss of numerical precision. When a model misbehaves in production and you must form a hypothesis as to why, the gap between a vague guess and a mechanistic one is the gap between superstition and engineering — between muttering at the machine and opening it on the bench.

And it matters for the largest reason, the one I opened with and have been saving. These models are being handed steadily more consequential work. The question of whether a model is doing what it appears to do — whether its stated reasoning is its real reasoning, whether it harbours some objective other than the one we intended — is not a philosophical ornament. It is a safety question of the first order, and it is not answerable from the outside. No quantity of testing the behaviour can establish what the model is for, because behaviour is precisely what a model with something to hide would be most careful to control. That can be established only by looking in. Mechanistic interpretability is, at present, our only serious instrument for the looking — its one companion, the reading of a model’s written-out reasoning, being a genuinely useful instrument whose alarming fragility the eleventh episode will weigh. I shall not oversell the point; the point does not need it.

In the next episode we leave the panorama and descend into the particulars — into the attention mechanism, the most important of the layers, the place where information actually moves between positions. We shall pay, in full, for the two words I borrowed today on credit: the query and the key. And we shall find that a single attention head, which looks like one thing, is in truth two separate machines wearing one coat — one that decides where to look, and a quite different one that decides what to say once it has looked. I shall have it ready.

Until then, sir.

— End of Episode One.
