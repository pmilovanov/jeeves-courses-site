# Superposition and the Toy Models

*A Course in Mechanistic Interpretability -- Episode Four*

## The Fold We Have Been Avoiding

Good day, sir.

In each of the three episodes behind us I have leaned on a single assumption, and I have leaned on it without quite admitting how much weight it was bearing. The assumption is that the model’s units of meaning -- what we have been calling *features* -- exist as *directions* in the residual stream, one direction per concept, and that with the right instrument we might read them off. The “French” direction. The “Golden Gate Bridge” direction. The “preceded-by-Honoria” annotation. I have spoken of these as though they lie about the notebook tidily, awaiting only a sufficiently clever lens.

And in the first episode I told you the disquieting thing that undermines this tidiness -- though I told it quickly and moved on, the way one mentions a structural crack to a prospective buyer and then hurries him toward the garden. I told you that the model packs *far more* distinct features into the notebook than the notebook has dimensions to hold them at right angles. A model whose residual stream has, say, twelve thousand dimensions appears to traffic in *millions* of distinguishable concepts. The arithmetic does not balance. There are not enough perpendicular directions to go around. And the model’s resolution to this shortage -- the thing it actually does -- is to give up on perpendicularity and *fold the features together*, storing them along directions that are merely *nearly* perpendicular, overlapping slightly, treading faintly on one another’s toes. This is **superposition**, and I asked you, last episode, to face it squarely. Today we do.

I should not like you to mistake it for a minor technical wrinkle. Superposition is the central reason this entire science is *hard*. If features lay along the coordinate axes, one per dimension, interpretability would be a weekend’s filing: read off each number, consult the legend, done. If they merely lay along clean perpendicular directions, not aligned to the axes but at least orthogonal to each other, it would be harder but still tractable -- a rotation away from easy. But they do *neither*. They lie folded together at slight angles, thousands upon thousands of them sharing a space too small to hold them straight, and *that* fold is what we must somehow see through. Every difficulty in the episodes to come -- the need for an elaborate microscope in Episode Five, the slipperiness of circuits in Episode Six -- traces back to this fold.

So today I will not gesture at superposition. I will *manufacture* it, in a model small enough to understand completely -- a toy built precisely so that we may watch it decide to fold -- and from that toy we shall extract three things. First, *when* a model resorts to superposition and when it declines to. Second, *what geometric shapes* the folded features settle into, which turn out to be startlingly regular and to connect, rather pleasingly given your background, sir, to a classical problem in physics. And third, a fact I find genuinely unsettling and which I will not soften: that a model can perform real *computation* upon features *while they remain folded*, never unpacking them at all -- which means that even a perfect catalogue of a model’s features might fail to capture what the model is actually *doing*. And along the way I shall pay two outstanding debts: the promissory note from the first episode concerning features that are not directions at all, and the one from the second concerning the lone corner of the model where the coordinate axes mean something.

Let us build the toy.

## Part One -- Two Questions, Made Precise

Before the construction, let me sharpen the two questions the toy is meant to answer, because vague questions yield vague toys.

The first question is *when*. We have established that superposition is *available* to a model -- that the geometry of high-dimensional space, by the near-perpendicularity argument of the first episode, *permits* far more nearly-orthogonal directions than strictly orthogonal ones. But availability is not necessity. A model is not *obliged* to fold its features merely because it *could*. So: under what conditions does a model actually *choose* superposition, and under what conditions does it decline, keeping its features clean and simply doing without the ones it cannot fit? There must be a trade-off governing the choice, and we want its terms.

The second question is *what structure*. If the model does fold features together, it cannot do so carelessly -- features stored along directions that overlap too much would interfere ruinously, each contaminating the readout of the others. So the folding must be *organised*: the overlapping directions must be arranged to minimise the damage. What does that arrangement look like? Is it haphazard, or does it fall into patterns? And if patterns, which ones, and governed by what?

To make these answerable, the toy must give us two knobs to turn -- two properties of features that we suspect govern the trade-off -- and let us watch the model’s behaviour as we turn them. The two properties are these.

The first is **importance**: how much the model’s success depends on representing a given feature well. Some features matter enormously to the task; getting them wrong costs dearly. Others matter little; neglecting them costs almost nothing. In a real model this is set by how much each concept contributes to predicting text well; in our toy we shall simply *assign* each feature an importance and see how the model treats important and unimportant features differently.

The second is **sparsity**: how *often* a given feature is actually present, as opposed to absent. And this one is the crux, so let me dwell. Real-world features are overwhelmingly *sparse* -- meaning that, at any given moment, the vast majority of them are simply *not active*. The concept “Golden Gate Bridge” is absent from almost every sentence ever written; the concept “subjunctive mood in a relative clause” is absent from almost all of them too. On any particular token, only a tiny handful of the model’s millions of features are switched on; the rest lie dormant. Features are *rarely* present, and -- here is the consequence that will turn out to matter most -- they are therefore *very rarely present at the same time as each other*. Two random rare features almost never co-occur, simply because each is almost never around at all. We shall let our toy’s features be tunable from *dense* -- usually present -- to *sparse* -- usually absent -- and the heart of the episode is what changes across that range.

Importance and sparsity. Two knobs. Now the machine they are attached to.

## Part Two -- The Toy, Built to Be Understood

Here is the construction, and I will keep it in words, as is our custom, because the words are clearer than the symbols and kinder to the voice reading them.

We begin by *manufacturing* a supply of synthetic features -- let us say a fairly large number of them, more than the toy will have room to store straight. Each feature is, on any given example, either *present* (with some strength) or *absent*, and we control how often it is present by the sparsity knob, and how much the model is penalised for misrepresenting it by the importance knob. So we hold, on each example, a long list describing which features are present and how strongly -- the *true* state of the world we wish the model to capture.

Now the toy itself, which is deliberately almost trivial. It does three things in sequence. *First*, it takes that long list of feature-values and *squeezes it through a bottleneck* -- a linear transformation down into a space with *fewer dimensions than there are features*. This bottleneck is the whole point: it is too small to hold the features separately, and so it forces the model to *decide* what to do about the shortage. It is our stand-in for the residual stream, which likewise must hold more features than it has dimensions. *Second*, the model expands back out of the bottleneck, by the transpose of that same transformation -- the very same directions, read back out -- attempting to reconstruct the original long list. *Third*, it passes the result through a single nonlinearity -- a *rectified linear unit*, which is merely the operation “keep positive values as they are, and clamp negative values to zero.” And the model is trained, by the usual gradient descent, to make its reconstruction match the original feature-list as closely as it can, with errors on each feature weighted by that feature’s assigned importance.

That is the entire toy. Manufacture features of known importance and known sparsity; squeeze them through a bottleneck too small to hold them straight; expand and reconstruct; penalise the reconstruction errors. The reason for this exact design is that we can *open the bottleneck and look* -- inspect, directly and completely, *how the model chose to lay its features down in the cramped space*. We can ask, for each feature, which direction in the bottleneck the model assigned to it, and how those directions sit relative to one another. There is no mystery the toy can hide from us; it is small enough that we may know it entire. And what we find, as we turn the sparsity knob from dense to sparse, is a clean and decisive answer to our first question.

## Part Three -- The Phase Transition

Turn the sparsity knob to *dense* -- make the features usually present, usually crowding in all at once -- and the model makes a particular choice, and it is the *conservative* one. It selects the *most important* features and assigns each of them its own clean, perpendicular direction in the bottleneck; and it simply *throws away* the rest -- the less important features get no direction at all, are not represented, are abandoned. The model packs in as many features as it has dimensions, orthogonally, in order of importance, and discards the overflow. There is *no superposition*. Each represented feature sits in its own private direction, uncontaminated, and the unrepresented features are written off as a loss.

This behaviour has a name you will know, sir, and it is worth naming because the correspondence is illuminating -- indeed, for the *linear* cousin of our toy, with the output nonlinearity removed, it is an exact theorem: in the dense regime, the model is doing **principal component analysis**. It is keeping the few most significant directions of variation and discarding the rest -- exactly the dimensionality-reduction that principal component analysis performs. And the *reason* it behaves so conservatively when features are dense is the one we anticipated: when features are usually present *together*, any overlap between their directions would cause constant, ruinous interference -- every feature’s readout perpetually corrupted by all the others crowding in at the same moment. With dense features, superposition is simply not worth it; the interference cost is paid on every example. So the model declines to fold, keeps what it can hold straight, and abandons the rest.

Now turn the knob the other way -- toward *sparse*, toward features that are usually *absent*, that only rarely appear and almost never appear two-at-once. And the model’s behaviour changes, and it changes *abruptly*. Past a certain point, the model *stops* discarding features and instead begins to *fold them together* -- representing *far more* features than the bottleneck has dimensions, packing them into overlapping, nearly-perpendicular directions. It enters *superposition*. And -- this is the striking part -- it will represent even the *unimportant* features this way, the ones it would have discarded outright in the dense regime. Sparsity has changed the calculus entirely.

Here is *why*, and it is worth getting exactly, because it is the whole secret. The cost of folding two features into overlapping directions is *interference* -- but interference only *bites* when both features are *present at the same time*, because only then do their overlapping directions actually collide and corrupt each other. If the two features are sparse -- if each is rarely present, and so the two are *almost never present together* -- then the collision *almost never happens*. The model gets to enjoy the benefit of representing both features, for the price of an interference that is only paid on the rare occasions both happen to be active at once. Sparsity makes superposition *cheap*, because it makes collisions *rare*. The rarer the features, the more of them the model can safely cram in, because the more confident it can be that any given pair will not show up together to interfere. Superposition is not a bug, not a failure of capacity -- it is the model’s *optimal strategy* for storing many rare things in a small space, and it kicks in precisely when the things become rare enough that the strategy pays.

The full bookkeeping is worth a moment more, because the researchers did not stop at a single knob; they mapped the *whole territory*. For each feature, one can chart its fate as a function of both knobs at once -- its sparsity along one axis, its importance along the other -- and the resulting map has the unmistakable look of a *phase diagram*, the kind a metallurgist keeps pinned over the bench. Three territories, with sharp borders. In one region -- dense and unimportant -- the feature is simply *not represented*: abandoned, as we saw. In a second -- important enough to earn it -- the feature is given a *dedicated dimension* of its very own, perpendicular and private. And in the third -- sparse enough for the economics to work -- the feature is admitted into *shared accommodation*: represented, but in superposition, its direction overlapping its neighbours’. Three phases of representational matter, if you will permit the physics: absent, crystalline, and folded -- with transitions between them as sharp as any in a textbook.

I want to stress that *sharpness*, because it echoes a theme from last episode and because it tells us something about how these models are organised. The shift between regimes is not a gentle ramp; it is an abrupt *phase transition*, with a recognisable *before* and *after*. Below a threshold of sparsity: clean directions, discarded overflow, principal component analysis. Above it: folded directions, everything represented, superposition. The model changes its fundamental storage strategy at a critical value, the way water, cooled, does not gradually stiffen but *freezes* at a particular temperature. You met a sudden reorganisation last episode in the induction bump and in the grokking model’s long-deferred epiphany; here is another, in the geometry of representation itself. These models are full of such thresholds, and a good deal of the field’s recent excitement is about finding and understanding them.

So our first question is answered, and answered precisely: a model resorts to superposition when its features are *sparse enough*, because sparsity makes the interference cost of folding rare enough to be worth paying for the benefit of representing more. And real features, as I argued, are *extremely* sparse -- which is exactly why real models are drenched in superposition, and why our work is hard.

## Part Four -- The Geometry of the Fold

Now the second question -- *what structure* the folded features take -- and here the toy delivers something I genuinely did not expect the first time I encountered it, and which I think will please you, sir, given your physics.

When the model enters superposition and lays its overlapping features into the cramped bottleneck, it does not arrange them haphazardly. It arranges them into *strikingly regular geometric configurations* -- the directions distribute themselves with the orderliness of a crystal. The simplest case, when two features must share a single dimension, is what the field calls an **antipodal pair**: the model points the two features in *exactly opposite* directions -- one along a line, the other directly against it. They share the dimension by pointing the two opposite ways down it, which is the arrangement that keeps them maximally distinguishable -- and the reason the two do not simply cancel each other is the toy’s final clamp: each feature’s readout looks only for *positive* extent along its own arrow, and the rectifier zeroes the negative shadow its room-mate would otherwise cast, so each tenant reads his own side of the room and never the other’s. And as you give the model more features to fit into a small space, the configurations climb a ladder of regular shapes: three features arrange themselves into an equilateral *triangle*, their directions equally spaced around a circle; further features settle into *pentagons*, into three-dimensional figures like *tetrahedra*, into a whole zoo of uniform polytopes -- the most symmetrical arrangements available in whatever number of dimensions the bottleneck affords.

Why such regularity? Because the model is, whether it knows it or not, *solving a packing problem* -- and the regular shapes are the solutions. Consider what the model wants. It wants to fit many feature-directions into a space too small for them to be perpendicular, while keeping the *interference between any two as small as possible* -- and interference between two directions is governed by how *aligned* they are, by the angle between them. So the model wants to arrange its directions so that *every pair is as close to perpendicular as the cramped space allows* -- to spread them out so that no two point too nearly the same way. It is seeking the arrangement of directions that *maximises the minimum angle* between them -- that spreads them as evenly as possible over the available space.

And *this*, sir, is a problem you have very likely met in another guise. It is, in essence, the **Thomson problem** -- the question of how to place a number of mutually repelling charges on the surface of a sphere so that they settle into the lowest-energy configuration. Like charges push apart; they seek to be as far from one another as possible; and they settle, when they reach equilibrium, into exactly these regular distributions over the sphere -- the antipodal pair, the triangle, the tetrahedron, the same family of symmetrical figures. The model’s features, repelling one another through the interference cost, find their lowest-energy arrangement by the same logic that governs charges on a sphere. The model is doing electrostatics it was never taught, minimising an energy it was never told about, and arriving at the configurations a physicist would recognise on sight. I find that a rather lovely thing -- that gradient descent, grinding away at reconstruction error, should rediscover the geometry of repelling charges. The crystalline order in the bottleneck is not decoration; it is the visible shape of an energy being minimised.

I should add one refinement, because it matters and because it complicates the pretty picture in an instructive way. The arrangement is not *perfectly* uniform, because the features are not *equally important*. Importance *warps the geometry*: a more important feature claims more room for itself -- a direction more nearly perpendicular to all the others, better protected from interference -- while less important features are crowded into the leftover angles, made to tolerate more overlap. So the true picture is of charges on a sphere that *differ in magnitude*, the larger ones commandeering more space and pushing the smaller ones into the gaps. The geometry is regular, but it is regular *as weighted by importance* -- a crystal grown under an external field that pulls it out of true. The model spends its scarce perpendicularity where it matters most, and economises where it can afford to.

And there is a second refinement, which the toy reveals when one stops treating the features as strangers to one another and starts giving them *social lives*. Real features are not statistically independent: some are *correlated* -- “this is Python code” and “this is a variable name” keep close company -- while others are *anticorrelated*, scarcely ever found in the same room -- “this text is formal legal boilerplate” and “this text is a toddler’s bedtime story,” say. Endow the toy’s features with such relationships, and the model’s seating arrangements respond with the shrewdness of an experienced hostess. *Anticorrelated* features are the ideal candidates for sharing: since they never arrive together, their interference is never actually realised, and the model places them with perfect cheerfulness into the tightest accommodation available -- the antipodal pair, two guests assigned one room on the well-founded expectation that they will never both be in it. *Correlated* features are the dangerous pairings -- they *do* arrive together, constantly, so any overlap between them would be paid for on every visit -- and the model accordingly spends its precious perpendicularity on exactly them, arranging frequently co-occurring features into locally orthogonal little sets, clean right angles purchased where the collisions would otherwise be commonest. Push the correlation to its extreme -- features that essentially always appear together -- and the model may give up distinguishing them at all, collapsing the pair into a single joint direction, one concept where the world supplied two. The general principle is worth stating, because it governs real models too: superposition is rationed by *collision risk*. The model’s geometry is, at bottom, an actuarial table -- and the premiums are set by co-occurrence.

## Part Five -- Memorisation, Generalisation, and the Double Descent

Before we leave the toy, one further interrogation, told briskly here because the desk carries it in full. Everything so far assumed an unlimited diet of fresh examples. Ration it -- train the same toy on a *fixed* dataset, tiny to vast -- and the bottleneck’s tenancy changes in kind. Given plenty, the toy stores the true generating *features*, folded and crystalline, and generalises. Given a handful of examples seen over and over, it stores *the training examples themselves*, each datapoint assigned its own direction in superposition -- the whole dataset pressed like flowers; train loss superb, test loss dreadful. And at intermediate rations, caught between strategies -- too many examples to memorise comfortably, too few to pin down the features -- its test error *peaks*: worse than the pure memoriser, worse than the pure generaliser. You will recognise that silhouette, sir: it is **double descent**, and the toy offers the phenomenon something it had largely lacked, a mechanistic reading of the interior hump as the no-man’s-land between representational strategies. The deepest point survives every compression, and I would have you carry it: in the bottleneck’s geometry, a *memorised example* and a *learned feature* are the same kind of object -- a direction, in superposition, earning its keep by reducing loss. Memory and understanding are not two faculties but one storage medium with two kinds of tenant, and the statistics of the diet set the portfolio. When it is next debated in your hearing whether a model “really understands or merely memorises,” you may hear the question for what it is: not a dichotomy but an allocation, continuously rebalanced according to what the data will pay.

## Part Six -- Features That Are Not Arrows: The Days of the Week

I have now twice promised -- once in the first episode, again in this one -- to show you features for which the word “direction” is the wrong word, and the moment has come to pay. This part is the strangest in the episode, and I would ask you to hold it with both the delight it deserves and the unease it ought to cause, because it bears directly on whether our central hypothesis is *true* or merely *useful*.

Consider the days of the week. Seven concepts -- but not seven *separate* concepts, like seven unrelated nouns. They come with structure: an order, and worse, a *cyclic* order. Tuesday follows Monday; Sunday is succeeded by Monday again; the sequence has no end and no beginning, only a perpetual rotation. Now ask: how should a model represent such a family? Seven independent directions would store the days but lose the *wheel* -- nothing in seven arbitrary arrows says that Saturday is adjacent to both Friday and Sunday. A single line -- the days laid out along one direction by number -- captures the order but breaks at the weekend, leaving Sunday stranded at one end, infinitely far from the Monday it touches. There is exactly one shape that respects the structure: a *circle*. And when researchers went looking inside real language models -- armed with the sparse autoencoder we build next episode, and with clustering methods tuned to find features that travel in groups -- a circle is what they found. The days of the week, laid out in order around a closed loop, occupying a small flat patch of the residual stream: not seven arrows, but one *ring*. The months of the year, likewise, were found arranged on a ring of twelve. The model, never instructed in the topology of the calendar, had inferred it from usage and built the appropriate shape.

And the finding was not left at the level of a pretty picture, because pretty pictures, as this course keeps insisting, are where rigour goes to die. The researchers intervened. They set the model arithmetic problems on the calendar -- *today is Monday; what day will it be in two days?* -- and then reached into the activations and *seized the circle*: they replaced the hidden state’s coordinates in the discovered two-dimensional subspace with another day’s position on the ring -- averaging the rest of the layer away -- so that Monday became Thursday in the circle, and only in the circle. The model’s answers moved around the ring to match, and the circle alone did nearly all the work that patching the entire layer would have done. The ring is not an artefact of our visualisation; it is the working representation, causally implicated in calendrical reasoning -- the model genuinely *computes with the wheel*. There is a pleasing echo here of an old acquaintance: you will recall, from the grokking story, the modular-arithmetic model that reinvented addition as rotation around circles; and further back still, the curve detectors of the vision anatomy, a family of features stepping smoothly around the compass of orientations. The circle, it turns out, was never an exotic guest in these systems. Wherever the underlying concept is cyclic, the model is inclined to build the cycle.

Now the unease, in measured dose. The field calls such objects **multi-dimensional features**, or speaks of features living on a *manifold* -- a curved or closed surface -- rather than along a direction; and their existence is a documented amendment to the linear representation hypothesis. The hypothesis does not collapse: a circle, note, is still a profoundly *low-dimensional* and *geometrically regular* object, locally well-approximated by directions, and the instruments built on the linear picture did, in fact, find it. But the amendment is real, and its honest reading is this: *the model’s ontology of representations is richer than our leading hypothesis, and we do not yet know how much richer.* There is a respectable minority position in the field which holds that the strong form of the feature hypothesis -- everything the model represents, decomposable into a discrete dictionary of directions -- may simply be wrong, with directions as the legible *fragment* of a representational scheme we have only partially charted. I do not adjudicate the dispute; I report it, and I file it where this course files all such things -- with the limitations of Episode One, under the heading of ignorance whose shape we at least now know. When we build the microscope next episode, remember the days of the week: the instrument assumes arrows, and some of what it is pointed at is wheels.

## Part Seven -- Computation Inside the Fold

Now the unsettling part, which I promised not to soften, and which is the most important single idea in this episode for understanding the *limits* of the instruments we are about to build.

Everything I have said so far concerns *storage*: how the model crams many features into a small space. You might reasonably hope that superposition is *only* a storage trick -- that the model folds features together merely to *hold* them, and that whenever it actually needs to *use* a feature -- to compute something with it -- it must first *unfold* it, retrieve it to some clean dedicated direction, operate on it there, and refold it. If that were so, our task would be merely difficult: build an instrument that detects the unfolding, catch the features at the moment they are used, and we should see everything the model does. The fold would be a filing inconvenience, not a barrier to understanding.

It is not so. And the toy models show us it is not so, which is one of the more sobering things they show us. A model can perform genuine *computation* upon features *while they remain in superposition* -- never unfolding them, operating on the folded representation directly. Researchers have demonstrated models computing real nonlinear functions on superposed inputs: detecting whether a particular *combination* of features is jointly present, computing things like the *absolute value* of a folded quantity -- and on the theory side one may construct even the *exclusive-or* of two superposed features -- that tricky operation, true when exactly one of its two inputs is true, which cannot be computed by any simple linear readout and yet which the construction manages *without* ever pulling its inputs apart into clean directions. Indeed the theoretical work runs further still: it has been shown that a layer can, in principle, compute *many more* simple logical functions than it has neurons, the computations themselves packed into shared hardware just as the features are packed into shared directions -- compression not only of the warehouse, sir, but of the workshop. The features are used while folded, transformed while folded, combined while folded.

Sit with what this implies for our project, because it is a genuine and permanent limitation rather than a temporary one. Suppose we build the perfect instrument -- a flawless catalogue of every feature the model represents, every direction in its folded notebook correctly identified and named. We shall attempt exactly this in the next episode. Even granting us total success at *that*, we might *still* fail to understand what the model is *doing* -- because the *computation* may be happening among the folded features in a way that our catalogue of features, however perfect, does not capture. Knowing the complete list of ingredients does not tell you the recipe, and knowing the complete list of features does not tell you the computation performed upon them. The fold is not merely where the model *stores* its meanings; it is, at least sometimes, where the model *thinks*. And a microscope that resolves the storage may still be blind to the thinking.

I lay this down now, before we build the microscope, so that when we build it you will hold it with appropriate skepticism -- will see it for the powerful-but-partial instrument it is, rather than mistaking it for a solution to the whole problem. The honest valet shows the flaw in the cloth before the sale, not after. Computation in superposition is the flaw in the cloth, and I would have you see it in good light.

## Part Eight -- The Privileged Basis, the Neuron, and the Bridge to the Microscope

There remains one matter to settle before we can build, and it resolves a loose end I have left dangling since the second episode -- a promissory note I made and have not paid. It will also explain *why the word “neuron” still means anything at all* in these models, and it will deliver us precisely to the doorstep of the next episode.

Recall, from the second episode, the claim that the residual stream has *no privileged basis* -- that you could rotate its coordinate axes to any orientation without changing the model’s behaviour, because every head reads and writes the stream through transformations that would rotate right along with it. The rotation washes out; no orientation is special; and therefore features are free to point in *any* direction, with no reason to align to the coordinate axes. That was the argument, and it stands. The residual stream is, in the relevant sense, *isotropic* -- the same in every direction, playing no favourites among orientations.

But I flagged, and set aside for this episode, that *one part of the model is different*. That part is the *interior of the feed-forward clerk* -- the hidden layer inside the feed-forward block, the sealed-room specialist from the second episode. And the thing that makes it different is the *nonlinearity* it applies, the same rectified-linear-unit operation our toy used: the operation “keep positives, clamp negatives to zero.” Here is the crucial property of that operation: it acts *on each coordinate separately*. It looks at each individual number in the list and decides, number by number, whether to keep it or zero it. And an operation that treats the coordinates *individually* is an operation that *cares which directions are the coordinate directions* -- because if you rotated the axes, the operation “clamp each coordinate” would now clamp *different things*, and the model’s behaviour *would* change. The rotation no longer washes out. The per-coordinate nonlinearity *breaks the rotational symmetry* and *singles out the coordinate axes as special*. The feed-forward hidden layer, unlike the residual stream, *has* a privileged basis.

And *this* is why the word “neuron” survives. A neuron is, precisely, *one coordinate of that privileged basis* -- one of the individual numbers the nonlinearity acts upon. Because the basis is privileged there, the individual coordinates -- the neurons -- are *not* arbitrary; they are the directions the model’s own nonlinearity singles out, and so they carry *some* genuine meaning. Look at a single neuron in the feed-forward layer and you will often find it responding to *something* recognisable. This is why the earliest interpretability work, before this whole apparatus was understood, could make any progress at all by staring at individual neurons: in that one privileged corner of the model, the coordinates mean something.

But -- and here is the bridge, the exact place where this episode hands off to the next -- the neurons are *not cleanly* meaningful, and the reason is the very superposition we have spent the episode dissecting. Because the model has *more features than it has neurons*, even this privileged basis is forced into superposition: the model packs *multiple unrelated features into each neuron*. And so when you examine a real neuron, you find it firing not on one clean concept but on a *baffling assortment* -- the same single neuron responding to, say, academic citations *and* web-request boilerplate *and* text in the Korean script, three things with no earthly relation to one another, jammed into one coordinate because the model had more features than coordinates to hold them. The field calls such a neuron **polysemantic** -- many-meaninged -- and polysemanticity is the direct, visible fingerprint of superposition pressing even on the privileged basis. Nor is it a quirk of language models: the vision anatomists of our first episode, working through their image classifier organ by organ, kept finding the same affliction -- one celebrated unit responded to the faces of cats *and* the fronts of cars *and* the legs of cats, a portmanteau no designer would countenance and no training process minded in the least. The neuron is a *somewhat* meaningful handle, but a *hopelessly entangled* one: you cannot understand the model by reading its neurons, because each neuron is a tangle of several features at once.

I must also tell you about the road not taken, because the field tried it, the trial was instructive, and its failure is the final argument for the instrument we build next episode. Confronted with polysemanticity, a natural engineering impulse arises: if superposition is the disease, change the architecture so the disease cannot take hold -- design an activation function that *rewards* the model for keeping one feature per neuron, and train models that are interpretable *by construction*. The experiment was run, in earnest, with an activation designed to encourage exactly such monosemanticity. And at first it appeared to work: many more neurons in the resulting models responded to single, nameable things, and there was a brief season of optimism. Then the accounting was completed. The architecture had not *eliminated* superposition; it had *relocated* it. The pressure that drives the folding -- more features worth representing than dimensions to hold them -- was untouched by the new activation, and the model, still subject to that pressure, had learned to hide its folded features where the new activation’s incentives did not reach, in configurations the cheerful clean neurons sat atop like a respectable shopfront over a busy cellar. The interpretability gained in the visible neurons was partly an illusion, paid for by features driven into deeper obscurity. The lesson deserves italics, sir: *superposition is not an architectural accident to be legislated away; it is an economic response to scarcity, and it will reappear, in disguise if necessary, wherever the scarcity persists.* If we cannot prevent the folding, there is only one road left -- accept the model as it is, folded and entangled, and build an *external* instrument to unfold it after the fact.

And there is the precise statement of the problem the next episode exists to solve. We have features we want to read. They are folded into superposition throughout the residual stream, pointing in arbitrary directions -- when they are not, indeed, bent into rings. Even in the one corner with a privileged basis -- the neurons -- superposition has tangled multiple features into each coordinate, leaving us polysemantic handles rather than clean ones. And the one attempt to forbid the folding by architectural decree was quietly outmanoeuvred by the very pressure it sought to forbid. We cannot read the features by reading the dimensions, we cannot read them by reading the neurons, and we cannot train the problem away. We need an instrument that takes the folded, tangled, superposed representation and *recovers the original clean features* -- un-folds the fold, dis-entangles the tangle, and hands us back the monosemantic directions the model is really using. That instrument is the *sparse autoencoder*, and building it properly -- building it, breaking it, and being honest about it -- is the whole of our next episode.

## Part Nine -- The Engineer’s Dividend

This episode has been the most theoretical of the series, and you might suppose, sir, that a toy autoencoder and a phase diagram have little to say to a man with production traffic. I would suppose otherwise, and I shall make the case in four items. A caveat first, in the interest of the honesty this course trades on: the first item or two below rest on reasoning the mechanism *affords* rather than on results the literature has nailed down -- mechanistically informed hypotheses, not settled fact. I shall flag which is which; the value of a mechanism, after all, lies partly in the quality of the guesses it lets one make.

*First -- quantisation, and why its damage is uneven.* You will have observed the practitioner’s folklore: a model quantised to fewer bits sails through the headline benchmarks and then turns out, in production, to have quietly lost its grip on some narrow capability -- a rare language, an obscure domain, a delicate format. The superposition picture offers a reading. Every folded feature lives on a *margin* -- the gap between its signal and the interference floor of its overlapping neighbours -- and the phase diagram told us the margins are not distributed equally: important, common features were bought generous protection; the sparse long tail was crammed into the leftover angles, its margins thin by design. Quantisation is, mechanically, the addition of coordinate-wise noise -- a general raising of the interference floor. And when the floor rises, it is the thin-margined tenants who drown first: the rare features, which is to say the rare capabilities. The headline benchmark, dominated by common features with fat margins, registers nothing. The moral is operational and cheap to act on: *evaluate quantised models on their tails, not their means* -- the mechanism predicts that is precisely where the bodies are.

*Second -- distillation and small models, read off the phase diagram.* A small model is a small bottleneck, and the phase diagram is, quite literally, a schedule of what a capacity-constrained model will keep: the important-and-dense get private dimensions, the moderately useful get shared accommodation, and the long tail of the least valuable features gets evicted entirely. This is a mechanistic gloss on the characteristic personality of distilled and small models -- fluent on the common, fragile on the rare, their failures concentrated on precisely the niche entities and minority languages whose features sat past the representational means test. When you choose a smaller model for a workload, you are not buying a uniformly noisier copy of the large one; you are buying a different settlement of the same phase diagram, and the difference is concentrated in the tail.

*Third -- fine-tuning, forgetting, and the re-priced actuarial table.* I called the model’s geometry an actuarial table priced by co-occurrence; note, then, what fine-tuning does. Training on a narrow distribution *changes the statistics* -- features common in your fine-tuning data become dense and important; features that served the old, broad distribution become, by the new accounting, rare and dispensable -- and gradient descent, ever the dutiful actuary, re-prices the table. Dimensions are scarce real estate, and the old tenants’ leases are not protected. There, in one image, is a mechanistic gloss on catastrophic forgetting and on the regressions that haunt every fine-tuning pipeline, your own included, sir: the capability you lost was not deleted out of spite; it was *outbid*. The same picture rationalises the folk wisdom that gentle, low-rank, small-learning-rate adaptations forget less -- a tenant who may only redecorate cannot evict -- though I flag this paragraph, per my caveat, as mechanism-flavoured reasoning rather than theorem.

*Fourth -- and this one is firmly established -- superposition sets the agenda for every interpretability tool you will ever deploy.* If you take one operational sentence from this episode, let it be this: *the model’s internals are not organised for your convenience, and any tool that reads activations coordinate-by-coordinate -- neuron dashboards, naive probing of individual dimensions, the visualisation your vendor is proudest of -- is reading polysemantic tangles and will mislead you with confidence.* The respectable instruments are the ones built in knowledge of the fold: probes trained on directions, dictionaries learned by sparsity, the autoencoder of the next episode. Superposition is why those instruments must exist, and knowing it is what will let you read their output -- and their failure modes -- like an engineer rather than a tourist.

## Closing -- What We Have Earned, and Where It Points

Let us gather the episode.

We made superposition rigorous by *building* it -- a toy model small enough to understand entire, given features of known **importance** and **sparsity**, squeezed through a bottleneck too small to hold them straight. And the toy answered our questions cleanly.

*When* does the model fold? When features are *sparse enough*: dense features make overlap ruinously costly on every example (the model performs importance-ranked principal component analysis and discards the overflow), while sparsity makes collisions rare enough that folding pays -- and the full map over both knobs is a genuine *phase diagram*, its three territories (absent, crystalline, folded) separated by sharp borders. Real features being extremely sparse, real models are saturated with superposition. *What structure?* Crystalline packings -- antipodal pairs, triangles, tetrahedra -- because the model is spreading directions to maximise the minimum angle, exactly as repelling charges settle in the **Thomson problem**; importance warps the crystal, and correlation prices the rooms: anticorrelated features billeted together, correlated ones bought clean right angles, superposition rationed throughout by *collision risk*. Not every feature is an arrow -- the days of the week live on a causally functional *circle*, a documented amendment to the linear hypothesis. The toy adjudicated memory against understanding as one medium with two tenants, giving **double descent** its mechanistic gloss. The model can *compute* on folded features without ever unpacking them -- the workshop compressed along with the warehouse -- so a perfect feature catalogue may still miss the computation. And the second episode’s debt is paid: the feed-forward interior, alone in the model, has a *privileged basis* -- its per-coordinate rectifier singles out the axes, which is why neurons mean anything -- yet superposition tangles several features into each, rendering them **polysemantic**, and the architectural attempt to forbid the fold merely taught it to hide. We cannot read the dimensions, we cannot read the neurons, and we cannot train the problem away.

Which leaves us needing an instrument that can take the folded, tangled, superposed representation and recover the clean, monosemantic features the model is actually using. In our next episode we build that instrument -- the **sparse autoencoder** -- and I shall do it in the manner I think it deserves: not as a triumphant unveiling, but as an honest commissioning. We shall build the microscope, hold it up to the light, and I shall show you, candidly, every smudge on the lens -- the features it underestimates, the features it kills, the features it splits in two, the deep and unresolved problem that we have *no ground truth* against which to check whether what it shows us is really there -- and a recent and rather bracing dispute over whether the instrument earns its keep at all. A microscope is only as trustworthy as your knowledge of its distortions, and I intend you to know them. I shall have it ready.

Until then, sir.
