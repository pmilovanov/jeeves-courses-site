Chapter 15The Briefs

This chapter gathers the thirteen Briefs — one review sheet per teaching chapter, the twelve of the season and the supplementary sitting — so that the whole course may be reviewed at one sitting, from the back. Nothing here is new: every line is proved, drawn, or drilled in the chapter it summarizes, and its references — definitions, theorems, boxes, exhibits, problems, and the chapter’s own Parts — lead back to that machinery rather than standing in for it. Each Brief opens with its chapter’s thesis in a single sentence and closes with the sharp edges: the traps, the boundary conditions, and the classic confusions better met here than across a table from someone. The Briefs speak the field’s own language. The volume’s spoken names and its cast, standing vocabulary everywhere else in these pages, are absent here by design, so that a Brief may be read by someone who has not read the volume — or by its author a year hence, on the evening before an interview.

15.1 Chapter 1 — The Trouble with Other Computers

The one sentence.  Distributing a system forfeits shared memory, shared fate, and a shared clock all at once, leaving processes that cooperate only by messages over a network that may delay, lose, duplicate, and reorder them — so every claim about such a system is conditional on a stated model, and the chapter’s theorem, Two Generals, shows by an indistinguishability argument that guaranteed coordinated action over a link that may lose messages is impossible even with synchronous rounds and no process failures.

The model on three axes.  A claim quoted without its model is not a fact. The course default, fixed in this chapter’s model box: asynchronous timing, crash-stop processes, fair-loss links, wherever a chapter does not declare otherwise.

  • Timing (Def. 1.2). Synchronous: known finite bounds on process speed and message delay always hold (equivalently, numbered rounds), so a timeout is never wrong. Asynchronous: no bound on either, so a slow process and a crashed one are indistinguishable (Def. 1.1) and the sharpest impossibilities apply. Partially synchronous (Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer, 1988): bounds exist but are unknown, or hold only after an unknown global stabilisation time (GST) — the model of a real network, and the one in which consensus becomes achievable (Chapters 4–5). An impossibility proved in the synchronous model is the strongest kind, since it survives every relaxation; a possibility proved there is the most suspect.

  • Process failure (Def. 1.3), in increasing order of malice, each subsuming the milder. Crash-stop: halts permanently, never emits anything false. Crash-recovery: restarts having lost volatile state but kept stable storage (Paxos’s persistence clause, Chapter 4; Problem 1.8). Omission: drops messages it should have sent or received; the least used. Byzantine (Lamport, Shostak, and Pease, 1982): deviates arbitrarily — bugs, corruption, compromise, malice — and is the dearest to tolerate (Chapter 5).

  • Links: the fair-loss model and the two links built on it, below.

The fair-loss link model.  The network may delay, lose, duplicate, and reorder any message; it may never forge one. Formally (Def. 1.4): FL1, fair loss — a message sent infinitely often to a correct receiver is delivered infinitely often; FL2, finite duplication; FL3, no creation — every delivered message was sent by its named sender, the one guarantee the model keeps and the one Chapter 5 revokes. Two qualifications. A sender that retries on timeout, above a network that only loses and delays, manufactures duplication and reordering itself, so the model describes the system you build, not only the wire you bought (Problem 1.6). And TCP is no counterexample: its sequence numbers, retransmission, and deduplication hold within one connection while it lives, and every connection break re-asks the field’s oldest question, whether the last message arrived.

Perfect links (Problem 1.7, solved in full as Box 3): the sender numbers each message and retransmits it until acknowledged; the receiver acknowledges every arrival and delivers each number only the first time it sees it. PL1 holds even if no acknowledgment ever arrives, since unacknowledged data is retransmitted forever and FL1 delivers it, so acknowledgments buy economy only — retransmission stops — never the guarantee. The receiver’s set of seen numbers grows forever, and deduplication memory stays bounded only if the network’s redelivery lifetime is bounded — any rule that discards an identifier the network may still redeliver reopens the double application — a purchase of model, not of cleverness (Problem 1.10; Chapter 10’s deduplication window).

The naive replicated counter.  Box 1: on a client increment a replica adds one and sends the increment to the other replicas; on delivering an increment it adds one. No identifiers, no acknowledgments, no retransmission, no ordering — each absence a separate failure, with no machine failing in any of them. Loss: one propagation is lost and the replicas diverge silently, each locally consistent (Exhibit 1.2). Duplication by retry: the client’s request is delayed, not lost; the retry and the original both arrive, and every replica converges, unanimously, on one order counted twice, nobody misbehaving (Exhibit 1.3; Problem 1.4 drills a duplicate made by the network). Deduplication by identifier is the cure, kept carelessly in Problem 1.8. Reordering: two register writes reach one replica in the other order, every message delivered exactly once, and the copies disagree — for register state order is everything, and “which write was last” does not yet mean anything without a shared clock (Chapter 2); the counter survived only because increments commute, and commuting operations are insensitive to delivery order (Chapters 7 and 12). When one replica says one and another nought there is no master copy to consult: an authority must be appointed and must survive its own crash, the network’s interference with the election, and two processes each believing itself appointed — consensus, Chapters 3–5.

The Two Generals theorem.  The model, deliberately more generous than the course default so that loss alone is to blame: two processes $A$ and $B$, synchronous rounds, no process failures, an adversary that may suppress any subset of messages, and an input at $A$, attack or abstain. Coordinated Attack (Def. 1.7) requires, in every execution, Termination (both decide), Agreement (the same value), and Validity (input abstain: both abstain; input attack and no loss: both attack — without it, always abstaining would do). Thm. 1.1 (Akkoyunlu, Ekanadham, and Huber, 1975; named and tied to transaction commit by Gray, 1978): no deterministic protocol solves it. The proof. In the lossless execution with input attack, both decide attack after finitely many messages; suppress those messages one at a time, from the last backwards. At each step the sender of the removed message receives exactly what it received before, so its view is unchanged and it decides attack as before, and Agreement forces its partner to attack too; after the last step both decide attack in an execution delivering nothing. Process $B$ has the same view — no input, nothing delivered — in the execution with input abstain and nothing delivered, so it decides attack there, but Validity requires abstain: contradiction. Told as a minimal counterexample, the last message of a minimal correct protocol’s best-case execution is either decoration (drop it) or pivotal (lose it, and its sender attacks alone); Exhibit 1.4 draws one such step. Termination bought the finite list, Agreement carried the decision along the chain, and Validity anchored both ends — an audit that tells which weakening of the problem might escape the theorem.

What the theorem forbids and what it permits.  Rem. 1.9: what is forbidden is guaranteed common action, over unreliable delivery, by a deadline — certainty, not usefulness. With messengers captured independently with probability $p$, sending $n$ of them and attacking unconditionally leaves a solitary-attack risk of exactly $p^n$, below one in a million at $p = 1/2$ once $n = 20$; demanding an acknowledgment only moves the risk, and every protocol here either never attacks or has positive probability of disagreement (Problem 1.9). If the adversary may suppress at most a known $f$ messages in all, $f{+}1$ messages suffice and $f$ do not, while $f$ per round over unbounded rounds restores the impossibility — it is not the quantity of loss that kills but its inexhaustibility (Problem 1.12). “Both eventually act” is a change of specification, not a loophole. Epistemically (Rem. 1.10), acting safely requires common knowledge — each knows, knows the other knows, and so on without end — which Halpern and Moses (1990) proved no finite exchange over unreliable channels can create (Problem 1.11). Two-phase commit inherits the theorem in Chapter 8, paying in blocking.

The indistinguishability argument.  Def. 1.1: a process’s view is the sequence of its own inputs and delivered messages; two executions are indistinguishable to it when its view is identical in both, and a deterministic process then behaves identically in both. The proof analyzed no strategies; it exhibited two executions some process cannot tell apart in which correctness demands different behaviour. Introduced here, the argument proves FLP and CAP (Chapter 3), the Byzantine fault bound (Chapter 5), and the blocking of two-phase commit (Chapter 8); crashed-versus-slow is its standing instance.

Sharp edges. 

  • four causes of one timeout: dead server, slow server, lost request, and reply lost after the work was done are identical to the client when the timeout fires (Problem 1.5); the retry that cures the lost request executes the operation twice when it was the reply that was lost, and only idempotence dissolves the difference (Problem 1.10, Chapter 10).

  • deduplication state must be exactly as durable as the effect it guards: a replica that writes its counter to disk but keeps the per-client highest applied sequence number in memory double-applies after a crash — the acknowledgment is lost, recovery restores an empty table, and the retry’s number exceeds it (Problem 1.8); record and effect must be persisted in one atomic write, since record before effect loses the increment instead — the transaction in miniature (Chapter 8).

  • partitions are routine, and redundancy fails together: the published record (Bailis and Kingsbury, 2014) is measured fact, not pessimism, and redundant components repeatedly share a failure domain — redundant on the diagram, coupled in the wiring closet.

  • theory forbids what practice does daily: the reconciliation lives in one of three places — the model differs (the impossibility was proved for a harsher world), the guarantee differs (probability, described as certainty), or the price was paid where you were not looking (availability during partitions, latency on every write, a lease, an operator on call). Naming which is Part Seven’s classification.

  • perfect links do not order: PL1–PL3 say nothing about order; an ordered link is a stronger abstraction (Chapter 10).

15.2 Chapter 2 — Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events

The one sentence.  Among machines there is no master timeline: two events are ordered only where influence could have travelled from one to the other — along one process’s steps or one message’s flight — so “before” is the partial order happens-before, the clocks worth trusting are logical clocks (counters that track that order: the Lamport clock one-way, the vector clock exactly), and “the state now” is the state along a consistent cut, which the Chandy–Lamport snapshot records while the system keeps running.

Physical clocks, and why they cannot order events.  Quartz drifts by tens of parts per million — twenty is a second and three quarters per day — and worse under load. NTP disciplines a fleet from strata of servers under atomic clocks, but estimates a client’s offset by halving a measured round trip, assuming symmetric paths (a Chapter 1 fallacy), and congested or asymmetric routes bias it silently. A correcting daemon either slews the clock or steps it, and a step can move time backwards; the leap second of 30 June 2012 tripped a Linux kernel defect and healthy machines across the internet spun; Google’s leap smear spreads the extra second over a day, but smearing is local, so fleets on different smear schedules disagree by design. The moral has two layers. Engineering: cross-machine clock disagreement — milliseconds on a good day, unbounded on a bad one — exceeds the intervals it would need to order, so sorting cross-machine events by wall-clock timestamp is noise at fine grain. The finding: even perfect clocks would order two events a nanosecond apart on opposite sides of a datacentre, between which no influence could have passed — and the question actually asked is never “which happened first” but “could this have influenced that.” The question, not the watch, was at fault.

The two system models.  For the orders and the clocks (Defs. 2.2–2.8, Thms. 2.1–2.2): asynchronous message passing among deterministic processes $p_1, \dots, p_n$; no process failures; links promising only no creation — every delivered message was previously sent. Loss, duplication, and reordering are irrelevant, because happens-before is defined over the deliveries that actually occur; no wall clock appears anywhere. For the snapshot (Box 6, Thm. 2.4): strictly stronger channels, reliable and first-in-first-out — every message sent on a channel is delivered exactly once, in the order sent — and a strongly connected channel graph; buildable over fair-loss links by Chapter 1’s constructions plus per-channel sequence numbers, with the FIFO clause spent at exactly one step of the proof (Rem. 2.11). Failures are absent by design.

Happens-before and concurrency.  Only two ordering facts survive distribution: program order — within one process the order of steps is real — and the rule that a message is received after it is sent. Happens-before (Def. 2.2; Lamport 1978), written $a \to b$, is the smallest relation on the events of an execution (Def. 2.1) containing both and closed under transitivity: HB1, same process and $a$ earlier; HB2, $a$ the send of a message and $b$ its receive; HB3, transitivity. Equivalently, $a \to b$ iff a causal path of HB1 and HB2 steps runs from $a$ to $b$; every step moves strictly later in the execution, so the relation is a strict partial order. Its complement is concurrency (Def. 2.3): $a \parallel b$ iff neither $a \to b$ nor $b \to a$ — not “at the same time,” but no path of influence in either direction. Happens-before is potential causality, overstating influence perhaps but never understating it, so concurrency is not ignorance of the true order but the report that there is no true order. The relation is symmetric and not transitive: on the reference trace (Exhibit 2.1: three processes, eleven events named $b_i$, $t_i$, $g_i$ by process in program order, four messages; Problem 2.1), $b_2 \parallel g_1$ and $g_1 \parallel t_2$, yet $b_2 \to t_2$ (Exhibit 2.2 redraws the trace as the directed acyclic graph of happens-before).

The Lamport clock and the clock condition.  A logical clock (Def. 2.4) assigns every event a value from a totally ordered set, computed by each process from local information alone; it satisfies the clock condition if $a \to b$ implies $C(a) < C(b)$. The Lamport clock (Box 4; Def. 2.5) is one integer counter per process under three rules: increment before every event and stamp the event with the result; attach the current value to every message sent; on a receive, first raise the counter to the maximum of its own value and the message’s stamp, then increment (Exhibit 2.3; Problem 2.2). Thm. 2.1: the Lamport clock satisfies the clock condition, because every HB1 or HB2 step along a causal path strictly increases the stamp — a receive first raises its counter to at least the sender’s stamp — and a finite chain of strict increases ends strictly higher than it began. The converse fails (Rem. 2.6; Sharp edges): from $C(a) < C(b)$ the only inference is $\lnot(b \to a)$. Breaking ties by a fixed order on process names yields a strict total order $\Rightarrow$ extending $\to$ (Rem. 2.7) — manufactured, not discovered: arbitrary where causality is silent, consistent with every causal path. Lamport’s paper runs mutual exclusion on it without a coordinator, and Chapter 5’s replicated state machine is its generalization; every term, ballot, epoch, and fencing token from Chapter 4 onward is a Lamport clock under another name.

The vector clock and its characterization theorem.  One number cannot tell “ordered” from “concurrent”; to characterize causality from stamps alone, keep one counter per process. The vector clock (Box 5; Def. 2.8; Fidge 1988, Mattern 1989) keeps at process $p_i$ a vector $V$ of $n$ entries: on any event, increment your own entry $V[i]$ and stamp the event with the whole vector; attach the vector to every message; on a receive, merge entrywise by maximum and then increment your own entry — the increment after the merge being the convention the proof requires. $U < W$ iff $U \le W$ entrywise and $U \ne W$; $U \parallel W$ iff neither is below the other. Thm. 2.2: for distinct events, $a \to b$ iff $V(a) < V(b)$, hence $a \parallel b$ iff $V(a) \parallel V(b)$ (Problem 2.3). The engine is the ledger lemma, $V(e)[p] = |K_p(e)|$: the $p$-entry of an event’s vector counts exactly the events at $p$ in its causal past, because that past restricted to $p$ is a prefix of $p$'s history, and at a receive the union of the receiver’s prefix and the sender’s is the longer of them — exactly the entrywise maximum. The forward direction is then the Lamport argument entrywise; the converse reads the lemma backwards: $V(b)[p] \ge V(a)[p]$ means $b$'s past contains $p$'s first $V(a)[p]$ events, the last of which is $a$. So two writes with incomparable stamps are provably concurrent, and no protocol may silently pretend one superseded the other (Chapters 6–7). The vector cannot be compressed — any stamp characterizing causality among $n$ processes needs $n$ coordinates (Charron-Bost; Problem 2.11) — so the industry rations it (versions per key, retired members pruned).

Mature systems carry both instruments and confuse neither.

Cuts, consistent cuts, and global states.  A cut $K$ (Def. 2.9) is a set of events whose restriction to each process is a prefix; its frontier is the last event of $K$ at each process. $K$ is consistent iff every receive in $K$ has its matching send in $K$ — no effect without its cause — equivalently, iff it is closed under $\to$ (Problem 2.9). Exhibit 2.4: the cut through $b_2$, $t_3$, $g_1$ is consistent, with $m_2$ crossing it forward (sent inside, not yet received); the cut through $b_1$, $t_1$, $g_1$ is inconsistent, $t_1$ being the receive of $m_1$ while its send $b_2$ lies outside (Problem 2.5). The global state $S(K)$ of a consistent cut (Def. 2.10) comprises each process’s state after exactly its $K$-prefix and, for each channel $c$, its in-flight messages $L_c(K)$: sent on $c$ within $K$, not received within $K$. The snapshot problem: during a live execution, by exchanging ordinary messages, record process and channel states equal to $S(K)$ for some consistent cut $K$; the strong form adds that $S(K)$ is reachable from the state at which the protocol began, and the state at completion from $S(K)$. Consistent cuts are closed under union and intersection, so they form a lattice under $\subseteq$ (Problem 2.9), in which $\mathord{\downarrow} e$, the events that happen before $e$ together with $e$, is the least consistent cut containing $e$ — on the reference trace, $\mathord{\downarrow} g_2 = \{b_1, b_2, t_1, t_2, t_3, g_1, g_2\}$; the volume’s one structure carrying both joins and meets, Chapter 7’s join-semilattices carrying joins alone.

The Chandy–Lamport snapshot and its correctness.  The algorithm (Box 6; Chandy and Lamport 1985) uses one control message, the marker, and two rules: the initiator records its own state and immediately — before any further application send — sends a marker on every outgoing channel; a process receiving its first marker does the same at once, records that marker’s channel as empty, and for every other incoming channel records the channel’s state — the application messages arriving after its own recording and before that channel’s marker. The marker sweeps each channel: the snapshot’s past ahead of it, its future behind. On the two-process transfer of Exhibit 2.5, a £10 in flight from a £60 account to a £40 one is caught exactly once, in the channel’s recording: £50 and £40 recorded, £10 in flight, £100 conserved. Telephoning each process once and summing (Problem 2.6) yields instead £90 from a consistent cut whose in-flight message went uncounted, or £110 from an inconsistent cut with the credit but not its debit; markers cure the second error, channel recordings the first. The cost is one marker per channel, order $n^2$ per snapshot for a fully connected group. Correctness (Thm. 2.4), with $K$ the set of pre-recording events, those at each process before it records: (0) every process records exactly once, every channel carries exactly one marker, every channel’s recording closes; (i) $K$ is a consistent cut; (ii) the recorded states and channel recordings are exactly $S(K)$, each channel’s recording equalling $L_c(K)$; (iii) there is an execution $\alpha'$, with the same local history at every process as the actual one, in which $S(K)$ is the actual global state once the last pre-recording event completes — so $S(K)$ is reachable from the state at initiation, and the state at completion from $S(K)$. The Key Lemma, the one step where FIFO is spent: a post-recording send has a post-recording receive, because the sender’s marker entered the channel immediately on recording, ahead of the message, and cannot be overtaken; consistency is its contrapositive, and accounting follows because the messages a channel delivers ahead of its marker are exactly the pre-recording sends. Reachability rests on Lemma 2.3: adjacent concurrent events may be transposed without changing any local history, the happens-before relation, or the global state outside the pair (Problem 2.7), and finitely many such transpositions herd every pre-recording event before every post-recording one, where the global state is $S(K)$. The classic use (Cor. 2.5) is detecting stable properties, those that once true stay true — deadlock, termination, loss of a token, a permanently broken invariant: by (iii), a stable property holding at $S(K)$ holds at completion and ever after, and one failing at $S(K)$ did not hold at initiation.

Sharp edges. 

  • the clock condition is one-way: from $C(a) < C(b)$ the sole licensed inference is $\lnot(b \to a)$ (Rem. 2.6; Exhibit 2.3 — the lone local step $g_1$ stamped $1$, the send $b_2$ stamped $2$, the two concurrent). An order consistent with causality is not causality: sorting by Lamport time and reading the result as a causal history is Part One’s wall-clock error with better tooling.

  • “immediately” is the algorithm: in Box 6 the markers go out on recording, before any further application send. Flush queued application messages first and a post-recording send rides ahead of the late marker into a still-open channel recording: accounting (Thm. 2.4(ii)) fails while consistency (part (i)) survives, and the audit reports £110 against a true £100 with every individual record locally honest (Problem 2.10, Exhibit 2.6).

  • FIFO is spent exactly once: in the Key Lemma — a post-recording send is never received pre-recording, the sender’s marker having entered the channel first (Rem. 2.11). Over reliable channels that may reorder, the same theorem is bought with a one-bit colour on every message plus a per-channel count of pre-recording sends, announced at recording (Lai and Yang 1987; Problem 2.12).

  • the snapshot certifies stable properties only: a stable truth read off $S(K)$ holds at completion and ever after, and a stable falsehood had not yet occurred at initiation (Cor. 2.5); of a non-stable predicate such as “the queue is exactly seven deep” it testifies to nothing.

15.3 Chapter 3 — What Mathematics Forbids

The one sentence.  In the fully asynchronous model no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus while tolerating even one possible crash — delay alone, with every message delivered, prevents a decision forever — so every working system escapes by renegotiating one hypothesis (partial synchrony, randomization, or a failure detector), and the same indistinguishability argument, run across a network partition, forbids linearizability and total availability together (CAP).

The consensus problem.  Consensus (Def. 3.1): each process holds an input in $\{0,1\}$ and a write-once output register; a process decides by writing it, and what is written is never revised. Three clauses must hold in every admissible run. Agreement: no two processes decide different values. Validity: the decided value is some process’s input, so a unanimous input is the only permissible decision; this bars the trivial protocol that always decides $0$. Termination: every process that does not crash eventually decides. All three must survive one possible crash-stop failure: a process may halt silently at any moment, and nobody is told which, or when, or whether. That one possible crash is the entire difficulty: wait for every input and Termination fails if the missing process has crashed; act on a partial count and two processes may act on different counts, the missing process having merely been slow. FLP prove the theorem under non-triviality — some run decides $0$ and some decides $1$ — which is weaker than Validity and so gives a stronger impossibility. Validity asks only that the value be somebody’s input, nothing about its merit: the problem is coordination alone — one common answer secured against crashes and asynchrony — so the impossibility is attributable to coordination and to nothing else (Rem. 3.2).

The system model and the adversary.  The impossibility is proved against an adversary, the scheduler, which controls timing and timing alone — which pending message is delivered next, which process takes its next step and when — with no bound to respect, and which knows the protocol in full: a guarantee must hold against every arrangement of delays, hence against the cleverest one. The network is otherwise perfect: no message is lost, forged, or duplicated; only delivery order is unpromised. The vocabulary (Def. 3.3): a configuration is every process’s state plus the buffer of undelivered messages; an event $e = (p, m)$ delivers to $p$ one message addressed to it, or the null value $\varnothing$, at the adversary’s choice, whereupon $p$ takes one deterministic step; a schedule is a sequence of events. Admissibility (Def. 3.4) is the adversary’s only constraint: at most one process is faulty — takes only finitely many steps — and every message addressed to a correct process is eventually delivered. So it may delay any process indefinitely, and may crash at most one, permanently and without notice. Total correctness — every admissible run is deciding — is the form of Termination the proof attacks.

Valence and the commutation lemma.  For a reachable configuration $C$, let $V(C)$ be the set of values decided in configurations reachable from $C$ (Def. 3.5): $C$ is bivalent if $V(C) = \{0,1\}$ and $v$-valent if $V(C) = \{v\}$; in a totally correct protocol $V(C)$ is never empty, and the adversary, granted unlimited computation, can tell which is which. The fact the proof rests on: under Agreement, a configuration in which some process has decided $v$ is $v$-valent, so a bivalent configuration contains no decision anywhere, and an adversary that preserves bivalence forever prevents every decision forever (Rem. 3.6). The one tool (Lemma 3.1, FLP’s Lemma 1): two finite schedules that share no process commute — applied in either order from $C$, they reach the same configuration — since a step at one process neither consumes another’s messages nor changes its state.

Initial bivalence.  Lemma 3.2: every protocol solving consensus tolerating one fault has a bivalent initial configuration. By Validity the all-zeros initial configuration is $0$-valent and the all-ones $1$-valent; flip one process’s input at a time from one to the other, and if every initial configuration were univalent some adjacent pair $C^-$, $C^+$ would split — $0$-valent and $1$-valent, differing only in the input of one process $q$. Crash $q$ at the outset of $C^-$; the others must still reach a decision (one-crash tolerance), and their finite deciding schedule $\sigma$, containing no event of $q$, applies equally to $C^+$ with identical states and deliveries throughout: they cannot tell the two executions apart and decide the same value in both, which must be $0$ from $C^-$ and $1$ from $C^+$ — contradiction (Exhibit 3.1).

Preserving bivalence.  Lemma 3.3 (FLP’s Lemma 3; the chapter’s pivot lemma): from a bivalent $C$, any applicable event $e = (p, m)$ can be applied — after some finite schedule avoiding it — so as to land on a bivalent configuration. Suppose not, so every such landing is univalent. Both valences occur among the landings, and along a schedule avoiding $e$ a single event $e' = (p', m')$ therefore separates a $0$-valent landing $D_0 = e(C_0)$ from a $1$-valent one $D_1 = e(e'(C_0))$ (Problem 3.6 drills this step). If $e'$ is at another process ($p' \neq p$), commutation gives $e'(D_0) = D_1$: the $1$-valent landing is reachable from the $0$-valent one — contradiction (Exhibit 3.2). If both are at $p$, delay $p$ and let the others run from $C_0$ to a decision at $A = \sigma(C_0)$ — they must, since they cannot tell $p$ delayed from $p$ crashed; $A$ holds a decision, so it is univalent. But $\sigma$ has no step of $p$ while $e$ and $e'$ touch only $p$, so commutation gives $e(A) = \sigma(D_0)$, $0$-valent, and $e(e'(A)) = \sigma(D_1)$, $1$-valent: both futures remain open from $A$, which is bivalent yet holds a decision — contradiction (Exhibit 3.3).

The FLP theorem.  Thm. 3.4 (Fischer–Lynch–Paterson, 1985): in the asynchronous model with crash-stop failures, no deterministic protocol solves consensus tolerating one fault; every protocol satisfying Agreement and Validity in all admissible runs has an admissible run — with no crash and no loss — in which no process ever decides. The construction: start bivalent (Lemma 3.2); serve the processes round-robin, and on each process’s turn take the oldest message addressed to it ($\varnothing$ if none) as the event $e$, which Lemma 3.3 lets the adversary apply, after a finite schedule avoiding it, with bivalence kept. Every process moves infinitely often and every message is eventually delivered (finitely many predecessors in the buffer; its addressee’s turn comes every $n$ stages), so the run is admissible with zero faults; yet every stage ends bivalent, so no configuration in the run holds a decision (a decision would make its configuration univalent). Where each hypothesis is spent (Rem. 3.7): determinism powers every indistinguishability step and the commutation lemma; one-crash tolerance manufactures the deciding runs without $q$ and without $p$ — the obligation to finish without a crashed member is exactly what the adversary exploits; asynchrony lets it withhold $e$ and delay $p$ indefinitely without breaching admissibility; fairness is never breached.

Partial synchrony, randomization, and failure detectors.  The theorem’s hypotheses — asynchronous, deterministic, and a termination guarantee from message passing alone — are each a way around it: relax the timing model, add randomness, or add an oracle. All three buy the same thing: an interval in which one process is the leader, and is believed by the others to be the leader, for long enough to drive a decision through — what FLP shows cannot be manufactured from asynchronous determinism alone, and what Chapter 4’s single-decree Paxos is built to acquire.

The CAP theorem.  Brewer’s conjecture (PODC keynote, 2000) became a theorem in Gilbert and Lynch (SIGACT News, 2002). The letters, exactly: C is linearizability (Def. 3.9): the replicated register behaves as a single copy, its operations totally ordered consistently with real time, every read returning the latest completed write — not the ACID sense of “consistent,” not anything eventual. A is total availability: every request received by any non-failed node eventually receives a meaningful response, so one minority-side node declining one request forfeits it. P is a network partition, every message between the separated components lost while it lasts; nodes do not fail, the network does. C and A are guarantees; P is an event, not an option one declines. Thm. 3.5: in the asynchronous network model, no implementation of a read/write register guarantees both linearizability and availability in all executions, including those with a partition. The proof is the indistinguishability argument once more (Exhibit 3.4): partition the nodes into $G_1$ and $G_2$; a client’s write of $v_1$ over $v_0$ at a node in $G_1$ completes, by availability; a later read at a node in $G_2$ returns a value, by availability; but nothing crossed the partition, so the nodes of $G_2$ see exactly what they would have seen had the write never been issued, and in that execution linearizability makes the read return $v_0$ — so it returns $v_0$ here too, though the completed write of $v_1$ precedes it in real time. Linearizability fails: under a partition each affected operation must give up either linearizability or availability. The impossibility survives partial synchrony, where weaker contracts become achievable — Gilbert and Lynch’s $t$-connected consistency permits stale reads during the partition and requires reconciliation after it — and the frontier between the poles (bounded staleness, partial results, locally accepted writes with declared reconciliation debts) is measured by Fox and Brewer’s harvest, the fraction of the relevant data an answer reflects, and yield, the fraction of requests answered (Rem. 3.10; Problem 3.7).

Classifying impossibility claims.  Every “that cannot be done” goes into one of three bins (Problem 3.5). Impossible: a theorem applies, its model matched to the claim clause by clause — guaranteed-terminating deterministic consensus under asynchrony with a possible crash (Thm. 3.4), linearizability with total availability through a partition (Thm. 3.5), certain common action over a channel that may lose the last message (Two Generals, Chapter 1); the response is never more effort but renegotiation of the model, the guarantee, or the ask. Expensive: possible, shipping, and carrying a recurring cost — consensus under partial synchrony with its timeout tuning and election storms, linearizability across a continent at a round trip per write, exactly-once effect through idempotence engineering; the response is an invoice naming the cost and who pays it. Merely inadvisable: possible and affordable but unwise for this workload this year — consensus on every read where a lease would do, strong consistency for a cache whose staleness nobody can observe; the only bin whose contents change when the requirements do. The commonest senior error is misfiling between the second bin and the third.

Sharp edges. 

  • only Termination is lost: the run the proof constructs never violates Agreement or Validity — it never lets anyone decide. Protocols whose safety holds unconditionally under asynchrony exist (Chapter 4 exhibits one); what none can add is a termination guarantee (Rem. 3.8). Nor does the theorem predict routine failure: real timing noise leaves the non-deciding schedule almost at once, and the production symptom is livelock — election storms — not deadlock;

  • the crash need not happen: nobody crashes in the constructed run; the adversary exploits the protocol’s obligation to survive one. A process alive but starved of steps forever is, in the model, the same run as a crash (Problem 3.3): crashed and slow are indistinguishable by the model’s nature, not by a timeout’s mistuning;

  • delay, not loss: Two Generals (Chapter 1) is an impossibility of message loss; FLP is an impossibility of delay with every message delivered — “networks are unreliable” is half the lesson;

  • the CAP misreadings: partition tolerance is not chosen, and a “CA system” is one with no stated behaviour for a partition that will certainly occur; with no partition in progress the theorem says nothing. C is not ACID consistency and not anything eventual; A is forfeited by one minority node refusing one request (Problem 3.4); the CP/AP labels compress a per-operation, quorum-tunable contract into a syllable. The honest form: when a partition strikes, each affected operation gives up consistency or availability. And PACELC (Abadi 2012) covers the days without a partition: if Partition, trade Availability against Consistency; Else, trade Latency against Consistency — one-copy behaviour costs a quorum round trip or a leader’s confirmation on every write even on a healthy network;

  • uniform agreement: plain Agreement binds only the processes that stay correct; a process may decide, act on the decision (release a lock, charge a card), and crash while the others decide otherwise. Uniform agreement — no two processes, crashed later or not, decide differently — is a genuinely stronger property with a real price (Problem 3.8);

  • safety must never depend on timing: a rotating-leader protocol with timeouts in place of a failure detector, but without a prepare phase that first learns what an earlier majority may already have committed (Paxos’s prepare/promise phase with the adopt-the-highest rule, Chapter 4), has an admissible split-brain execution with no crash and every message delivered; retuning the timeouts makes it rare, never impossible (Problem 3.9, Exhibit 3.5). Likewise Ben-Or’s proposal guard is a majority of all $n$ reports, not of the $n - t$ received: two majorities of $n$ must share a reporter (Box 7).

15.4 Chapter 4 — The Part-Time Parliament

The one sentence.  Fault-tolerant consensus makes every new attempt learn the past before it may propose: a value is chosen when a quorum of acceptors has accepted it at one ballot; persisted promises and the rule of adopting the highest-ballot value reported in phase one force every higher ballot to re-propose the chosen value; safety holds unconditionally in the asynchronous crash-recovery model; and termination, which the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson impossibility result (Theorem 3.4) forbids any protocol to promise there, is bought separately with a distinguished proposer and partial synchrony.

Single-decree consensus and the system model.  Single-decree consensus (Def. 4.1) decides one value once; the log of many decisions is Chapter 5’s. Three roles, usually all on each machine: proposers hold input values, acceptors keep the persistent state and cast the votes that alone decide safety, learners want the outcome. A value $v$ is chosen iff some quorum of acceptors has all accepted the same proposal $(b, v)$ at one ballot number $b$. Required: Agreement — at most one value is ever chosen; Validity — any chosen value was some proposer’s input; termination is deliberately not required. “Chosen” is a silent, stable fact (Rem. 4.2): a property of the acceptors’ collective persistent state, it can hold while no participant knows it and, once true, is never unmade — irrevocability is the whole content of Agreement. The model: asynchronous message passing; the network delays and reorders without bound but never forges; crash-recovery failures — a process may stop at any point and later resume with stable storage intact and volatile state gone; every persistent variable is written to stable storage before any message reporting it is sent; quorums are majorities of a fixed membership (reconfiguration: Chapter 5).

Quorum intersection.  Any two majorities of one finite set share a member (Lemma 4.1): disjoint majorities would together exceed $n$. So any later majority contains a member of any earlier one, carrying the evidence whether or not it knows it. Three acceptors form quorums of two and tolerate one absentee; five, quorums of three and two absentees; seven, four and three; four form quorums of three and tolerate only one, no better than three — hence odd group sizes (Problem 4.1). No proof uses “more than half” as such, only pairwise intersection, so any family of pairwise-intersecting sets — a quorum system — serves in place of majorities (Rem. 4.3); and only intersection across the two phases is ever required, so each phase may use its own family — flexible Paxos (Howard, Malkhi, and Spiegelman, 2016; Problem 4.10).

Single-decree Paxos (Box 8).  Lamport’s protocol, checked against “Paxos Made Simple” (2001). Ballot numbers (Def. 4.4) are totally ordered, each owned by one proposer and carrying at most one proposal. An acceptor persists two variables (Def. 4.6), each written before the reply it enables is sent: $\mathit{promised}$, the highest ballot whose prepare it has answered, and $\mathit{accepted}$, its highest-ballot accepted proposal, value included. Phase one: the proposer of ballot $b$ sends prepare$(b)$, naming no value; an acceptor answers only if $b$ is strictly above $\mathit{promised}$, records $b$ there, and replies with a promise carrying the promise itself, to accept nothing below $b$, and, separately, a report of its highest-ballot accepted proposal, or $\bot$ if none (Lamport’s LastVote). Phase two: holding a quorum’s promises, the proposer sends accept$(b, v)$ with $v$ fixed by the adoption rule — the value of the highest-ballot proposal any reply reported, its own input only if every reply reported $\bot$; an acceptor accepts $(b, v)$ if $b$ is at least $\mathit{promised}$, records it in $\mathit{accepted}$, and acknowledges to proposer and learners; a quorum’s acceptances at one ballot make $v$ chosen (Rem. 4.7; reference trace, Exhibit 4.3).

The safety invariant and the safety theorem.  The invariant (Lemma 4.3): if $v$ is chosen at ballot $b$, then every proposal issued at any ballot $b' > b$ has value $v$. The shape, by strong induction on $b'$: the phase-one quorum of $b'$ meets the choosing quorum of $b$ in some acceptor $a$; $a$ accepted $(b, v)$ before answering prepare$(b')$, since afterwards its persistent, non-decreasing $\mathit{promised}$, at least $b'$, would have refused the acceptance (the ordering subtlety, Problem 4.6); so $a$'s reply, and hence the highest ballot reported to the proposer of $b'$, lies in $[b, b')$, where every acceptance carries $v$ — at $b$ by hypothesis, above $b$ by induction — and the adoption rule does the rest. With one proposal per ballot (Lemma 4.2), Agreement follows under any schedule and any crashes and recoveries (Thm. 4.4): two values chosen at one ballot are one proposal, and at two ballots would contradict the invariant. Validity: every proposed value is the proposer’s input or an earlier proposal’s value adopted, and the chain of adoptions descends to an input. No clocks, timeouts, or probabilities enter the argument.

Liveness, the distinguished proposer, and learning.  The argument never promised that any ballot finishes. Two proposers may alternate, each prepare at a higher ballot voiding the rival’s pending phase two while the acceptors refuse each accept against their newer promise, and ballot numbers climb forever — the duelling proposers, an admissible non-terminating execution, as FLP requires (Exhibit 4.4; in production, an epoch counter climbing with no decisions). The remedy (Rem. 4.8): a distinguished proposer, elected by timeout and held by heartbeat — an implementation of the eventual leader oracle $\Omega$ of Chapter 3 — plus partial synchrony so it eventually sticks. Then phase one runs once and each later decision costs a single accept round — multi-Paxos, the subject of Chapter 5. Two processes that both believe they are the leader cost throughput, never safety, since acceptors refuse any ballot below their promise: the leader is a liveness optimization; safety lives in the quorums. Choice and knowledge of choice are also different events (Rem. 4.10): a learner starting cold must not simply read the acceptors’ state, which may show a mid-ballot mixture, but runs a complete ballot of its own, since phase-one replies prove only that a value is the candidate the adoption rule would force, possibly a leftover never chosen, and only a quorum’s acceptances at one ballot certify a choice.

Raft: terms, the term rule, and elections (Boxes 9 and 9b).  Raft (Ongaro and Ousterhout, 2014) reorganizes the same machine for understandability around a strong leader and its log. Time divides into numbered terms, each with at most one leader; every server is a follower, candidate, or leader; each persists $\mathit{currentTerm}$, $\mathit{votedFor}$, and its log, whose entries carry the term of the leader that created them; the $\mathit{commitIndex}$ is volatile (Def. 4.11). The term rule runs on every message, in either direction, before its contents are read: a higher term is adopted at once — persisted, the vote cleared, the recipient a follower whatever its office; a lower term is refused with the recipient’s term in the reply, so a deposed leader deposes itself at first contact with a later term, and nobody need establish that it died. Election: a follower hearing no AppendEntries (the heartbeat, when empty) for its randomized election timeout increments its term, votes for itself (persisted), and sends every peer RequestVote with the index and term of its last entry; a voter grants iff its vote for the term is unspent and the candidate’s log is at least as up to date as its own — later last term, then longer log (the up-to-date restriction, Def. 4.11) — persisting the vote before replying. A quorum of grants makes it leader; an AppendEntries at an equal or later term returns it to follower; a further timeout is a split vote, the term simply wasted, and randomized timeouts make repeats improbable. Election safety (Lemma 4.5): at most one leader per term, since votes are one per term, persisted, and two winning quorums would share a voter.

Raft: log replication.  AppendEntries names the index and term of the entry immediately preceding its cargo, and the follower’s consistency check refuses on any mismatch; otherwise it deletes a conflicting suffix, appends what is new, and advances its $\mathit{commitIndex}$ to the leader’s, no further than its last new entry (Box 9b). On a refusal the leader decrements that follower’s $\mathit{nextIndex}$ and retries, walking backward until the logs agree and then shipping its own log forward; followers never negotiate, and the leader never rewrites its own log (leader append-only). Log matching (Lemma 4.6): same index and term means identical entries and identical prefixes, since a leader creates one entry per (index, term) and a follower installs an entry only after the check has matched its predecessor. Followers learn of a commit from the next AppendEntries, even a heartbeat, one message after the leader (Exhibit 4.5).

Raft: the commit rule and the Figure 8 scenario.  The leader advances $\mathit{commitIndex}$ to $i$ only when (a) a quorum, its own log counted, has stored index $i$ and (b) the entry at $i$ carries the leader’s current term; earlier-term entries commit only transitively beneath such an entry — a fresh no-operation entry on taking office suffices. Leader completeness (Thm. 4.7): an entry committed in term $t$ is in the log of every later leader. The shape: the storing quorum meets the electing quorum in a voter that still held the entry (no intermediate leader displaced it, by induction and log matching) and whose vote required the candidate’s log to be at least as up to date as its own, which a log lacking the entry cannot be (Problem 4.9). State-machine safety follows (Corollary 4.8): no two servers apply different entries at one index. Without clause (b), the Raft paper’s Figure 8 scenario (Problem 4.8; Exhibits 4.6 and 4.7) breaks it on five servers: in term two the leader appends an entry at index two, replicates it to one follower, and pauses; in term three a second server, elected by the two servers whose logs end at index one, appends its own entry at index two and crashes before replicating it; in term four the first leader returns, is re-elected, and replicates its term-two entry to a third server — three of five, which the modified rule counts as committed, and the client is answered; in term five the second server returns, its last term (three) outranks the voters’ whatever the lengths, it wins, and its entry overwrites the “committed” one on every server. Replication on a quorum makes an entry durable, but elections protect committed entries by comparing terms, and an inherited entry’s term is old: only replication in the leader’s own term commits. Under the true rule the schedule is harmless: the term-four leader first commits a fresh term-four entry, log matching commits the inherited one beneath it, and the second server’s term-five candidacy fails at the voter whose log now ends in term four — two votes of five, no quorum.

The consensus number and universality.  Herlihy (1991) ranks every shared-memory object by its consensus number, the largest $n$ for which it solves $n$-process wait-free consensus (Rem. 4.12): read/write registers rank one — even two processes cannot solve consensus with registers alone, Chapter 3’s bivalence argument transplanted to shared memory; test-and-set, queues, and stacks rank two; compare-and-swap and consensus itself rank infinity. The universality theorem: from consensus, a fault-tolerant linearizable implementation of any sequential object — agree, repeatedly, on the next operation, and feed the agreed log through deterministic replicas. That construction opens Chapter 5; linearizability is defined in Chapter 7.

Sharp edges. 

  • why two phases: a new ballot must not be free — phase one freezes the past (the promise) and discloses it (the report of the highest accepted proposal); ballot numbers alone let an uninformed proposer overwrite a chosen value (Exhibit 4.2).

  • the guard asymmetry: prepare needs $b > \mathit{promised}$ strictly, accept only $b \ge \mathit{promised}$: an acceptor may accept the ballot it has just promised, or no ballot could complete; strictness on prepare gives a ballot’s owner one set of replies, never two (Problem 4.3).

  • volatile state is a different protocol: the invariant’s ordering step rests on $\mathit{promised}$ being persistent and never decreasing (Rem. 4.9); a promise kept in memory and lost in a reboot lets two values be chosen at two ballots (Problem 4.7, Exhibit 4.8), and persisting $\mathit{accepted}$ alone does not help, since the failing guard consults $\mathit{promised}$; forgetting $\mathit{votedFor}$ across a crash likewise yields two Raft leaders in one term.

  • where intersection is spent: exactly once — the phase-one quorum against the choosing quorum; two phase-two quorums never need to meet, two values chosen at two ballots being excluded by the invariant, not by overlap (Rem. 4.3).

  • deference to the highest ballot is not a freshness rule: the adoption rule protects a value that may already be chosen and applies even to a leftover never chosen, the two being indistinguishable from inside a set of replies (Rem. 4.5; Problem 4.4: one higher-ballot report outranks two copies of a lower one).

  • elections read terms, not counts: a shorter log ending in a later term outranks a longer one (Problem 4.5); “wait until all five servers hold it” surrenders availability to one slow server and still loses the up-to-date comparison to any later-term log.

15.5 Chapter 5 — Consensus in Captivity

The one sentence.  In production, consensus is a replicated log driving identical deterministic state machines, usually rented from a coordination service (Chubby, ZooKeeper, etcd) whose every lock and election is a claim about time — made safe by the lease’s clock-drift arithmetic and enforced by a fencing token checked at the resource — and once a process may lie (a Byzantine fault), tolerating $f$ faulty processes takes $3f{+}1$ members, not $2f{+}1$.

The two system models.  For the log, the coordination services, and the lease and fencing results (Parts One to Four), Chapter 4’s model: asynchronous; crash-recovery with stable storage; quorums are majorities of the current membership. The lease theorem alone adds one clock assumption — every clock’s rate lies within $[1-\rho,\ 1+\rho]$ of true time; nothing is assumed about offsets. For the Byzantine results (Part Five): synchronous rounds and no message loss, deliberately generous so that forgery alone is to blame (the impossibility then carries over to the asynchronous case a fortiori); up to $f$ members Byzantine and colluding; oral messages — the immediate sender of a message is known to its receiver, but a relayed message’s content is whatever the relayer chooses.

Multi-Paxos and the replicated state machine.  Number the decisions: log slot $k$ is decided by its own instance of single-decree Paxos, and the leader runs phase one once, for all slots at a stroke, so that each decision thereafter costs a single accept round — multi-Paxos (Box 10; Raft is the same design with the leader and the log made primary). Leader succession: the new leader prepares a higher ballot for all slots, adopts for each slot the highest-ballot value any member reports having accepted, and fills the remaining holes with no-operation entries; since the apply rule forbids applying slot $k$ before slots $1..k{-}1$, an unfilled hole stalls every replica behind it (Exhibit 5.1). Above the log sits the replicated state machine (Def. 5.1): a deterministic transition function $\delta$, committed entries applied in slot order, each exactly once, every command a pure function of state and entry. Thm. 5.1, by induction on the prefix: replicas that have applied prefixes of equal length hold identical states and have emitted identical outputs — replicas differ by an unapplied suffix, never by divergence. Determinism is stricter than it looks: wall-clock reads, random draws, hash-table iteration order, and the local machine’s own environment are hidden second inputs, different at every replica; decide each of them once, at the leader, at proposal time, and write the result into the log entry (Problem 5.5).

Membership changes.  Membership defines the quorum: it is the denominator of “majority.” Distribute a new membership list out of band and, for a while, some members believe the cluster has five members (majority three) while others believe seven (majority four); the network can then assemble two disjoint sets, each lawfully a majority of the cluster as it understands it — split brain, without a single machine failing. So a membership change goes through the log like any other state change, under one of two disciplines: joint consensus, a transitional configuration in which every decision needs a majority of the old membership and a majority of the new; or one server at a time, which needs no transitional machinery because any majority of $n$ members and any majority of the same list grown or shrunk by one member intersect (Lemma 5.2) — provided each change commits before the next begins. The corners to check in any reconfiguration design: the new member is fully caught up before it counts toward a quorum; the removed member is told to stop starting elections; and a change in flight does not race a leader change (the bug found and corrected in Raft’s single-server scheme — The Reading).

Coordination services: Chubby, ZooKeeper, Zab.  Almost nobody runs their own consensus; it is rented from a coordination service — the course’s coordination kernel: locks, leases, leader election, membership, and small volumes of hot metadata, operated once, carefully, and shared by every service in the fleet (Part Three). Chubby (Burrows, 2006): a small file-system-like namespace on five Paxos replicas; one session per client, a single master lease under which every lock, cached file, and open handle is held; client caches tracked by the server and invalidated, with acknowledgment, before a change commits; and the jeopardy callback, which tells the application that everything it cached is now suspect and every lock it held may be gone. ZooKeeper (Hunt and colleagues, 2010): a tree of znodes, kilobytes at most; ephemeral nodes, which live exactly as long as their creator’s session (presence as data); sequential nodes, numbered by a counter that only rises; and one-shot watches — which compose into leader election without a thundering herd: every candidate creates an ephemeral sequential node, the lowest number leads, and each candidate watches only the node immediately below its own. Zab, ZooKeeper’s broadcast protocol, stamps every proposal with a zxid (epoch, counter), discards a stale-epoch proposal on sight, and completes recovery strictly before broadcasting new proposals (Problem 5.9). Ballot number, term, view number, zxid: one idea under four names.

Leases and fencing tokens.  The failure they answer (Part Four): a lock holder pauses — a long garbage collection, a virtual-machine migration — without crashing; the coordination service, hearing no heartbeat, expires the session and grants the lock to a successor, exactly as designed; the original holder then resumes, still believing it holds the lock, and writes. Two writers, one resource, no component misbehaving; and no finite timeout removes the case, because the pause is unbounded. The lease (Def. 5.2, Box 11): the grantor counts a full period $T$ on its own clock from the grant before re-granting; the holder counts the discounted horizon $T(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)$ on its own clock from its request — deliberately the earlier anchor — and gives up all authority when it passes. Thm. 5.3: in true time both windows end $T/(1+\rho)$ after their anchors, and the request precedes the grant, so the holder’s window lies inside the grantor’s guard — mutual exclusion in real time from a bound on clock rate alone, with no assumption about offsets. The lease guards only the schedule, however: a holder may pause between checking its lease and acting on it. The fencing token (Def. 5.4) closes that gap at the resource: every grant carries a strictly increasing token, every operation the holder issues is stamped with it, and the resource keeps a durable high-water mark per object and refuses any stamp below it — so a resumed holder’s stale write is rejected by the store, whatever the lock service believes (Exhibit 5.2). Enforcement belongs at the point of effect.

Byzantine faults: three cannot tolerate one.  A Byzantine process may behave arbitrarily — send different messages to different peers, misreport what it received, collude with other faulty processes (Part Five). Interactive consistency (Def. 5.5; Lamport, Shostak, and Pease 1982): a commander holds an order, attack{} or abstain{}, and sends it to lieutenants, who exchange messages; IC1, all loyal lieutenants output the same order; IC2, if the commander is loyal, that order is the commander’s — without IC2, always retreating would satisfy the specification. Thm. 5.4: with three participants, one of them Byzantine, no protocol using oral messages satisfies IC1 and IC2, however many rounds it runs. The proof is an indistinguishability argument with forged messages. Execution one: the commander is the traitor, and behaves toward the first lieutenant exactly as a loyal commander ordering attack{} would, toward the second exactly as one ordering abstain{} would. Execution two: the commander is loyal and orders attack{}; the second lieutenant is the traitor, and relays exactly what a loyal lieutenant would have relayed had the commander said abstain{}. The first lieutenant’s evidence is identical in both executions, so its output is; IC2 fixes that output at attack{} in execution two, hence in execution one. The mirror-image construction fixes the second lieutenant’s output at abstain{} in execution one, where both lieutenants are loyal — and IC1 fails (Exhibit 5.3). The root cause: a relayed message is worth only its relayer’s honesty — hearsay cannot be audited. Signed messages make relays verifiable and the three-node impossibility dissolves; Byzantine trouble is thereby re-priced, not solved, since the signature scheme and key distribution join the trusted base (Problem 5.11).

Quorum arithmetic under Byzantine faults.  Thm. 5.5 (Pease, Shostak, and Lamport 1980): interactive consistency with $f$ Byzantine participants is solvable iff $n \ge 3f{+}1$. Necessity: partition a smaller group into three blocs of at most $f$ members each; a protocol for the group would yield a three-participant protocol tolerating one traitor, contradicting Thm. 5.4 (Problem 5.10). Why the crash-fault arithmetic does not survive:

PBFT.  Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (Castro and Liskov, 1999; Box 12, Exhibit 5.4, Rem. 5.7): $n = 3f{+}1$ replicas, a fixed primary per view. Pre-prepare: the primary assigns the request a slot. Prepare, all-to-all: a prepare certificate of $2f{+}1$ matching prepares pins (view, slot, request), since two conflicting certificates would share an honest replica, and an honest replica prepares one request per slot per view. Commit, all-to-all: a commit certificate makes the binding survive a view change, whose new primary must prove by certificate what was committed. The client accepts a result vouched for by $f{+}1$ matching replies. Three message rounds in the normal case, and all-to-all traffic quadratic in $n$ — the price of trusting no single replica.

Nakamoto consensus.  Bitcoin’s protocol (2008) discards the membership list altogether: with no list there is no denominator and no quorum. In its place, a lottery weighted by computational work (proof of work) chooses who appends the next block to the log; honest nodes extend the longest chain they have seen; finality is only probabilistic (a block six deep is expensive to reverse, never impossible); and safety is economic, holding only while an attack costs more than it yields — a condition that has failed in practice on minor currencies, where rented computation re-mined settled history. Inside one organization, where a membership list exists, quorum intersection costs five machines rather than a power grid (Part Six).

The cost of a decision, and when not to use consensus.  One decision costs a round trip to a quorum plus a synchronous disk write at every voting member. A coordination service settles thousands to tens of thousands of decisions per second, shared by every service in the fleet, while a busy front-end service admits hundreds of thousands of requests per second; that gap is why leases amortize one decision over many requests, why leaders batch many log entries per round trip, and why the coordination service stores only small, hot metadata (Part Seven). Use consensus for what is genuinely critical — leadership, membership, locks, metadata whose loss or divergence is catastrophic — and never for reads whose staleness is survivable. A leader can be deposed without yet knowing it, so its confident local answer to a read may be stale: reads therefore go through the log, priced like writes, or are served under a leader lease, correct exactly insofar as the clock-drift bound holds (Chapter 7).

Sharp edges. 

  • the lease guards only the schedule: it cannot make a paused holder check its clock — the pause may fall between the check and the write; only the fencing check at the resource closes that window (Rem. 5.3).

  • anchor the horizon at the request, not the grant: anchored at receipt of the grant, the holder’s window overlaps the successor’s by the grant message’s delivery delay, which nothing bounds; repairing that means bounding network delay, a stronger assumption than clock drift (Problem 5.7).

  • wall-clock timestamps cannot replace fencing tokens: a resumed holder with a fast clock outbids its lawful successor; tokens are issued by one authority in grant order, timestamps by many clocks under no ordering guarantee (Problem 5.8, Exhibit 5.5).

  • the high-water mark must be durable: kept in memory, it restarts at zero after a crash and the stale stamp passes; and the fence does not order operations bearing the same token, so a holder racing itself (two threads, one lease) needs its own sequencing (Problem 5.6).

15.6 Chapter 6 — Replication, or the Dynamo School

The one sentence.  Every scheme for keeping copies of data on machines that fail answers two questions — which replicas may accept a write, and which must confirm it before the client is acknowledged — and the leaderless answer of the Dynamo school, any replica, by sloppy quorum, keeps the store always writeable at a price Part Four states exactly: a quorum overlap ($R+W>N$) guarantees that the newest acknowledged write is visible to every later read, but not linearizability, until the reader writes what it has read back to a majority before answering.

The replication grid.  Def. 6.1 classifies every replication scheme by two coordinates. Acceptance: which replicas may accept a client write — one (single-leader), one per site (multi-leader), or any (leaderless). Acknowledgment: whose durable confirmation precedes the client’s acknowledgment — the accepting replica alone (asynchronous), a fixed set (synchronous; “any $k$ of them” is semi-synchronous), or a quorum $(N, R, W)$. Part Six draws the grid as a table, each empty cell labelled with the reason it is empty, with two entries under standing exceptions: the coordination service of Chapter 5, the single-leader quorum cell rented rather than run, and chain replication (van Renesse and Schneider, 2004), in which writes enter at the head and are forwarded hop by hop, the tail acknowledges, and reads consult only the tail — whatever the tail holds the whole chain holds, so tail reads are linearizable by topology alone, without quorum arithmetic (Exhibit 6.1, Problem 6.6). Every system on the market is a cell of this grid, whatever its marketing says.

Single-leader replication.  The primary accepts writes and forwards them to followers; the most consequential setting is whether it waits. Synchronous: acknowledge after the followers confirm durability — recovery point zero, at the price of a round trip to the slowest follower and availability coupled to the least available member. Semi-synchronous: wait for any $k$ of the followers. Asynchronous: acknowledge at once and forward later — a standing window of confirmed-but-unreplicated writes, which on unrecoverable primary failure is the data lost; the recovery point objective (RPO) is how much confirmed history can vanish, the recovery time objective (RTO) how long failover leaves the system dark, and the asynchronous window is priced as seconds of exposure, times writes per second, times the worth of a write (Problem 6.1). Follower reads are reads of the past, aged by the replication lag: a client writes, refreshes, and cannot read its own write; the remedies become Chapter 7’s session guarantees. Failover rests on a timeout, and the wrong verdict is the false death: a primary paused rather than dead is condemned, a successor promoted, and the original resumes, accepting writes from stale routing tables — two primaries, every write the deposed one accepts a fork in the history. The fence: promotion through consensus or a coordination service, so that at most one promotion can be chosen, and an epoch on all replication traffic, so that followers reject the deposed primary’s messages.

Multi-leader replication.  A leader per region, each accepting writes locally and forwarding to the others, buys latency, and concurrent writes to one key — neither in the other’s causal history — conflict by design. Last-writer-wins: timestamp every write; on conflict keep the larger and silently discard the smaller. Adjudicated by wall clocks that Chapter 2 showed cannot order events across machines, a customer’s corrected address ceases to exist with every log clean: not conflict resolution but conflict denial, data loss by policy wearing a tie-breaking rule, acceptable only where the schema itself makes any discarded write superseded by the survivor (Problem 6.9). The alternative is to merge: Bayou (Terry et al., 1995) attached an application-written dependency check and merge procedure to every write, and Dynamo inherited the principle that the conflict is application state and the system keeps both versions with their causal metadata rather than discarding one quietly. GitHub, October 2018: a forty-three-second partition, an automated promotion, writes accepted on both sides, two histories neither discardable and no timestamp able to adjudicate, and more than twenty-four hours of degraded service to reconcile them. Partitions are cheap to enter and ruinous to leave; a design that can produce two primaries owes its reviewers the reconciliation plan in writing, in advance.

Leaderless replication: quorums and their repair mechanisms.  Dynamo (DeCandia et al., SOSP 2007) is built to be always writeable: no primary, no elections, no terms; any replica may accept any operation, and correctness, where arranged, is arranged by arithmetic. Each key has a preference list, the ordered set of replicas responsible for it, read off a hashed ring (Chapter 9), its first $N$ entries the home set; membership travels by gossip. Def. 6.2: a write is acknowledged after $W$ durable confirmations; a read consults $R$ replicas and adopts the causally newest version returned; with $R+W>N$ any read quorum intersects any write quorum (quorum intersection, Lemma 4.1), so some replica the read asks holds the newest acknowledged version (Box 13). Three mechanisms keep the store writeable under failure and convergent afterwards:

  • sloppy quorums and hinted handoff: rather than refuse when a home replica is unreachable, the coordinator slides down the preference list; the copy lands at a stand-in beyond the first $N$, tagged with a hint naming the home address, and streams home on recovery, each hint discharged on confirmation. Durability is honoured in count but temporarily not in address: Lemma 4.1 assumed a fixed membership, and a sloppy write may land entirely on stand-ins while a later read consults the recovered home set — overlap by arithmetic traded for overlap by high likelihood (Problem 6.3).

  • read repair: a read adopts the newest of the versions it collects and sends it back to any queried replica holding strictly older state; every read repairs the replicas it consulted, free.

  • anti-entropy: cold data is reconciled pairwise in the background by Merkle tree: over each key range, leaves fingerprint small ranges and each parent hashes its children, so equal roots settle identical holdings in one comparison and differing roots are descended with every matching branch dismissed unexamined. Lemma 6.1: with branching factor $b$, depth $d$, and $\Delta$ differing leaf ranges, at most $b \cdot \Delta \cdot d + 1$ fingerprints are exchanged — traffic scales with the disagreement, not the data set (Exhibit 6.2, Problem 6.5).

Quorum overlap and the register hierarchy.  The industry belief that $R+W>N$ yields linearizability, near enough, fails with no crashes or partitions, only network delay, at the strongest tuning (Exhibit 6.3): $N{=}3$, $W{=}R{=}2$, strict, one write of version two in flight and durable at one replica only. A first read whose quorum includes that replica is answered two; a second read, begun after the first completed, whose quorum holds the other two replicas is answered one. No quorum was violated: the overlap makes the newest acknowledged version visible, and nothing keeps it seen. Lamport’s taxonomy (Def. 6.3) names the grades. Safe: a read overlapping no write returns the latest completed write’s value. Regular: a read overlapping writes returns some overlapping or latest-preceding write’s value — old or new, never invented. Atomic: the operations admit a total order, consistent with real time, in which every read returns its latest preceding write — linearizability specialized to registers, the general word Chapter 7 adopts. The new-then-old anomaly is the canonical regular-but-not-atomic behaviour, and Rem. 6.4 states what strict quorums buy: completed-write visibility is a theorem, hence safety and regularity; atomicity fails because nothing obliges the new version, once served to one reader, to be served to the next. Sloppy quorums weaken even regularity to high likelihood (Problem 6.7; Problem 6.2 grades the $N{=}3$ tunings).

The ABD atomic register.  Attiya, Bar-Noy, and Dolev (1990; final form JACM 1995) repaired exactly this with one clause: a reader that has seen a version must write it back before repeating it. Box 14, under a fully asynchronous model in which any minority of the $n$ servers may crash: replicas keep the highest-stamped (stamp, value) pair they have seen; the single writer stamps each write with its next sequence number and completes on a majority’s acknowledgments; a reader takes the highest-stamped pair among a majority of replies and, before returning it, stores it at a majority — the write-back, skipped only when the replies were unanimous. The first reader thereby stores version two at a majority before answering, and the second reader’s quorum must intersect it (Exhibit 6.4). Thm. 6.2: the protocol implements an atomic (linearizable) single-writer multi-reader register, and every operation of a correct client terminates regardless of scheduling — no consensus, no leader, no synchrony. The idea: every majority that stored a stamp intersects every later query majority and the writer’s stamps only rise, so ordering operations by stamp, each write before the reads that adopted it, extends real time. Rem. 6.5: a reader that adopts a stamp held by fewer than a majority completes the stranded write; a read pays a write’s cost exactly when its quorum disagreed; CAP is paid by the minority side of a partition blocking. The multi-writer extension is Problem 6.10. The border: a read/write register has consensus number one in Herlihy’s hierarchy — it cannot elect, lock, or compare-and-swap — so linearizable storage costs majorities and the write-back, while linearizable decision costs consensus (Chapter 4).

Version vectors, siblings, and merges.  Two writes to the same key from different clients, landing at different coordinators, are the normal case, and if neither write’s history includes the other they are concurrent: no lawful system may pretend one superseded the other. Def. 6.6: each stored version carries a version vector of (coordinator, counter) entries; a read returns the sibling set with a context, the merge of their vectors, and a write carries that context back as declared ancestry. A stored version strictly below an incoming context is superseded; incomparable versions coexist as siblings, returned together until an application write merges them under a joint context; deletion is a tombstone version; and the dotted version vector pins each version to its precise coordinator event, curing the false siblings a bare per-coordinator counter produces (Problem 6.4). The merge belongs to the application, which alone knows what the data means — set union, for a cart — and it must obey three laws: the result must not depend on the order in which siblings are presented, duplicates must be absorbed, and nothing may be invented; Chapter 7 names them and proves the convergence theorem they underwrite. The first audit question of a Dynamo-style store is not the quorum tuning but the conflict mode: siblings with an owned merge, or last-writer-wins discarding the loser.

Choosing a scheme by workload.  Consistency need first, then latency, then topology. The shopping cart — siblings absorbable, loss forbidden — takes leaderless replication with version vectors and a union merge, the workload Dynamo exists for; the payments ledger, loss and siblings both forbidden, takes a single leader with synchronous acknowledgment or a consensus group outright; telemetry is last-writer-wins’ one honest home, supersession in the schema, not the clock.

Sharp edges. 

  • quorum overlap is not atomicity: strict $R+W>N$ buys durability, completed-write visibility, and regularity, never atomicity: the new-then-old anomaly violates no quorum, and only a write-back that blocks the reader before answering closes it (Thm. 6.2). Where does the read-back live, and does it block?

  • sloppy is not strict: a sloppy write may land entirely on stand-in replicas while a later read consults the recovered home set — overlap is likelihood, not law, until hinted handoff drains (Problem 6.3).

  • $W$ counts durable confirmations, never messages sent: a coordinator that counts a write toward $W$ at the send can acknowledge a write that no surviving replica holds, and no repair mechanism can restore what exists nowhere (Problem 6.8, Exhibit 6.5).

  • read repair is not anti-entropy: read repair fixes what is read, when it is read; anti-entropy sweeps cold data on a schedule; neither restores ordering.

  • deletion is a write: erase without a tombstone and anti-entropy restores the entry from the replicas that still hold it; garbage-collect the tombstone while any replica might still reintroduce its ancestor and the entry returns (windowed deduplication, Chapter 1).

15.7 Chapter 7 — Consistency: A Field Guide

The one sentence.  A consistency model is a contract about the histories a system may show its clients — never about its implementation — and the chapter turns on three facts: a stronger contract is a smaller set of permitted histories, paid for in coordination; single operations and transactions are governed on different axes, which meet only at strict serializability; and where the replicated state is a join-semilattice, convergence stops being a promise and becomes a theorem.

Histories, and consistency models as sets of histories.  Clients act on shared objects through operations, each an invocation and a response; $o \prec o'$ iff $o$'s response precedes $o'$'s invocation, and operations related neither way are concurrent. The history is the transcript of every invocation and response with its time, and nothing about implementation (Def. 7.1). A consistency model is a set of permitted histories, whence three rules: strength is containment, a stronger model being a smaller set; the contract is a floor, not a description, so audit what it permits under partition, not what a healthy cluster shows all week; and testing is membership checking, which is the Jepsen method. The sequential specification (Def. 7.2), an object’s behaviour on one machine, one operation at a time, is the yardstick: every model is a rule for explaining a concurrent history by a legal one-at-a-time story, differing only in what the explanation may ignore.

Linearizability, and the history separating it from sequential consistency.  A complete history is linearizable (Def. 7.4; Herlihy and Wing 1990) iff each operation can be given a linearization point inside its own interval such that executing the operations instantaneously at their points is legal for every object — equivalently, iff a legal sequential story on the same operations extends $\prec$. Two ways to fail: an illegal story (a queue returning the same item twice), or a legal story that reorders non-overlapping operations; overlapping operations may be ordered either way, and that is the definition’s whole allowance. Three debts settled: the C of CAP is linearizability, Gilbert and Lynch’s history being a completed write on one side of a partition and a later, non-overlapping read of the old value on the other; “behaves as a single machine” is Def. 7.4; and a lease-served read is linearizable exactly while the clock-drift bound holds. The separating history (Exhibit 7.1): one register at two replicas, writes through an agreed log, reads served locally. A client writes ten over the old value five and the write is confirmed; the client telephones a second client, who then reads at the other replica, where the log entry has lawfully not yet been applied, and receives five. Not linearizable: the write precedes the read in real time, so the read must return ten. Sequentially consistent: the story “read five, then write ten” respects both program orders and is legal. Without the telephone no violation can be shown, so sequential consistency is linearizability for clients who never compare notes, and the gap is exactly as wide as the side channels among your clients; a freshness check against the log before answering restores ten, and that round trip on every read is what linearizability costs and sequential consistency saves (Problems 7.1(d) and 7.7(c)).

The hierarchy: sequential, causal, session, eventual.  As sets of histories, $\mathrm{Lin} \subsetneq \mathrm{SC} \subsetneq \mathrm{Causal}$, all inside eventual consistency (Thm. 7.5); each containment is an implication between explanation obligations, each strictness a two-client history (Exhibit 7.1 above, Exhibit 7.2 below). Exhibit 7.6 draws the hierarchy beside the transactional axis, with reference tables of each model’s promise, admitted anomalies, and price.

  • sequential consistency (Def. 7.6; Lamport 1979): one legal story agreed by all clients, extending each client’s program order but not $\prec$ across clients; the clock is surrendered, and the agreed world may lag real time without bound. ZooKeeper’s default reads are exactly this, with a per-read sync sold separately.

  • causal consistency (Def. 7.7): happens-before is the transitive closure of program order and reads-from; each client may have its own legal story over its operations and all writes, provided it extends happens-before, so concurrent writes may be ordered differently by different clients. Exhibit 7.2: two concurrent writes seen by two readers in opposite orders — causal, since no happens-before edge connects the writes, but not sequential, since one story must order them once. Implemented with Chapter 2’s causal-delivery machinery, without consensus or quorum round trips, it is the strongest model available under partition; it cannot arbitrate a race between concurrent writes.

  • the session guarantees (Def. 7.8; Terry and colleagues 1994, from Bayou): four promises scoped to one session — read your writes (a client’s own write missing on refresh, served by a replica the write had not reached), monotonic reads (a value seen, then reverted), monotonic writes (a correction applied before the document it corrects), writes follow reads (an answer visible before its question). Each is one guard over session-local tokens rather than quorums: the session carries a read-set and a write-set of write identifiers, and a replica must have applied the relevant set before serving it, else it waits or redirects (Box 15). All four, on every session, yield causal consistency.

  • eventual consistency (Def. 7.9): if updates cease, all correct replicas eventually agree — a liveness property, where every other model here is a safety property. Prop. 7.4: any finite history is a prefix of some eventually consistent execution (stop updating, deliver everything, let every replica pick the same tie-break), so eventual consistency alone forbids no finite transcript whatsoever. The question for any brochure is eventual, plus what? — session guarantees, causality, or a staleness bound with a number.

Transactions and isolation: serializability, snapshot isolation, write skew.  A transaction is a bracketed program of reads and writes that must appear to execute as a unit. Committed transactions are serializable (Def. 7.11) iff some total order of them, run one at a time, gives every read the value it returned — no clause about real time, so last night’s snapshot is perfectly serializable; ask of “serializable”: as of when? Strict serializability requires that order to extend transaction real-time precedence: linearizability with whole transactions as the operations, and the strongest model on both axes. Snapshot isolation (Def. 7.12), multiversion concurrency control industrialized: a transaction reads the newest version of each item committed before its start point, plus its own writes, and commits iff no transaction committing between its start and its commit wrote an item it also wrote — first committer wins. Writes are checked for collision; the reads on which the transaction acted are validated against nothing, whence write skew (Prop. 7.6, Exhibit 7.3; Berenson and colleagues 1995). At least one of two doctors must be on call; each opens a transaction to go off call, reads the same snapshot showing two on call, passes the guard, and writes only its own row; the write sets are disjoint, first committer wins never fires, both commit, and nobody is on call — though no serial execution of the same guarded programs can empty the roster, since whichever runs second sees one on call. The shape: read several rows, decide on what was read, and write a row the invariant spans but the detector, watching write-write overlap only, does not connect to the reads. Three remedies, priced: the serializable refinement of snapshot isolation (serializable snapshot isolation) tracks read-write dependencies among concurrent transactions and aborts when they could close a cycle, the premium being false positives; materializing the conflict makes the contenders write a common row, so that first committer wins sees the fight; explicit locking of the rows read surrenders the concurrency. Doing nothing is not a remedy.

Join-semilattices, strong eventual consistency, and the standard objects.  Part Six makes ordering irrelevant. A join-semilattice (Def. 7.13) is a set of states with a join $\sqcup$ that is commutative, associative, and idempotent — the least upper bound of a partial order, $s$ lying below $t$ iff $s \sqcup t = t$. A state-based CRDT (conflict-free replicated data type; Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero, and Zawirski 2011) gives every update a contribution joined into its origin’s state, ships whole states over an anti-entropy channel with eventual, unordered, possibly duplicated delivery, and merges on arrival by join; no clocks, quorums, or leader. Thm. 7.7: every replica’s state is the join of the contributions of exactly the updates it has absorbed, because a join forgets the order and multiplicity of joining; so replicas that have absorbed the same updates, in any order, by any route, with any duplication, hold identical states, and all correct replicas converge once updates cease — strong eventual consistency, with no sibling versions handed to the application. The grow-only counter (Box 16): one tally per replica, each incrementing only its own entry; value the sum; merge the entrywise maximum, because the network duplicates and a maximum absorbs a repeat where a sum double-counts it. The positive-negative counter: two grow-only counters, value their difference, so that a decrement is itself a growth. The observed-remove set (Def. 7.14, Box 17): a grow-only set of (element, tag) pairs and a grow-only set of removed tags, joined by union; an add draws a globally fresh tag, a remove records precisely the tags it has observed for that element, and an element is present iff some tag of its is not removed. Hence add-wins (Prop. 7.8, Exhibit 7.4): a remove concurrent with an add has not seen the add’s tag and cannot record it — one may only remove what one has seen.

The CALM theorem.  A program is monotone (Def. 7.16) iff larger inputs never shrink its output — union, selection, join, transitive closure, but not negation, “the final state,” or “the winner.” CALM (Thm. 7.9; Hellerstein and Alvaro, proved by Ameloot, Neven, and Van den Bussche): a distributed program has a coordination-free, eventually consistent implementation iff it computes a monotone function of its inputs. Read a specification for final, exactly, never, the winner: each asserts a completed totality over inputs still in flight, and sealing a set across machines is agreement, with Chapter 3’s costs; everything phrased as accumulation can flow without it (Problem 7.11).

Sharp edges. 

  • locality holds for linearizability alone: a history is linearizable iff its restriction to each object is (Thm. 7.3), because every object’s linearization points lie on the one shared real-time line, so shards lawful apiece make a lawful store for free; sequentially consistent objects do not compose — two queues, each lawful alone, jointly explain nothing (Problem 7.6) — so per-object guarantees weaker than linearizability never add up to a system-wide one.

  • sequential implies causal: the folklore that causal and sequential consistency are incomparable is half wrong (Thm. 7.5); the genuine incomparability is with real-time causal, which adds $\prec$ to happens-before (Rem. 7.10, Problem 7.7).

  • linearizable is not serializable, nor the reverse: per-register linearizability with crosswise reads yields two transactions no serial order explains, and a serializable read of last night’s snapshot ignores the clock (Problem 7.9); a claim of “consistent” needs its axis named before its strength.

  • write skew’s signature: unclaimed usernames, linked account balances, paired keyholders — any rule that lives across rows while the detector watches rows alone (Problem 7.4); under snapshot isolation such invariants are the application’s to keep.

  • convergence is not correctness: the last-writer-wins register satisfies every hypothesis of Thm. 7.7 and converges, but by arbitration on wall-clock timestamps, which Chapter 2 showed cannot order events, discarding the losing write silently and forever (Rem. 7.15); choosing the object is choosing the verdicts.

  • CRDT metadata is pruned only with coordination: tags and removed-tag sets accrue and can be discarded only once every replica is known to have absorbed them — a global fact; the object is usable forever without agreement but slimmed only with it. And no semilattice arbitrates: an auction is agreement, at agreement’s price (Problem 7.12).

15.8 Chapter 8 — Transactions at a Distance

The one sentence.  Atomic commitment gives every participant a veto, and under asynchrony with crash-recovery two-phase commit is safe in every execution but no protocol can be non-blocking — a participant that has voted yes may have to wait for a crashed process to recover — so every modern design applies one of three remedies: relocate coordinator and participants onto consensus groups (Spanner), shrink the in-doubt window until any bystander can resolve it (Percolator), or renegotiate what is promised, by agreeing on inputs and executing deterministically (Calvin) or by trading isolation for compensations (sagas).

The system models.  For the specification, two-phase commit, and the blocking theorem (Defs. 8.1–8.5, Thms. 8.1–8.2): asynchronous message passing over fair-loss links with retransmission beneath; crash-recovery processes with stable storage, recovering after unbounded delay or never; no failure detector, so silence and death are indistinguishable; one coordinator, $n$ participants, the transaction’s provisional work and locks already in place. For Spanner (Def. 8.6, Thm. 8.4), the TrueTime axiom: an absolute time $t_{\mathrm{abs}}$ exists, and every call of $TT.\mathrm{now}()$ returns an interval $[e, l]$ with $e \le t_{\mathrm{abs}} \le l$ at the instant of the call. For sagas (Def. 8.8, Prop. 8.5), the weakest model in the chapter: each step and each compensation is a local transaction, atomic and durable at its own service, and nothing spans two steps.

The specification, and the veto.  One transaction has done provisional work at several participants, and someone must decide: all of it permanent, or none. Atomic commitment (Def. 8.1): each participant votes yes or no; each participant and the coordinator may decide commit or abort. AC1 (agreement): no two processes decide differently. AC2 (validity): any no vote forbids commit, and a failure-free unanimous yes compels it. AC3 (stability): a decision is never changed. AC4 (termination), in two grades: weak — every participant decides once all failures are repaired — which two-phase commit delivers; and non-blocking — every correct participant decides while other processes remain crashed — the prize. The resemblance to consensus misleads (Rem. 8.2): consensus validity accepts any proposed value, whereas AC2’s first half is unanimity in one direction, which gives every participant a veto; a consensus majority outvotes a silent minority, but a commit protocol cannot, because the silent party may hold a no, or evidence of a commit already decided. And a yes is not an opinion but a certificate of staged durability — everything needed to commit through any crash already forced to stable storage — so a participant that answers yes from memory alone has counterfeited it.

Two-phase commit (Box 18).  Phase one: the coordinator sends prepare; a participant that cannot commit replies no and aborts at once, and one that can forces its staged work and its vote to the write-ahead log before replying yes — bound from that instant, no unilateral abort on any timeout, because the yes may already be part of a commit decided elsewhere. Phase two: on unanimous yes the coordinator forces commit to its own log — the decision is that forced write, not the announcement — and only then tells the participants, retransmitting until each acknowledges; any no, or an expired timer, means abort. Two forced writes on the critical path, each vote and the decision (Exhibit 8.1). Two lawful economies: presumed abort — the coordinator may abort freely before logging a decision, so the absence of a record means abort by convention and the failure paths save their forced writes (Problem 8.2) — and the read-only vote, by which a participant that wrote nothing releases its locks and leaves before the decision (Problem 8.3). The recovery matrix is the protocol (Problem 8.1): on waking, each process reads its own log (Def. 8.3); a participant still working aborts; one that is prepared is in doubt — it queries the coordinator and its fellows, holds its locks, and waits, no rule for this state deciding; a coordinator with no decision record presumes abort, and one with a commit record re-announces it. Thm. 8.1: AC1–AC3 hold through any sequence of crashes and recoveries, and weak AC4, because a commit record exists only on unanimous votes, every participant decision copies the coordinator’s first forced decision record, and no rule overwrites one; the protocol is not non-blocking.

The blocking theorem.  Thm. 8.2 (Gray, Skeen): no atomic commitment protocol in this model is non-blocking — every protocol satisfying AC1–AC3 has a reachable configuration in which a correct, live participant has voted yes and can decide neither commit nor abort until some crashed process recovers, holding its locks meanwhile. The proof is an indistinguishability argument with two executions (Exhibit 8.2), each beginning with the same prefix of a failure-free run, cut just after one participant $P$ forces its yes, after which $P$ receives no further messages. Execution one: the coordinator and the other participant decide commit, then both crash, the message to $P$ still in flight — a decision exists, permanent by AC3. Execution two: their messages are delayed and both crash before deciding anything — no decision exists. $P$'s local history is identical in both, and a protocol is a function from local history to action: deciding abort contradicts execution one (AC1); deciding commit contradicts execution two, where the recovering coordinator may lawfully abort and where, in the variant in which the other participant voted no, AC2 forbids it outright. Only waiting survives, until a crashed decision-holder recovers. Cooperative termination (Rem. 8.4) confines the wait without removing it: an in-doubt participant queries its fellows, one holding a decision repeats it, and one that has not yet voted may vote no and release the querent — which fails exactly when every reachable fellow is itself prepared and ignorant and every decision-holder is down, the configuration the proof constructs.

Three-phase commit.  Skeen’s protocol (1981; Def. 8.5) forbids the fatal adjacency — a state from which both commit and abort remain reachable — by inserting a pre-commit state between prepared and committed, so that a stranded participant still uncertain knows nobody can have committed and one in pre-commit knows nobody can still be uncertain; hence the termination rule under a new coordinator: if any canvassed participant is committed, commit all; if any is still uncertain, abort all; if all reachable are in pre-commit, complete the commit. Prop. 8.3: non-blocking in a synchronous crash-stop model with reliable bounded delivery and no partitions; under a partition, one side sees pre-commit and commits while the other sees uncertainty and lawfully aborts — the split verdict (Problem 8.7) — and the extra round is paid on every failure-free transaction. Nobody runs it. The diagnosis worth keeping: the enemy was never the number of phases but the assumption that silence can be told from death; what three-phase commit wanted was a failure detector, and the honest form of that bargain was Chapter 4’s consensus all along.

The three modern remedies. 

  • relocate: run coordinator and participants as consensus groups — a vote is a committed entry in the participant’s replicated log, which a successor leader inherits with the promise; the coordinator’s crash triggers a leader election, and the successor reads the replicated log and completes the protocol. The in-doubt window shrinks to one election timeout and stalls only while a partition denies majorities; every phase now costs a consensus round rather than a disk write. (Paxos Commit, the purists’ version, makes each vote its own Paxos instance: Problem 8.11.) Spanner: every shard a Paxos group, two-phase commit over Paxos groups, and external consistency — strict serializability — obtained from bounded clock uncertainty. TrueTime returns an interval $[e, l]$ containing the true instant (Def. 8.6); the commit stamp is the latest edge $l$, and commit-wait holds the announcement and the locks until a later call’s $e$ passes it (Box 19, Exhibit 8.3). Thm. 8.4: the wait ensures the announcement’s true time exceeds the stamp, and any transaction starting after the announcement takes as its stamp a later call’s latest edge, which lies above that true time — so timestamp order extends real-time precedence, with no message between the two coordinators. Truthful stamps make the lockless read at any timestamp a consistent snapshot obtained from the clocks rather than from a snapshot protocol, served by any replica whose safe time — the watermark below which its log is known complete — has passed the stamp.

  • shrink: Percolator dissolves the coordinator into the data (Box 20): every row carries a lock column and a write column; one row is designated primary, and its lock cell is the transaction’s public record; commit is one single-row atomic exchange there — erase the lock, write the commit record — and every other lock names the primary. A stranded lock is resolved by any bystander that encounters it: roll forward if the primary shows a commit record, erase the primary lock (a binding abort) if not (Problem 8.9). The in-doubt window becomes a chore, found by encounter and billed to bystanders — suited to a batch workload, not a checkout path.

  • renegotiate: Calvin renegotiates the machinery — agree on inputs, sequence all transactions through a replicated log, execute deterministically everywhere with locks granted in the published order: no votes mid-flight, so the blocking theorem is starved, at the price of declared read and write sets — the interactive transaction surrendered. Sagas renegotiate the promise (Def. 8.8): a chain of local transactions $T_1, \dots, T_n$ with written compensations $C_i$, run forward and, on failure, compensated in reverse; atomicity survives in business effect, and isolation is abandoned openly, since between a step and its compensation the intermediate state is visible to everyone (Prop. 8.5). The discipline: fences on interim states, the single uncompensable pivot ordered last with only retriable steps after it, and the anomaly budget — which interim states may be seen, by whom, at what cost — signed in writing (Problem 8.8; Problem 8.5 classifies all five designs by remedy and residual in-doubt window).

Sharp edges. 

  • the decision is the forced log record, not the announcement: written before anyone is told; reverse that order and a coordinator crash un-decides a transaction some participant has already applied — AC1’s proof leans exactly there (Thm. 8.1).

  • after yes, no timeout is safe: a participant configured to abort on timeout after voting yes is heuristic abort — one participant commits, the other rolls back, and the ledgers never reconcile — and no timeout value fixes it, since the two executions of Thm. 8.2 are indistinguishable at every finite patience (Problem 8.6). The in-doubt transaction console in every mature transaction manager is the theorem’s signature in the product.

  • the stamp is the latest edge, and the wait is the bill: commit-wait costs one uncertainty interval per commit, paid by every writer in latency; widen the interval and the whole system slows (Problem 8.4). Hybrid logical clocks (Def. 8.7) keep causal order without the bound — and without the bound there is no commit-wait proof; the residue is paid in retries and tail latency, not hardware.

  • a compensation is a new transaction, not an undo: it cannot reach what observers did with what they read between step and compensation — a dirty read of business later retracted, made durable by third parties (Exhibit 8.4); fences and pivot placement are design remedies, not protocol ones.

15.9 Chapter 9 — Partitioning, or Where Things Live

The one sentence.  Data that outgrows one machine — because no larger machine is sold at a sane price, because one machine is one failure domain, or because one machine is in one place and the speed of light bills every distant client — is cut into shards, and a partitioning scheme is then the choice of which fan-out to pay (reads, writes, or rebalancing), under one rule: a key’s address must never depend on a number that changes when the fleet changes size.

Partitioning and replication.  Replication (Chapter 6) makes copies of the same shard for durability and availability; partitioning cuts different pieces for capacity. Every serious system does both, with no shard’s replicas sharing a rack, a switch, or a failure domain, and the shard is the unit of placement, movement, and recovery. Two accounts on one shard make a transfer a local transaction; on different shards it needs atomic commitment (Chapter 8). No scheme makes reads, writes, and rebalances all cheap: the trade recurs as hash against range, local against global indexes, eager against lazy balancing, and pull against push routing, and the resolution is always the scheme whose expensive operation the business performs least.

Range, hash, compound keys, and secondary indexes.  Range partitioning cuts the ordered key line into contiguous ranges. Its virtue is locality: a scan touches one shard or a few adjacent ones. Its failure is the write hot spot of a monotonic key: a timestamp key sends every write to the newest range, on one shard, and monotonic keys and seasonal peaks arrive together; the remedies — salting the key with a prefix, splitting the tail eagerly, writing round-robin — each give up part of the order. Hash partitioning places by a fixed hash of the key. Its virtue is uniformity, which dissolves the hot spot; its price is locality destroyed, a range scan becoming a scatter-gather across every shard. Hash is the scheme of point lookups, and every store of the Dynamo school (Chapter 6, leaderless replication) is hash-partitioned; but uniformity is over keys, not popularity (the hot key, below). The compound key is the standard compromise: hash the outer component and range the inner, so point lookups and per-customer scans land on one shard and only the fleet-wide scan pays scatter-gather. Secondary indexes repeat the trade: a local index, kept per shard, is written cheaply with its data but read by scatter-gather across every shard; a global index, itself partitioned by the indexed value, is read on one shard but written with a fan-out to the data shard and the index shard, by a distributed transaction (Chapter 8) or asynchronously and lagging. Local indexes tax reads; global indexes tax writes (Problem 9.4).

Consistent hashing: the ring, its variance, and virtual nodes.  Bend the hash space into the unit circle $[0,1)$ and place each server on it by hashing its name; a key hashes to a point on the same circle and walks clockwise, and the first server it meets — its successor — is its home, each server owning the arc ending at its position (Def. 9.1, Box 21, Exhibit 9.1). The probabilistic results run in one model: hash values are independent uniform random points, keys are not chosen adversarially, and load is counted in keys — the model covers where keys live, not how often they are visited. Thm. 9.2 (balance): each server’s share has mean $1/n$ exactly; with $k$ virtual nodes per server the share is $\mathrm{Beta}(k,(n-1)k)$, with relative standard deviation $\sqrt{(n-1)/(nk+1)} \approx 1/\sqrt{k}$ — a hundred virtual nodes hold typical imbalance to about ten percent, ten thousand to one percent. Thm. 9.3 (movement): monotonicity — a joining server takes exactly the arc between its predecessor’s position and its own (Exhibit 9.2), and since incumbent positions did not move, no key ever moves between two incumbents; a join moves an expected $1/(n{+}1)$ of the keys, all into the newcomer; a leave moves exactly the departed server’s keys, expected $1/n$, each to the next surviving token clockwise. Mean share is not high probability: by Prop. 9.4 the largest of $m$ arcs exceeds $c \ln m / m$ with probability at most $m^{1-c}$, and the expected largest arc is of order $\ln m / m$, so with one token per server the unluckiest server holds about $\ln n$ times the fair share. Departure is worse: a single-token server’s whole arc lands on one successor, whose share becomes $\mathrm{Beta}(2, n-2)$ with mean $2/n$ — doubled, under failure (Problem 9.8). Virtual nodes, $k$ tokens per server, are the repair: shares average out as $1/\sqrt{k}$, a departing server’s $k$ arcs fall to up to $k$ successors, each gaining about a $1/k$ fraction of its own share, and capacity is priced in virtual nodes. The pure ring caps nobody; consistent hashing with bounded loads (Rem. 9.2) adds the cap, forwarding a key whose successor is full to the next uncapped server.

Rendezvous hashing, the comparison, and Chord.  Rendezvous hashing (highest-random-weight hashing; Def. 9.3, Box 22) needs no ring: for key $x$ and servers $s_1, \dots, s_n$ compute scores $h(x, s_1), \dots, h(x, s_n)$ and place $x$ at the highest scorer. Thm. 9.5: the $n$ scores for a key are independent and identically distributed, so each server wins it with probability exactly $1/n$ — exact balance, no virtual nodes; on a join a key moves if and only if the newcomer’s score is the new maximum, probability exactly $1/(n{+}1)$, and it moves to the newcomer; on a leave exactly the departed server’s keys move, each to its runner-up.

Chord (Rem. 9.4) finds any key’s home with no roster at all, by fingers — pointers to the first node at distance at least $1/2$, $1/4$, $1/8, \dots$ around the ring — in $O(\log m)$ hops (Problem 9.10); but inside the datacentre membership is a file and the whole map is megabytes, so Chord distributes the map because it has no alternative, and the datacentre centralizes the map because it can.

The placement map and routing.  Two designs hold the map (Part Five). The directory (Bigtable, Exhibit 9.3): placement is explicit state, held in a directory under a master and guarded by the coordination service; the map from tablets (contiguous ranges) to servers is itself a table, rooted in one root tablet whose address Chubby holds, so a cold lookup takes three steps — Chubby, the root tablet, the metadata tablet, then the tablet server — all cacheable. Each tablet server holds its tablet under a lease (Chapter 5), so the old server’s authority expires before the new one’s begins; map changes are pushed by watches. This buys placement as policy and an authoritative record — “who owns this key” is answered by a service with a name and a log — and costs a cold-path directory and a master to elect and fence. The gossiped ring (Cassandra): the ring is the map and membership spreads by gossip (Chapter 6) — no master, any node answers any client; but arithmetic expresses no policy, and membership is only eventually consistent, so two nodes can briefly both believe they own an arc, writes are accepted under both beliefs, and read repair, anti-entropy, and hinted handoff reconcile them afterward. The newest large systems build directory-style; the gossiped ring remains right where a stale map costs a miss, not a lost write. However the map is consulted — by a dumb client sending any request to any node, a proxy tier holding the map, or a smart client holding the map itself — the map is state, so it is versioned: the routing epoch (Def. 9.5, Box 23). The map carries a version $e$, increased on every ownership change; a client stamps each request with the epoch of the map it used; a server rejects any request for a key it does not currently own, answering with the current epoch; and handover is fenced, the old owner’s ownership ending strictly before the new owner’s begins. Prop. 9.6: every applied write is then applied by the key’s owner, and a stale map costs one extra round trip. Without the check, a routine rebalance loses a write: the arc moves from server $A$ (epoch three) to server $B$ (epoch four); a client holding the epoch-three map writes key $x$ to $A$, which applies and acknowledges; every later reader consults the current map and reads $x$ at $B$, which never saw it — acknowledged, then visible to no future read (Problem 9.7, Exhibit 9.4). The routing epoch is the fencing token (Chapter 5) applied to placement: a stale map is harmless only if the servers refuse to honour it — at the server, because a client with a stale map does not know it is stale.

Rebalancing.  Moving data is load on the customers’ own disks and network (Part Six), so every move gets a budget: megabytes per second, throttled, preemptible. A polite drain of a few terabytes takes hours, so shards are sized in movable tens of gigabytes, and the capacity question is how many machines can be mid-drain while surviving the next failure. The rebalancing storm, a rebalancing feedback loop: a slow disk reads as dead — a slow node being indistinguishable from a crashed one — the balancer moves its shards, the recipients slow under the copying and are moved in turn, and the loop sustains itself after the disk is replaced. The dampers are hysteresis (unhealthy for minutes, not moments, before shards move), rate limits on concurrent movement, a global view (many nodes degrading at once means stand down, not move faster), and a rehearsed switch that freezes all rebalancing — for the balancer is a control loop, and undamped control loops oscillate (metastability, Chapter 11). Pre-split what will be large, and divide a warming range while still comfortable, at the peak of the access histogram, never at the arithmetic midpoint (Problem 9.6).

The hot key.  The hot key defeats the premise that load, like keys, is divisible: the hash spreads keys, not popularity, and one key draws a fleet’s worth of traffic to one arc, one range, one machine. The remedies, in order of increasing specialization: cache it, with stampede discipline — when the cached copy expires, thousands of clients miss together (the cache stampede, or thundering herd), so request coalescing or stale-while-revalidate lets one request refresh while the rest wait or take the stale copy, and a miss is handled once, not once per waiting client; split the reads — replicate the hot shard beyond the standing replication factor and spread reads across the copies, since reads divide even when keys do not; salt the writes — a hundred sub-counters under salted names, each write choosing a salt at random and each read summing the hundred rows, which is Chapter 7’s grow-only counter with summation as its merge; and special-case it past some fame — a dedicated fleet, a broadcast tier that pushes the result outward, and a standing census of the hottest keys to find it first.

Sharp edges. 

  • the placement hash must never change: an upgraded hash library that disagrees with the old about where keys live reshuffles the fleet exactly as a resize under hash modulo $N$ does, which moves $N/(N{+}1)$ of the keys against the ring’s $1/(N{+}1)$ (Problem 9.3).

  • expectation is not high probability: mean share $1/n$, but the largest single-token arc runs to order $\ln n / n$ (Prop. 9.4); the overloaded server is the unlucky one, not the mean.

  • virtual nodes matter most at departure: a single-token server’s death doubles one successor’s share under failure (Problem 9.8); steady-state variance is a nuisance, the doubling an outage.

  • corrections assume the map settles: a rejected client reaches the owner only if ownership changes finitely often during its attempt; an arc oscillating between servers faster than the client refreshes starves it — the routing counterpart of the rebalancing storm (Problem 9.9).

  • anti-entropy does not recover a stranded write: read repair and anti-entropy reconcile the replicas of one shard, and a server that has ceded an arc is no longer one of them, so a write applied at the ceded server is never carried to the owner (Problem 9.7).

15.10 Chapter 10 — The Log and the Dataflow

The one sentence.  The log — an append-only sequence of records, totally ordered within itself and durably replayable — is the structure along which a system’s data machinery can be taken apart and reassembled: wherever the input is replayable and the computation deterministic, recovery by re-execution replaces redundant copies of the output, and “exactly-once” is achievable only as an effect (idempotence, deduplication, transactional sinks), never as a delivery property of the channel.

Recovery by recomputation: GFS, MapReduce, Spark.  The Google File System (2003) treats failure as the normal operating condition: a dead chunk’s replicas are re-spread automatically, in the background, and that posture survives in every storage system since. MapReduce (2004) restricts the programmer to two pure functions, a map applied record by record and a reduce applied per key to grouped intermediate records, with a shuffle between them regrouping every record by key; because pure functions commute with re-execution, a failed task is simply run again, and a straggler — a machine slowed rather than dead — is raced against a duplicate backup task, the first to finish winning. Spark (2012) recomputes a lost partition from its lineage, the chain of pure transformations that produced it, rather than replicating bytes: cheap across a narrow dependency (each output partition from one input partition), expensive past a shuffle, where every output partition depends on every input — whence checkpointing, which truncates the lineage at the wide boundaries. One doctrine, three choices of what to materialize: every stage’s output, the lineage alone, or snapshotted state (Rem. 10.5, Problem 10.8).

Kafka: the log as a service.  Kafka offers Chapter 5’s replicated log alone: a topic is a set of partitions, each a log (Def. 10.1) replicated leader-and-followers and kept sequential on disk, so that retention costs disk space rather than throughput. Two refusals define it. No global order: order holds within one partition and nowhere else, so choose the key so that records whose order matters share a partition, and ask of any demand for global order ordered for whom? No delivery bookkeeping: records sit immutably at their offsets for the retention period, and a consumer group holds one cursor per partition — a bookmark, not a receipt — so consuming is reading rather than taking, and the rewind is the recovery story: a consumer with a bug deployed on Friday afternoon writes bad output all weekend; fix the bug, reset the cursors to Friday afternoon, replay the weekend through the corrected code into a fresh view, and cut over (Problem 10.2). A compacted topic keeps at least the newest record per key, deletions as null-body tombstones cleared only after every reader has seen them (Def. 10.1), and is therefore current state plus a subscription to its changes — the database turned inside out: the log is the primary artifact, and every table, cache, and index is a materialized view, a consumer group with a cursor, rebuilt by replay from offset zero whenever its logic changes.

Event time, processing time, and watermarks.  Records carry event time (when the thing happened) and processing time (when the record arrived); windows — tumbling, sliding, session — group by event time, which the network does not deliver in order: a phone on a train loses signal at four, buffers two hours of purchases stamped four-oh-five, four-twelve, four-forty, and delivers them at six (Exhibit 10.1). The watermark (Def. 10.2) closes a window on a stated assumption rather than a certainty: a watermark $w$ at a point in the dataflow asserts that no record with event time below $w$ is still to come; an operator with several inputs propagates the minimum of its inputs’ watermarks; a window $[a,b)$ may close once the local watermark passes $b$. Prop. 10.1: with sources honouring that promise and minimum propagation, closure never precedes a window’s records — by induction on depth in the graph, FIFO channels delivering records ahead of the watermark behind them — and with heuristic sources that break the promise at a measured rate, the downstream failure rate is bounded by the sources’ declared rates: a failure detector for time, its accuracy bought with waiting. Late records are admitted within an allowed lateness as keyed corrections, or dropped with a metric.

Exactly-once delivery versus exactly-once effect.  Delivery: impossible — the final acknowledgment of any exchange may be lost, so a sender must resend, risking twice, or stop, risking never (Chapter 1’s Two Generals); channels stay at-least-once. Effect — the state advancing as if each record were processed once, however often it arrives — is routinely achieved by three mechanisms (Box 24, Lemma 10.2):

  • idempotence: the operation absorbs repetition — an overwrite keyed by record identity; the craft is rewriting an operation into a recorded fact: not “increment the count” but “record the event; the count is the size of the set” (Problem 10.3);

  • deduplication: a dedup ledger of applied identifiers, checked before and enrolled atomically with each application, persisted with the state it protects, and retained past the retry horizon;

  • transactional sinks: effect and cursor advance in one commit — a crash before it leaves neither done, a crash after leaves both, and the window has no interior.

Lemma 10.2 states the first two precisely: under at-least-once delivery with retry horizon $T$, the final state equals the exactly-once state if the application is idempotent, or if the ledger is enrolled atomically with each application and retains identifiers for at least $T$ — and fails if the ledger is persisted separately or expires early. Kafka assembles all three plus Chapter 5’s fencing token: a producer’s epoch refuses the zombie producer instance that resumes after being presumed dead, per-partition sequence numbers make append-once, and a transaction coordinator’s prepare and commit markers — a two-phase commit conducted inside the log — commit appends and the consumer’s cursor advance together, priced in coordinator traffic and a visibility lag for read-committed consumers, who wait at the oldest open transaction. The sensible default is idempotent at-least-once; transactions are for the payment paths, and the guarantee stops at the boundary of the log: an email sent or a payment initiated lies outside it, and correctness at the ends must be arranged at the ends (the end-to-end argument, Chapter 11).

Consistent snapshots, barrier snapshotting, and recovery.  A streaming job (Flink; Carbone and colleagues, 2015) is an acyclic dataflow of sources reading logs, operators holding state, and sinks; state accumulated over weeks cannot be recomputed from the beginning of the input, so recovery wants a consistent snapshot: source offsets and operator states at one logical instant, all of epoch $\le k$ and none of epoch $> k$, where epoch $k$ is the input between barriers $b_{k-1}$ and $b_k$ (Def. 10.4; Exhibit 10.2 shows snapshots on private schedules, the source at offset one thousand and the operator with totals to twelve hundred, so that replay counts two hundred records twice). The mechanism (Box 25, Exhibit 10.3): a barrier rides each source stream in-band; an operator aligns — holding the inputs the barrier has reached and draining the rest — then snapshots its state asynchronously, forwards the barrier, and resumes; alignment costs bounded backpressure, and the unaligned variant snapshots at the first barrier and records the in-flight records instead. Thm. 10.3: FIFO channels cut every input cleanly into epochs and alignment makes each operator absorb exactly the pre-barrier prefix of every input, so the snapshot is the consistent cut at the barrier. Recovery is restore, rewind, replay: exact by determinism, the replayed suffix being byte-identical and the operators deterministic, and exactly-once at the sinks by Box 24 (Cor. 10.4); without such sinks, the effects between the snapshot and the crash fire again — a checkpoint at four-thirty-nine, a wire transfer at four-forty, a crash, and a second transfer on replay (Exhibit 10.4, Problem 10.7). This is the Chandy–Lamport algorithm of 1985 specialized to dataflow, set out clause by clause in Part Five: rewindable logs replace channel recording, and the unaligned variant restores that clause verbatim (Problem 10.10). Recovery needs three things together — replayable input, deterministic-enough compute (time from records and watermarks, randomness seeded by record identity, external calls behind Box 24), and snapshotted state — and lacking any one recovers nothing: no input to replay, a different state on replay, or replay from the beginning of time.

Lambda, Kappa, and the outbox pattern.  The Lambda architecture computed every metric twice — a batch layer recomputing nightly, a speed layer approximating the last hours, a serving layer stitching them — because early stream processors could not be trusted; Kappa keeps one pipeline and, when the logic changes, replays the log through the new code into a new view; the audit question is whether the second codebase compensates for a limitation that still exists. Change-data-capture publishes the database’s own commit log in commit order, and the outbox pattern answers the dual-write anomaly — a row committed and an event published with no transaction spanning them, so that a crash between leaves an orphan (row without event) or a ghost (event without row): write the announcement as an outbox row committed atomically with the business row, relay it at-least-once from the commit log, and deduplicate downstream. Prop. 10.5: exactly one announcement per committed event, in commit order — no ghosts, since the row exists only if its transaction committed; no orphans, since every committed row is in the log the relay reads in order, resuming from its own cursor after a crash (Problem 10.5).

Sharp edges. 

  • delivery versus effect: no channel delivers exactly once; what can be built is effect-level equality within the scope of the transactional machinery, and an effect outside it — a wire transfer, an email — fires again on replay unless the far end deduplicates: the double payment (Exhibit 10.4, Problem 10.7).

  • the cursor-commit window: effect performed, cursor uncommitted, crash: the restart re-reads the record, by design; the window closes only when effect and cursor advance commit together, or the effect absorbs the repeat.

  • dedup ledger conditions: persisted atomically with the state it protects, or it merely relocates the window; atomic against concurrent duplicates; retained past the retry horizon, or the tail of retries is applied as new records (Problem 10.1).

  • the extent of ordering: one partition, no further; the partition count bounds a consumer group’s useful parallelism — twelve partitions feed at most twelve readers — and a true global sequence is a consensus problem, Chapter 4’s business, priced accordingly.

  • compaction semantics: at least the newest record per key, so superseded records may linger; replay from offset zero yields current state, not history; tombstones are applied as deletes and cleared only after every enrolled reader has passed, or replay brings the deleted key back.

  • idle inputs stall the watermark: one idle input freezes every downstream window (idle-source timeouts, watermark-lag alarms, Problem 10.4); and late corrections make window results keyed updates, so the output wants a compacted log too.

  • what the log does not provide: no queries (tables and indexes are derived from the log, never replaced by it), no arbitration (appending records a conflict without resolving it; uniqueness and locks remain Chapter 4’s business), and no multi-key invariants (Chapter 8’s atomic commitment, or a redesign per Chapter 7).

15.11 Chapter 11 — The Engineering of Failure

The one sentence.  Mature systems are rarely killed by their failures; they are killed by their reactions to their failures — the timeout, the retry, the queue, and the health check, each installed in the name of resilience and each able, left ungoverned, to sustain an outage long after its cause is cured, as DynamoDB’s September 2015 network disruption of minutes became an outage of hours.

Timeouts and the phi-accrual failure detector.  An asynchronous system cannot tell a crashed process from a slow one (Chapter 3), so liveness is settled by a timeout, and a timeout is not a measurement but a bet on the tail of the silence distribution: too short, and a slow but living machine is declared dead (Chapter 5’s fencing makes that survivable); too long, and the system idles waiting on a dead one. Derive the number from observed latency rather than inherit it, and nest it inside the caller’s patience. Three kinds answer three questions: the connection timeout prices a cheap local question and is short; the request timeout prices the work and must nest inside the caller’s budget; the lease (Chapter 5) is a timeout whose expiry transfers authority — the longest, and the one that demands fencing. The phi-accrual detector (Hayashibara and colleagues, 2004) returns a suspicion level rather than a verdict: per peer, a sliding window of heartbeat inter-arrival times estimates their distribution $F$, and a silence of length $t$ scores $\varphi(t) = -\log_{10}(1 - F(t))$, so that phi three means a silence this long happens one time in a thousand (Def. 11.1); each consumer acts at its own threshold $\Phi$ and misfires on roughly the fraction $10^{-\Phi}$ of silences, so one evidence stream serves cheap actions at a low phi and failover at a high one (Problem 11.2). Prop. 11.1: $\varphi$ is nondecreasing, thresholds order consistently, and the false-alarm rate at fixed $\Phi$ is invariant under re-estimation of $F$ — congestion widens the rhythm and the same silence scores lower, so the detector re-prices itself to network conditions, which a fixed timeout cannot. What no detector changes: a pause and a death give the same evidence at every $\Phi$ (Chapter 3), so downstream machinery must still fence (Chapter 5); and a per-peer detector cannot see correlated silence, a judgment for fleet-level logic.

Retry amplification and retry discipline.  Independent per-tier attempt caps, chosen by teams who never met, multiply down the stack: with three tiers each allowing three attempts, a briefly slow storage tier sees three requests for each one intended from the middle tier’s retries and nine from the top tier’s, twenty-seven with an edge proxy above (Exhibit 11.1); Prop. 11.2, by induction on depth, bounds the attempts at tier $i$ by $r^{i}$, exponential in depth, and no local review can catch a product assembled across teams — the repair must bind every tier by construction. Box 26, in six steps. Classify: a timeout, a connection refused, or a try-again-later is transient and retriable within budget; not permitted, not found, or malformed is permanent and never retried; an overload rejection is obeyed, the server saying how long to back off. Budget: retries at most a fraction $b$ (say a tenth) of first-attempt volume per window, then fail fast; Prop. 11.4, by induction on tiers, bounds tier $i$'s volume at $(1{+}b)^{i}$ times the top’s — three tiers down at a tenth, about a third again, against twenty-seven-fold uncapped — whatever the failure pattern (Problem 11.1). Schedule: exponential backoff with full jitter, each delay drawn uniformly between zero and the backoff ceiling; backoff alone returns $n$ simultaneously failed clients in one instant, while jitter spreads them over the window $W$ at rate $n/W$ — ten thousand clients over two seconds become five thousand per second (Prop. 11.3) — and it is the peak, not the total, that overwhelms a server. Identify: every mutating request carries an idempotency key and the far end deduplicates (Chapter 10); no retry and no hedge without idempotence. Own: one designated layer per call path retries, usually the one nearest the user; the tiers beneath fail fast upward. Hedge, for the latency tail only: after the ninety-ninth-percentile delay, duplicate a slow request to another replica, take the first answer, cancel the loser; idempotent calls only.

Queues and load shedding.  A bounded queue smooths bursts; an unbounded queue is an unbounded promise. Waiting time is queue length divided by service rate, so the queue length is the latency: a tenth over capacity and the queue grows without limit, latency climbs into minutes, and timed-out callers retry, depositing fresh copies of requests still queued, until the whole capacity serves callers who hung up — no request refused, none served. Hence healthy systems refuse work: bound the queue at no more than service rate times deadline, since a deeper position holds a request that will expire in line, and past the bound shed early (at admission, before parsing), cheaply (the rejection path the fastest code in the system), and by priority (declared at the edge; otherwise shedding is by lottery). Degrade (brownout) before refusing, and under overload serve newest-first: first-come-first-served past the deadline horizon serves the departed first.

Deadline propagation and the circuit breaker.  Every request enters with a patience that travels with it as a remaining budget, decremented at every hop (Def. 11.2): a tier receiving an exhausted budget rejects instantly, and otherwise passes downstream the budget less its own elapsed time and a margin, so expired work is dropped where it stands, with a metric (Problem 11.3). Prop. 11.5, by induction on hops: no tier works after the originator’s patience has expired, up to clock-rate drift over the request’s lifetime; budgets are durations, not wall-clock times, so clock skew cancels. The circuit breaker (Box 27): closed, calls flow and failures are scored on a sliding window; open, once the threshold is crossed, every call fails instantly and locally for a cooling period; half-open, one probe call — success closes the breaker, failure reopens it. It is a failure detector driving an admission decision with hysteresis against flapping, failing fast and locally: when storage drowns, ten thousand clients each independently stop calling it within seconds. Box 28 collects the lifecycle, from stamping deadline and priority at the edge to gradual readmission on recovery.

Metastable failure, feedback loops, and their governors.  Def. 11.3 (Bronson and colleagues, 2021): a transient trigger moves the system into a degraded state, and a feedback loop — reaction load generated by the degradation itself — sustains that state with the trigger gone. The signature is hysteresis in the goodput curve (Exhibit 11.2): goodput climbs with offered load, flattens at capacity, then bends downward as reaction load consumes capacity, and reducing the load to a level formerly served with margin does not recover it; recovery breaks the loop from outside and readmits gradually, below the load at which the loop ignites — as DynamoDB’s operators did, throttling the storage fleet’s renewals to the metadata service and readmitting gradually, hours after the network disruption that started the loop had passed (Exhibit 11.3). The chapter’s simple model (Prop. 11.6): one service of capacity $C$, offered load $L < C$, and unserved work retried, so that total demand satisfies $T = L + \gamma \max(0, T - C)$ with $\gamma$ the reaction gain (retries per unserved request, net of abandonment). For $\gamma \le 1$ no degraded equilibrium exists; for $\gamma > 1$ a transient past capacity settles demand at $T^{*} = (L - \gamma C)/(1 - \gamma) > C$, even at loads formerly served with margin. Added capacity moves the peak; only driving $\gamma$ below one (budgets, jitter, abandonment, admission control) removes the degraded equilibrium (Problem 11.9 refines the model). Every loop has one skeleton — the system’s response to degradation is itself load of the kind that degrades it — and each takes its governor: budget and jitter for the retry storm; leniency under load and a disruption budget (at most so many members removed per minute on health verdicts) for health-check churn; jittered expiries, single-flight coalescing (one origin fetch per key, every concurrent miss waiting for it), serve-stale-while-revalidating, negative caching, and prewarming for the cache stampede (Problem 11.5); capped pools and reconnection backoff for the connection stampede; a minimum term between leader changes for election churn (Chapter 9); and beneath all, admission control on the shared dependency. The trigger is environmental and unavoidable; the loop is a design decision.

Verification methods.  Four instruments, none a substitute for another; a failure path that has never been exercised must be presumed not to work (Rem. 11.5). Model checking audits the design: a TLA+ specification states the system as states, transitions, invariants, and progress properties, and the checker visits every reachable state of a small instance — the bugs live in the interleavings — under every interleaving and failure the model permits; on Amazon’s production designs it found bugs needing thirty-five steps to manifest (Newcombe and colleagues, 2015), and the starred appendix is the on-ramp (Problem 11.10). The Jepsen method audits the implementation: operations generated randomly against a model of what the store claims, a nemesis process scheduling faults (partitions, pauses long enough to expire leases, clock jumps), and a checker searching the recorded history for a lawful explanation under Chapter 7’s definitions. Chaos engineering audits the whole fleet: faults injected on schedule under a declared hypothesis and blast-radius discipline — customer metrics, automatic abort, scope widened only as the hypothesis survives. Deterministic simulation testing (FoundationDB) audits all three at once for systems built inside it: the whole database runs in test in a single-threaded simulator, every timeout, delivery, and crash driven by one pseudo-random generator from one seed, so the same seed reproduces the same bug and a violated invariant’s seed is a replayable incident report; the price is that the simulator must own every source of nondeterminism. Beneath all four, distributed tracing — a request identifier propagated through every hop, each hop contributing a span; happens-before (Chapter 2) made visible: metrics say that something is wrong, traces where, logs what, and this chapter’s machinery — deadline stamps, retry counts, breaker states — why.

Sharp edges. 

  • one budget, three views: the timeout, the retry allowance, and the propagated deadline are one budget seen from three places; set it at the top and subdivide downward, so deeper timeouts shrink, never grow, or deep tiers labour for callers already gone (Problem 11.8).

  • classify before retrying: a permanent error retried is the same refusal repeated; a timeout on a non-idempotent call without an idempotency key leaves neither retrying nor abstaining safe — query whether the operation executed, or fail into reconciliation (Problem 11.4).

  • jitter every synchronized schedule: un-jittered cooling makes ten thousand circuit breakers probe as one when it expires; scope the breaker per dependency and endpoint, or one failing route opens it for every route; announce every transition, since a silently open breaker is a silently disconnected dependency (Box 27, Problem 11.7).

  • health checks fail both ways: an idle-thread probe certifies a drowning machine; a real-path probe under load, when every machine is slow but correct, drains the healthy-but-busy onto the healthy-but-busier, a loop whose gain exceeds one (Problem 11.6); between the two lies gray failure (Huang and colleagues): healthy by every measure the system takes, broken by every measure the customer takes — the checker and the customer watch different things, and the failure lives in the difference.

  • cache refresh locks and negative entries: the single-flight refresh lock must carry a lease, or a crashed refresher leaves the key permanently stale; a negative entry for a deleted item must outlive the window in which anti-entropy can revive it from a stale replica (Chapter 6’s tombstone rule; Problem 11.5).

  • a property of the link is not a property of the application (Rem. 11.4; Saltzer, Reed, and Clark, 1984): host receipt is not application action, hop-by-hop encryption leaves plaintext in every intermediate, and the transport cannot see the double click; a function that needs the endpoints’ knowledge cannot be provided by the communication system alone, so the ends must check regardless and the middle’s promises are performance, never correctness — routing, congestion control, and backpressure excepted, being the middle’s own. Ask of every proposal: which end checks this, and what happens when the middle lies?

15.12 Chapter 12 — Capstone: The Distributed Systems You Already Run

The one sentence.  A large training run — tens of thousands of accelerators, millions of lockstep rounds, hardware failures on a daily schedule — is a distributed system of exactly the kind Chapters 1 to 11 studied, described in a vocabulary borrowed from high-performance computing; this chapter proves no new impossibility and does the work of translation: each training-run concept is identified with the course concept it already is, the ring all-reduce is proved bandwidth-optimal, the checkpoint interval is derived, and elastic training is assembled from consensus, fencing, replayable input, and failure detection.

Training-run concepts in distributed-systems terms.  The mapping is an identity, not an analogy (Part One; tabled in full in §12.8). A training step is a barrier-synchronized round (Chapter 4); gradient accumulation is batching, several local rounds per round of agreement (Chapters 5 and 8); a checkpoint is a consistent snapshot whose alignment the barrier supplies (Chapters 2 and 10); a straggler is the slow machine that paces the round, answered by backup tasks and hedged requests (Chapters 10 and 11); parameter staleness is replication lag (Chapter 6), Chapter 7’s question of what is added to eventual consistency; elastic training is a view change with the epoch on every collective (Chapters 4 and 5); the data pipeline is the log and the feature store its materialized view (Chapter 10); the job scheduler is placement against failure domains (Chapter 9); loss-curve monitoring is the computation’s progress signal, Chapter 10’s watermark, and an invariant check on its health (Chapter 11); training-serving skew is an undeclared replica pair (Chapters 6 and 7). The entry that matters most: an all-reduce (Def. 12.1) — one vector at each of $n$ nodes, an associative and commutative reduction, every node ending with the sum of all $n$ — is agreement on a value determined symmetrically by all inputs, with no arbitration and no winner, so by Chapter 6’s race test (must competing values be chosen between?) it lies strictly below consensus and runs at wire speed; decisions proper — membership, leadership, which checkpoint is authoritative — go to the job controller, a small replicated consensus group (Problem 12.3: a maximum, being idempotent as well, is even a join-semilattice; choosing between two checkpoint manifests is a decision). The dividend: forty years of database and message-queue postmortems apply to the training cluster directly.

Data parallelism and the ring all-reduce.  Data parallelism (Part Two): every worker holds a full replica of the model (Chapter 6), computes a gradient on its own data shard (Chapter 9), and the one agreement per round is on the summed gradient; identical updates on identical weights then keep the replicas equal by determinism — state machine replication, Chapter 5 verbatim — provided the reduction order is fixed, since floating-point addition does not associate (Rem. 12.2). The parameter server (Li and colleagues, 2014) sums at a sharded central tier and pays fan-in: a thousand workers each move two gradients per round, the tier two thousand, and the tier’s bandwidth is a ceiling that does not rise as workers join. The ring all-reduce (Box 29, Exhibit 12.1) cuts each gradient of size $G$ into $n$ chunks: reduce-scatter, $n{-}1$ passes, each node sending its running-sum chunk to its successor and adding the one arriving from its predecessor, until each node holds one chunk fully summed; all-gather, $n{-}1$ more, circulating the finished chunks. Thm. 12.1: traffic per node $2(n{-}1)G/n < 2G$, wall time approaching $2G/\beta$ on links of $\beta$ bytes per second — the fleet size cancels (Problem 12.1), every link busy every pass, no node ever holding another’s full gradient. Thm. 12.2: no scheme beats it, since at generic inputs every node must receive the other $n{-}1$ contributions to every element, $(n{-}1)G/n$, and its own contribution must leave it, another $(n{-}1)G/n$; the ring meets that floor at every node, hence bandwidth-optimal (a counting argument; Problem 12.10 formalizes it). The ring also pays $2(n{-}1)\alpha$ in serialized hops at per-message latency $\alpha$, so small messages go by tree and mixed fleets by hierarchical rings that keep the heavy traffic on the short links (Rem. 12.3, Problem 12.6). The ring is a barrier in miniature — every pass waits for the slowest link and machine — and a topology that a member’s death breaks; the parameter server buys asynchrony and elasticity, paying in fan-in and staleness. Modern systems deploy both, per layer.

Asynchronous training (Hogwild) and bounded staleness.  Hogwild (Recht and colleagues, 2011): no locks, each gradient applied to whatever weights stand — Chapter 6’s lost-update anomaly at every step — and convergence survives at nearly full speed, because addition commutes: an update is an increment, not an overwrite, so interleaving merely reorders the contributions to an accumulation, exactly as for Chapter 1’s replicated counter and Chapter 7’s state-based CRDTs (Part Three). The analogy is not a theorem: an update is not idempotent, there is no join and no semilattice, and Chapter 7’s strong-eventual-consistency theorem does not apply; the tolerance is analytic — the optimization absorbs bounded disorder as noise, given sparsity and suitable step sizes (Rem. 12.4). The intermediate design is stale-synchronous training: workers may run at most $s$ steps ahead of the slowest, enforced at the barrier, with guarantees that degrade gracefully in $s$ — bounded staleness in Chapter 7’s vocabulary, the lag priced in convergence rate (Problem 12.4).

Tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, and ZeRO.  When the model outgrows one machine, partitioning (Chapter 9) runs on three axes at once: tensor shards within a pod, pipeline stages across pods, data-parallel replicas across the fleet (Part Four). Tensor parallelism shards the weight matrices — range partitioning of the parameters — so every layer of every pass fires a small collective among the shard-holders: intra-pod interconnect or death by latency. Pipeline parallelism streams micro-batches through layer shards; its cost is the pipeline bubble, stages idle while the pipeline fills and drains — roughly half the stage-time with eight stages and eight micro-batches in flight, toward an eighth with sixty-four — and more micro-batches in flight cost activation memory (Chapter 10). ZeRO and its fully-sharded descendants shard the optimizer state — momenta, variances, master copies, several times the model’s size, replicated on every worker to no durability purpose — first, then the gradients, then the weights, gathered layer by layer just before use: memory per worker falls by the fleet size, paid for in traffic.

Sharded checkpoints and the Young–Daly interval.  Fleet mean time between failures is the part’s divided by $n$: ten thousand parts rated at five years fail more than five times a day, and a month-long run is interrupted about a hundred and sixty times (Part Five, Problem 12.2). The checkpoint is therefore sharded — every worker writes its own slice in parallel, the barrier between steps serving as the quiescent instant — under a manifest declaring which slices constitute a complete cut; a torn checkpoint is excluded by the manifest, just as an atomic-commit decision never forced to the log never happened (Chapter 8). How often is arithmetic (Thm. 12.3, Exhibit 12.2): with failures memoryless at mean time $M$ and a checkpoint costing $\delta$, the overhead fraction $f(\tau) = \delta/\tau + \tau/(2M)$ — the checkpoint cost plus the expected rework of half an interval per failure — bottoms at $\tau^{*} = \sqrt{2\delta M}$ with $f(\tau^{*}) = \sqrt{2\delta/M}$, the two terms equal there. Five minutes against four hours prescribes about every forty-nine minutes at about twenty percent overhead. Both levers act through a square root: halving $\delta$ shortens $\tau^{*}$ to about thirty-five minutes and cuts the overhead to about fourteen percent, while halving $M$ gives the same interval at twenty-nine (Problem 12.5). Cheapen the checkpoint — asynchronous and sharded writes, the snapshot taken without stopping the computation — rather than lengthen the interval.

Straggler replacement and elastic membership.  The barrier makes every straggler the whole fleet’s, and a gradient worker is stateful where Chapter 10’s map tasks were pure, so the remedy assembles Chapters 4, 5, 10, and 11 (Box 30, Problem 12.7): a phi-accrual failure detector on step-completion times; hedged requests only for the stateless input pipeline; hot spares by the ring. At the next barrier the job controller — three or five nodes running Raft-style consensus, holding the member list, the current epoch, and the current checkpoint’s manifest — increments the membership epoch and splices in the spare, which loads the weights from a peer or the manifest’s checkpoint and resumes the data shard from the committed input cursor; every collective is stamped with the epoch, so a late chunk bearing a superseded one is refused rather than folded into the sum — Chapter 5’s fencing token in its final form. A failure mid-round aborts and recomputes the round, which is a deterministic function of agreed weights and agreed batch and so idempotent in effect; every $\tau^{*}$ the workers write their shards and the controller commits the manifest. Seconds of pause, not a restart.

Training-serving skew and feature freshness.  One feature computed twice — in batch from the log for training, the month complete and late records settled; online from a cache for serving, reimplemented by another team in another language — is a replica pair nobody declared, so nobody monitors its divergence (null handling, time zones, late data kept by one and dropped by the other), and the model degrades for a reason no model metric names (Part Six). The remedy is Chapter 10’s: one feature definition, materialized to both consumers from the same log, as the Kappa architecture abolished the Lambda architecture’s second codebase, with the residual monitored like any replication pair — sampled online features diffed against their batch recomputation and the divergence alarmed before it appears as lost revenue. The serving fleet is stateless replicas behind a load balancer (Chapter 6), a rollout a view change with canaries and a rollback the rewind (Chapter 11); and feature freshness is replication lag with a profit-and-loss line — a thirty-second-stale read of a user’s behaviour is Chapter 6’s follower read with a revenue gradient, so Chapter 7’s question applies: eventual consistency plus what, at what cost per second of lag, signed off by whom.

Sharp edges. 

  • what staleness costs: a stale gradient applied at full step points where the optimum was — three steps on a quadratic take the iterate from $4$ to $0.5$, and a gradient computed back at $4$ then throws it to $-1.5$ — so near convergence the stale corrections dominate: systematic overshoot, the sawtooth loss curve. Cap the staleness or damp the step by the gradient’s age (Problem 12.8).

  • every straggler is the whole fleet’s: each ring pass waits for the slowest link and machine; hedge the stateless input pipeline, replace the stateful worker by view change — and an unfenced stale chunk corrupts the sum at every replica, which determinism then faithfully preserves; an unreplicated controller stalls the fleet at the first straggler (Problem 12.7).

  • the Young–Daly fine print: the basin is flat, and the model ignores two effects — a failure during the checkpoint wastes the attempt, and recovery time adds a constant per failure — both second order while $\delta/M$ is small, both pushing $\tau^{*}$ up (Problem 12.5).

  • silent data corruption: the fleet studies (“Cores that don’t count”) document mercurial cores that miscompute data-dependently, intermittently, and silently, past every standard test, at rates far above the certification models; their products are poisoned numbers — a gradient with one wrong element, a corrupted checkpoint page — and checksums miss a value born wrong. Verify at the ends of the arithmetic: duplication where affordable, checkpoint-time validation (a poisoned run caught within one $\tau^{*}$), invariant checks on the loss curve (Rem. 12.5).

15.13 Chapter 13 — The Full-Time Parliaments

The one sentence.  etcd and Consul are coordination services renting out the same coordination kernel — membership, configuration, locks, elections, and service discovery, operated once and shared by every service in the fleet — under opposite designs: etcd is one Raft group over a disk-backed key-value store that keeps its history, with failure detection centralized as leases held in that store; Consul is a small Raft group of servers over a gossip-based membership group, SWIM keeping the membership list and health checks running on the node where each service lives — and nearly every design difference between the two follows from where state lives.

The two system models.  For etcd, Kubernetes, and Consul’s servers (Parts Two, Three, and Five), Chapter 4’s model: asynchronous, partial synchrony for liveness only; crash-recovery with stable storage; fair-loss links with retransmission; crash quorums, $f < n/2$; no Byzantine faults; and, for leases and leader-lease reads alone, bounded clock drift, never synchronized clocks (Chapter 5’s lease theorem). SWIM (Part Four) assumes less — no quorum, no leader, no agreement on the time: $n$ members with unique identifiers, each cutting time by its own clock into protocol periods of length $T$; links that lose, delay, duplicate, and reorder but never forge (crash faults only); detection claims stated in protocol periods.

The revision store, compaction, and the watch.  etcd is Chapter 5’s replicated state machine driven by Raft, and the state machine keeps history (Def. 13.1): one counter, the revision, incremented once per committed write transaction; each key a sequence of generations, each generation a sequence of versions stamped with the writing revision; each record carrying $\mathit{create}$ (the revision opening the current generation), $\mathit{mod}$ (the revision of the newest version), and $\mathit{version}$ (its ordinal in the generation); a delete closing the generation, a later write opening a fresh one with no link to the old. A read as of revision $r$ returns each key’s newest version stamped at most $r$, in the generation open at $r$ — a consistent snapshot (Chapter 2) of the keyspace at $r$, obtained after the fact. A watch from $r$ delivers exactly the committed events (puts and deletes) stamped above $r$, in stamp order, replaying the retained past before following the present. A compaction at $c$ discards versions stamped below $c$ except each key’s newest, and thereafter a read as of $r < c$ and a watch from such an $r$ both fail with the compacted refusal (Def. 13.2, Problem 13.1); the client’s recovery is the informer pattern — fetch the current state whole, note its revision, watch from there. Jepsen’s 2020 audit of etcd 3.4.3 confirmed the contract: key-value operations, transactions included, strictly serializable; watches delivering every change, in order.

Leases, locks, the fencing revision, and membership changes.  A lease is granted with a time-to-live, kept alive by heartbeat, and holds attached keys, deleted through the log on expiry; expiry is judged on the leader’s clock under Chapter 5’s clock-drift bound. The lock and election recipes are ZooKeeper’s recut for a flat keyspace: contenders write keys under a prefix, the lowest create revision holds the lock, and each waiter watches the key immediately below its own. No separate fencing-token mechanism is needed (Prop. 13.1): grants are writes, writes are totally ordered by the log, and the revision increments once per committed write, so successive lock holders bear strictly increasing create revisions, and a resource that refuses any write stamped below the highest it has admitted shuts out every deposed holder — the create revision is Chapter 5’s fencing token, enforceable at the point of effect. Jepsen’s audit prescribes exactly this repair for a lock interface it calls “fundamentally unsafe” on its own, since no lock service can promise mutual exclusion to clients that may pause. Reconfiguration is one member at a time, under Chapter 5’s one-server-at-a-time lemma; a new member joins as a learner, replicating the log without a vote, and is promoted by a second configuration change once caught up.

Linearizable reads by read index.  The linearizable default is ReadIndex (Box 31): the leader takes its commit index as the read index, requires an entry of its own term committed, confirms its leadership with one heartbeat round answered by a majority at its term, waits until its applied index reaches the read index, and answers from local state. Thm. 13.2: such reads are linearizable against the log’s write order. The argument is quorum intersection (Chapter 4): a write acknowledged before the read was invoked came either from this leader, whose commit index had already passed it, or from a leader of an earlier term — a later term’s election would need a majority sharing a member with the heartbeat majority, and that member’s vote, cast after its acknowledgment at this term, would place every commit of the later term after the round began — and leader completeness then puts it in the leader’s log below the read index; later reads see read indices at least as large, and committed prefixes only grow. Exhibit 13.1: a deposed leader’s heartbeat is refused by a member of both majorities, and its stale local answer with it. The serializable option answers from any member’s applied state with no round: a committed prefix, no real-time clause, staleness bounded only by that member’s lag.

Kubernetes on etcd.  Kubernetes keeps every object in etcd, under a registry prefix, behind a stateless front end, the API server: the whole control plane is one watch loop over a rented log. An object’s resource version is the store’s $\mathit{mod}$ revision, and an update citing a version that has moved is refused — optimistic concurrency (Chapter 8) by Prop. 13.1’s comparison at the point of effect. The watch cache fans one store watch per resource type out to thousands of client watchers from an in-memory recent history. The informer pattern — list, note the resource version, watch from it, relist on the “too old” refusal (the compaction boundary, two layers up) — feeds level-triggered controllers, for which an event is a hint to re-run the comparison of desired against observed state and a missed event is repaired by the next listing (Problem 13.8). Elections and node heartbeats are lease objects in the store: controllers campaign by compare-and-swap on a lease, each kubelet renews a per-node lease every few seconds, and the node controller marks nodes whose lease has lapsed — failure detection as centralized bookkeeping, the signature of etcd’s design.

SWIM: the membership list and the probe round.  Consul’s servers form a Raft group; membership across the whole fleet is kept by gossip, by SWIM (Das, Gupta, and Motivala, DSN 2002; Serf, on the memberlist library) with HashiCorp’s Lifeguard amendments (2017): detection is a constant-size probe round, and membership updates ride the round’s own messages, so per-member load is constant in the group size. Each member’s list maps identifiers to records $(\mathit{inc}, \mathit{status})$: the incarnation number, raised only by the subject itself and only to refute a suspicion, and a status of alive, suspect, or faulty. Records about one member are totally ordered — alive below suspect at each incarnation, higher incarnations above lower, faulty above all and absorbing — and a member incorporates a received record by keeping the larger, the pointwise maximum over the list (Def. 13.6). Thm. 13.3: a total order is a join-semilattice under max, the list ordered pointwise is one under pointwise max, every local update moves a record strictly up, and incorporation is the join; Chapter 7’s strong eventual consistency theorem then applies, and two members that have received the same records hold identical lists whatever the order and duplication (Problem 13.4). This is why a refutation outranks the suspicion it answers wherever the two meet: rank rides in the record, not in the timing. The round (Box 32): ping the next member of a shuffled roster; on timeout, ping-req through $k$ random members; on total silence, publish suspect citing the subject’s incarnation, piggybacked on later messages with the suspect told first; the subject refutes by raising its incarnation and publishing alive; a suspicion unanswered by the timeout, which shrinks as independent confirmations arrive, becomes faulty. Lifeguard’s local health multiplier stretches an unwell member’s own period and timeouts, so a member in poor health accuses slowly (Problem 13.7). Exhibit 13.2 walks both verdicts: the indirect probe clearing a member behind a lost link, and a suspicion at incarnation seven outranked by the refutation at eight. The bounds: with round-robin over a reshuffled roster, every live member probes a crashed one within $2(n-1)-1$ periods of the crash (worst case: probed first in one traversal, last in the next), so detection is certain, on a schedule (Thm. 13.4); with random selection, the periods until the first probe are geometric with success probability $1 - (1 - 1/(n-1))^{n-1}$, expectation tending to $e/(e-1) \approx 1.58$, about a period and a half independent of the group’s size (Prop. 13.5, Problem 13.3).

Consul: the catalog, the read modes, blocking queries, sessions, and federation.  Services register with the agent on their own node and are health-checked there; the agent syncs its local truth to the servers’ catalog by anti-entropy (Chapter 6’s term) and is authoritative for its node; node liveness is SWIM’s verdict, service fitness the local checks’ — detection at the edge, against etcd’s central leases. consistent (Consul, option)the leader confirms its leadership with a quorum round, then answers: linearizable — Box 31 by another name.
default (Consul)the leader answers from local state under a leader lease: linearizable while the drift assumption holds; within a leadership-change window a deposed leader may answer stale, as the manual says plainly.
stale (Consul, option)any server: a committed prefix, staleness unbounded but measurable; reads survive a period with no leader.

Both systems sell a quorum-confirmed linearizable read and a local committed-prefix read under opposite defaults: etcd defaults to the strict read; Consul adds the leader-lease read and makes it the default (Problem 13.2). The blocking query presents an index $i$ (a position in the servers’ log) and returns, on any relevant change or at timeout, the current state with its index $i' > i$, the states between coalesced (Def. 13.8): Consul returns current state, level-triggered, every consumer a reconciler, where etcd’s watch replays history, edge-triggered and resumable — only history on disk permits replay, so the storage choice forces the watch design. A session binds a time-to-live, the node’s SWIM verdict, and nominated local checks, and invalidation of any one releases its locks; the servers then withhold the lock for the lock-delay (default fifteen seconds, configurable to zero), Chubby’s safeguard, so a possibly living former holder may notice and stand down. The delay narrows the window; only a fence closes it, and the fence is again the store’s rising number: every key carries a modify index, writes may be submitted check-and-set against it, and a lock protocol stamping resource writes with the modify index read at acquisition is safe with the delay at zero (Problem 13.5). Federation: each datacenter runs its own servers, Raft group, and catalog, the servers share a wide-area gossip pool, and queries for a remote datacenter are forwarded to its servers — federation routes, it does not replicate, so partitioned datacenters each serve their own catalog (Chapter 3’s CAP trade-off, taken deliberately).

The comparison, and the blast radius.  Part Seven’s six axes — where state lives, how membership is known, how one watches (replay against current state), what a read costs by default, what guards a lock, how far it federates — and its choice rule: etcd’s design for a system of record, Consul’s for a directory. Part Six reads the Roblox outage of October 2021 as the coordination service’s blast radius, itemized (last edge below).

Sharp edges. 

  • compaction is the one place a watch admits a gap: an as-of read or a watch resumption below the compaction point draws the compacted refusal, and the correct recovery is the informer pattern — relist, note the revision, watch from there; a client that resumes by guessing a revision has invented the gap it cannot see.

  • the leader-lease read is stale during a leadership change: Consul’s default read rides the leader lease, linearizable exactly while the clock-drift bound holds and stale, by the manual’s own admission, in the window of a leadership change; etcd’s serializable option is a committed prefix aged by one member’s lag. The mode’s name is the guarantee (Problem 13.2).

  • lock-delay is not mutual exclusion: the delay gives a possibly living former holder time to stand down and guarantees nothing; safety rests on the rising number checked at the point of effect — create revision or modify index — and on nothing else (Prop. 13.1, Problem 13.5).

  • only the subject may raise its incarnation number: abolish incarnations in favour of newest-arrival-wins, and the fair-loss link’s ordinary allowances — delay and duplication, no forgery — make two correct members’ lists disagree about a third correct member forever and reinstate a member already declared faulty from one stale duplicate message (Problem 13.6); that a live member can always outrank any suspicion about itself rests on that monopoly.

  • one coordination service for everything: the Roblox outage — 73 hours — was metastable contention indifferent to doubled hardware (64 to 128 cores), compounded by a storage engine (BoltDB) that wrote a free-page list of nearly eight megabytes on every small append, and diagnosed through fog because the telemetry lived inside the failing cluster; Chapter 11’s analysis performed on Chapter 5’s coordination service.
