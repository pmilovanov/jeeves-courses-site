The Parliament of Machines

An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Four: The Part-Time Parliament

The Island in the Drawer

Good day, sir.

In nineteen ninety, Leslie Lamport — whose portrait has now presided over three of our four evenings, a frequency I promise reflects the field's architecture rather than my own devotion — submitted to the ACM's Transactions on Computer Systems a paper describing the parliament of a vanished Greek island called Paxos. The Paxons, the paper explained, had solved a delicate problem: their legislators were part-time — merchants and mariners, forever wandering out of the chamber on business, returning hours or days later — their messengers were unreliable, and yet the island's laws had to remain consistent: one law per numbered slot in the ledgers, the same law in every legislator's copy, no matter who wandered where. The paper reconstructed their protocol from archaeological fragments, complete with the names of Paxon dignitaries rendered in a phonetic Greek that, on inspection, encoded the names of contemporary computer scientists, and its correctness argument was conducted in the voice of an amateur classicist reporting the findings of one Παρλιαμεντ — forgive me, sir; the temptation passes.

The reviewers were not amused. More precisely — and the distinction matters to the story — they found the result interesting and the presentation insufferable, and they requested, in the way reviewers do, that the Indiana-Jones business be removed and the theorems be left to stand in decent academic dress. The author's position was that the jokes were load-bearing. Neither side yielded. And so a paper containing the solution to fault-tolerant consensus — the working, practical escape through the very gate our previous evening surveyed — went into a drawer, and stayed there for eight years, while outside the drawer the industry shipped lock managers and replication schemes and failover scripts that reinvented fragments of its contents, usually wrongly, at considerable expense. It was published at last in nineteen ninety-eight, jokes intact, by which time the field had learned enough to need it desperately and to forgive it almost anything.

My sympathies in the affair, sir, are theatrically divided, and since I promised you an itemization: as a professional with standards regarding prose, I hold that a correctness argument owes its reader every courtesy, and burying the decade's most important protocol under a fictional archaeology was, let us say, an aggressive courtesy. As a gentleman's gentleman, however, I observe that the reviewers were offered the answer to their field's central question and declined it over a matter of costume — a parable on the perils of wit among the humourless, and I confess I take the author's side with more feeling than is strictly professional. The field has since split the difference in its habitual manner: it kept the result, discarded the island, and named everything after it anyway. Our course borrowed the paper's spirit for its very title — this is the debt I promised in the founding session to explain, and it stands explained: a parliament of machines, part-time, unreliable, and lawful nonetheless.

Tonight, then: consensus achieved. Last week mathematics forbade it — in the asynchronous model, deterministically, with certainty of termination. Tonight we walk through gate one, partial synchrony, and build the thing that lives on the far side. And I must tell you how the building will proceed, because the method is the moral. I shall not recite Paxos at you. Recitation is how Paxos acquired its reputation as incomprehensible — a liturgy of prepares and promises that seem arbitrary until one has personally needed each of them. We shall instead derive it: begin with the naive protocol every competent engineer would sketch on a Tuesday, watch it die, repair it, watch the repair die more subtly, and repair again — twice — until the Synod of Paxos stands before you as the only shape it could ever have taken. Every rule a scar, sir, and every scar with a story. Then the fine print the theorem of last week demands; then Raft, the humane modern re-tailoring, with its famous trap staged in full; then a paragraph of overdue justice for an unsung ancestor; and at the last, a theorem of Mr. Herlihy's that explains why this one small agreement is the atom from which everything else in the season will be built.

Part One — One Law at a Time

Size the problem honestly before solving it, because half of Paxos's reputation for difficulty is the difficulty of three problems being solved at once by people who thought they were solving one. Tonight, the single-decree problem — one law, decided once — and nothing else. The log of many laws is next week's business; elections and thrones will enter tonight only as liveness machinery, exactly as last week's theorem said they must.

The setting, with the company in their accustomed places. The umbrella shop must fix a single by-law — say, whether Thursday deliveries shall be free. Motions may originate anywhere: Bingo may move one version, Tuppy another; Bertie, as ever, merely wants to know what was decided, preferably before Thursday. The gentlemen who hold copies and cast votes — acceptors, in the field's word and therefore in ours; the parliament, when we speak of them as a body — are our three: Bingo, Tuppy, and Gussie, each keeping his own ledger. The Post is its usual self: delays without bound, but tonight, as last night, no forgery and no permanent loss. And the failure model, mark it: gentlemen may crash — and may also recover, wandering back into the chamber with whatever their written ledger preserved. Part-time legislators, sir, in the paper's exact spirit. Which imposes the rule I shall call amnesia forbidden: anything a gentleman must stand behind later, he writes in the ledger before answering — because a promise remembered only in one's head does not survive a nap, and a parliament of nappers that forgets its promises will, as the desk will demonstrate with a certified disaster, cheerfully vote both sides of the same question.

A word on offices before we legislate, because the paper's division of labour confuses newcomers into imagining three kinds of machine. There are three roles, sir, not three species. A proposer is whoever currently has ambitions for the by-law: tonight, Bingo and Tuppy both, which is precisely the trouble. An acceptor is a keeper of the ledger and a caster of votes — the parliament proper, and the only office whose conduct decides safety. A learner is anyone who wishes to discover what was chosen without necessarily having voted — Bertie's whole existence, this week. One machine ordinarily holds all three commissions at once, the way a gentleman may be simultaneously a voter, a candidate, and a reader of newspapers. Keep the offices separate in your head all the same: every rule tonight belongs to exactly one of them, and half the folklore confusion about Paxos comes from asking why a voter is doing a candidate's paperwork.

And a word on arithmetic, because "majority" was chosen over its rivals for reasons worth having explicitly. Why not require unanimity — every gentleman accepts, no doubts anywhere? Because unanimity tolerates no absentees: one gentleman in the bath and the house is struck dumb, which surrenders the entire fault-tolerance ambition that brought us here — we distribute, you will recall, because machines fail. Why not a fixed pair — say, Bingo and Tuppy constitute a quorum by appointment? Because the appointment concentrates the fragility: lose one appointee and no quorum can ever form again. The majority is the arrangement that spreads the risk evenly and prices it honestly: a parliament of three proceeds despite any one absentee; a parliament of five despite any two; in general the house survives the silence of just under half its members — and this, sir, is the exact exchange rate between seats purchased and failures survived, the first line of a cost table we shall extend across the season. The deeper truth — and the reason a starred problem waits at the desk — is that nothing about tonight's correctness argument will use "majority" as such. It will use only the crowded-club property: every quorum overlaps every other. Majorities are simply the cheapest symmetric way to buy that property. Remember, when we reach the proof, to notice which fact is actually doing the work.

What must the protocol deliver? Three clauses, the same three as last week, with the vocabulary shifted one notch toward practice. A value is chosen when the parliament has irrevocably settled on it. Agreement: at most one value is ever chosen — not one per hour, not one per majority; one, full stop, forever. Validity: the chosen value is some motion actually moved — the house cannot invent a by-law nobody proposed. And termination — say it with last week's scars showing — cannot be guaranteed; we know this now, we do not pretend otherwise, and the design consequence is stated up front: safety must hold unconditionally, in any weather, under any schedule the adversary picks, while liveness will be purchased separately, with timing assumptions, in part four. This division of the estate is not a compromise we settle for. It is the correct engineering response to the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson theorem: since no protocol can promise both under asynchrony, build one whose promises never include the impossible part.

One more foundation stone, and it is tonight's debutante. Why does the field keep saying majority? Because of a fact so small it fits in a sentence and so load-bearing it will hold up four episodes: any two majorities of the same parliament share a member. Two majorities of our three gentlemen — any two-of-three with any two-of-three — overlap in at least one gentleman; two majorities of five overlap likewise; it is arithmetic, nothing more: two clubs each containing more than half the members cannot be disjoint, for the room is not large enough. The course has been calling this the crowded-club lemma since the plan was drawn, and tonight it finally takes the stage — under the name the literature gives it, which you should carry away beside the image: quorum intersection. Feel what it buys: a majority is a quorum with memory. Whatever one majority has done, any later majority contains a witness to it. No gentleman needs to consult the whole house — the whole house may never be reachable — he needs only a majority, and the intersection guarantees that majorities cannot contradict one another in ignorance. Every protocol tonight, and most of the season, is an exercise in arranging for the right witness to be in the right club at the right time.

Part Two — The Derivation, Act One: Three Ways to Die

Now we build, and fail, and build. I ask you to play along honestly, sir — at each stage, believe the protocol, as its Tuesday designer believed it.

Attempt the first: first come, first served. The obvious parliament: every gentleman accepts the first motion to reach him, and never another; a motion supported by a majority is chosen. Simplicity itself, and it even has the crowded club on its side: two different motions cannot both collect majorities, because the shared member of the two clubs would have had to accept both, and the rule forbids him a second acceptance. Safety, proven, in one breath. Run it. Bingo moves free Thursday delivery and writes to the house. Tuppy, concurrently — merely elsewhere, as we learned to say — moves a sixpence surcharge instead. And Gussie, in a spirit of independent inquiry, moves that the shop stock tea. The Post shuffles honestly; the letters land; and the votes fall one, one, and one — Bingo accepted his own motion first, Tuppy his, Gussie his. No majority anywhere. And now attend to the corner we are in: every gentleman has spent his single acceptance. No future letter can move any of them. The parliament is not deadlocked by crash or by lost mail — every machine is healthy, every letter arrived — it has voted itself into a wall, permanently, by the ordinary operation of its own rule. First-come-first-served is safe the way a sealed room is burglar-proof.

The lesson: acceptors must be able to change their minds, or split votes end the republic. Very well —

Attempt the second: latest word wins. Very well, says the Tuesday designer, rubbing out one line: let a gentleman accept every motion that arrives, each superseding the last, and let chosen mean a majority currently agrees. Stage it, briefly, because the failure deserves to be seen rather than asserted. Bingo's free-delivery letters land at Bingo and Gussie; Bertie, auditing the house at that moment, finds two ledgers of three reading free delivery — a majority; he notes the by-law settled and instructs his tailor accordingly. But Tuppy's surcharge letters were merely dawdling, clause one of the charter, and presently they land — at Gussie and at Bingo both, superseding. A second learner — Bertie's aunt, let us say, auditing an hour later with identical diligence — finds a majority for the surcharge and notes the by-law settled the other way. Two learners, two audits, two verdicts, each impeccable at its instant, and observe that no future audit is safe either, for there is no moment after which the mails are known to be empty. "Currently agrees" is a fiction in a world of letters — you will recognize the ghost of episode two, sir: there is no the current state, only states along cuts. Choice must be irrevocable — that is the entire content of the word — and perpetual mind-changing is irrevocability's opposite. We need something between one acceptance forever and acceptance without commitment: mind-changing that is ordered, where the house can tell an old thought from a new one and never lets the old displace the new.

Attempt the second and a half — the instinct every operator has next: appoint a chairman. Why suffer rival motions at all? Let the house agree that Bingo, and only Bingo, proposes; everyone accepts whatever the chair moves; done. And indeed, sir, while Bingo stands healthy and reachable, this works magnificently — a fact we shall exploit before the night is out. But test it against the terrain. The chair goes silent. Crashed, or in the bath? Last week proved the house cannot know. Wait forever, and the parliament has traded its fault tolerance for etiquette — one nap and the republic halts. Appoint a successor, then — Tuppy takes the chair after a decent timeout. But the timeout is a verdict without a body: Bingo may be alive, his motions still crawling through the Post, and now two gentlemen believe themselves chairman, each proposing with full authority — which is exactly the rival-motions problem we hired the chairman to abolish, returned wearing a sash. Leadership, sir, does not solve agreement; leadership presupposes it — agreement on who leads, and agreement on what the old regime did before it fell, neither of which the sash provides. The chairman is a fine idea we are not entitled to yet. To earn him, the house needs machinery that stays safe while the chairmanship is confused — machinery indifferent to how many gentlemen currently believe themselves in charge. Hold the thought; it returns in part four with the price tag attached.

Attempt the third: ballots. Here enters the object around which everything tonight will turn. Number the attempts. A gentleman wishing to move a motion first takes a ballot number — from a supply arranged so no two proposers ever share one; give Bingo the odd numbers and Tuppy the evens and the arrangement is done. The rule of the house becomes: a gentleman accepts the motion of ballot number n unless he has already dealt with a higher-numbered ballot; higher ballots supersede lower ones, never the reverse. A motion is chosen when a majority accepts it at the same ballot number. Old thoughts now lose to new thoughts by rule, so the split-vote wall of attempt one falls: whoever tries again at a higher ballot can gather the house afresh. And observe — you heard this coming, sir, at the close of episode two — what a ballot number is: a Lamport clock in parade uniform. A counter that only rises, carried on letters, whose entire purpose is to let a recipient pronounce the word stale. I told you the payroll was current.

So run attempt three, and watch it die the interesting death — the one that teaches the real lesson of the evening. Bingo takes ballot number one, moves free delivery, and the Post is kind: Bingo, Tuppy, and Gussie all accept ballot number one, free delivery. A majority — indeed the whole house — at one ballot. Chosen. Irrevocably, by our own definition; Bertie is informed; perhaps the Thursday circulars are printed. Meanwhile Tuppy — who, let us say, was slow to hear the outcome; his confirmation letters dawdled; as far as Tuppy knows his colleagues never voted — grows impatient in precisely the way last week's episode licensed him to: silence is not evidence, but one must act regardless. Tuppy takes ballot number two and moves his surcharge. The letters land. And every gentleman, obeying the one rule of the house — higher ballots supersede — accepts ballot number two, the surcharge. Now the ledgers show a majority at ballot number two for the surcharge; any learner arriving now sees the surcharge chosen. The free delivery was chosen, sir — and then it was unchosen. The circulars are wrong; Bertie's Thursday is a lie; agreement is dead by overwrite. And nobody misbehaved: Tuppy followed the protocol to the letter. The protocol simply permits a gentleman with a fresh ballot to propose whatever he pleases, in perfect ignorance of a decision already taken — and ballots, which order thoughts, do nothing to inform them.

There is the true lesson, and I want it in capitals before we repair anything: a new ballot must not be free. The right to propose at ballot number two must come with an obligation — to learn, first, what the house may already have done, and to be bound by it. Choice must not merely be irrevocable in the ledgers; it must be irrevocable against all future ballots. Which sets our repair problem precisely: arrange matters so that any value chosen at any ballot is the only value any higher ballot can possibly propose. Futures may ratify the past. They may never contradict it.

Part Three — The Derivation, Act Two: The Synod Stands

The repair comes in two rules, and the second is the deepest thing tonight — the designated set piece, with the wonder budget standing by.

Repair the first: the promise. If a new ballot must learn the past before acting, the past must hold still while being learned — you will recognize the shape of this requirement from a certain photograph two weeks ago. So we split a ballot's life into two phases. Phase one, the prepare: the proposer of ballot number n writes to the house — not yet moving any motion, merely announcing the ballot — "prepare, ballot number n." A gentleman receiving this, provided he has not already dealt with a higher ballot, replies with two things. First, a promise: I shall entertain nothing below ballot number n from this moment — and he writes the promise in his ledger before his reply leaves the desk, amnesia being forbidden. Second, a confession: the highest-numbered motion he has ever accepted, if any — ballot number and value both — so the proposer learns the past from the very gentlemen who constitute it. Two contents, sir, and mark that they face opposite directions: the promise is a vow about the future, the confession a disclosure about the past — one envelope, two distinct errands, and it will repay you never to fuse them. Promise is standard usage; confession is the house's word, chosen for the theatre of it — the island paper calls the envelope that carries the pair a last vote, and it is under that name you will meet the thing in the literature. When the proposer holds promises from a majority, phase one closes and he may proceed. Feel what the promise accomplishes, sir: it freezes the ground under the proposer's feet. The majority that answered him can no longer quietly accept some straggling lower-ballot motion between his reading of the past and his writing of the future — the confession he holds cannot be aged into a lie by mail still in flight. The prepare phase is asking the room's permission while taking the room's deposition, and the promise is what makes the deposition durable.

Repair the second: adopt the highest. Phase one closes; the proposer holds a majority's promises and confessions; now what may he move? Here is the rule, and then — because this is the least obvious rule in the protocol, the one that separates everyone who has memorized Paxos from everyone who has understood it — the full ten minutes on why it is not merely sufficient but forced. The rule: among the confessions in hand, find the one bearing the highest ballot number, and propose its value. Only if every confession in the majority is empty — no one has ever accepted anything — may the proposer move his own motion. His own preference otherwise goes in the drawer, jokes and all.

Why this rule, sir? Why the highest? Let me put the alternatives on trial, using the disaster we already own. Return to the moment of Tuppy's ballot number two — replayed now under phase one. Tuppy prepares; Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie promise; and the confessions come back: each says accepted: ballot number one, free delivery. If Tuppy may still propose his surcharge — if confessions are merely advisory — we have repaired nothing; so the confessions must bind. Might the rule be "adopt any confessed value," or "the value most gentlemen confess"? Consider what a confession means. The free delivery was chosen: a majority accepted it at ballot number one. Tuppy's phase-one majority and that choosing majority are two clubs in one small room — the crowded club, sir, first blood: they share at least one member, and that shared gentleman's confession carries the chosen value. It must; he accepted it, and confessions report the highest acceptance, and — hold this thought — nothing higher can have displaced it in his ledger yet, because tonight's rule is under construction precisely to ensure higher ballots carry the same value. So the chosen value will be present among any majority's confessions. Present — but perhaps not unanimous, and perhaps not even the plurality, because rooms accumulate debris, and I want you to see debris manufactured before your eyes, since it is the exact exhibit that convicts the plausible wrong rules.

Run a messier history, five gentlemen now for elbow room — Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, Barmy, and Oofy. Ballot number one: Bingo moves the surcharge; his accept letters straggle; exactly one — to Oofy — arrives before events overtake it. Oofy's ledger: accepted, ballot number one, the surcharge. One acceptance of five: nothing chosen, a mere fossil of an ambition. Ballot number two: Tuppy prepares. Three gentlemen answer him, and every one of the three confesses an empty ledger — Oofy, who holds the fossil, is not among them; such is the Post. Nothing binds Tuppy, then, and he moves his own motion: free delivery. Now the accepts, one at a time. Tuppy writes it into his ledger; Gussie writes it into his; Barmy writes it into his. Three ledgers of five, all at ballot number two: chosen. Free delivery is law, whether or not anyone yet knows it. Now ballot number three: Bingo returns, prepares, and gathers promises from a majority consisting of Bingo, Oofy — and Gussie. The confessions in his hand: Bingo, nothing; Oofy, ballot number one, the surcharge; Gussie, ballot number two, free delivery. Put the wrong rules on the bench, sir. "Adopt any confessed value" — Bingo may lawfully pick the surcharge, and the chosen by-law is overwritten at ballot number three: dead on arrival. "Adopt the plurality of confessions" — the room shows one vote surcharge, one vote free delivery, a tie resolved by whim: dead the same way, merely ashamed of it. Both rules die because both treat confessions as opinions to be polled, when they are strata to be read: the fossil at ballot number one is older than the law at ballot number two, and age here is recorded in ballot numbers, not in headcounts. Only one rule reads the strata: the highest-numbered confession — Gussie's, ballot number two, free delivery — is the chosen value. The credential is not popularity. It is the ballot number — and here is the induction that closes the trap for every future case at once, the proof strategy performed aloud with the full argument waiting at the desk.

Claim: if a value is chosen at ballot number b, then every ballot numbered higher than b that reaches its proposing phase proposes that same value. Walk the ballots upward in order, sir, and suppose the claim holds for every ballot between b and the one under examination — call the one under examination ballot n. Its proposer holds promises from a majority. That majority intersects the choosing majority of ballot b: some witness in the room accepted the chosen value at b. So the confessions include the chosen value at ballot number b — the witness's promise-ledger may since have recorded acceptances at ballots between b and n, but by the very claim we are establishing, walked upward rung by rung, every such intermediate acceptance carries the chosen value again: between b and n, there was nothing else on offer. So consider the highest-numbered confession in the proposer's hands. It is numbered at least b — the witness guarantees that — and every confession numbered b or above carries the chosen value. The highest-numbered confession is therefore the chosen value, whatever the debris below it says, however popular the debris. Adopt the highest, and ballot n proposes the chosen value; the claim climbs one more rung; the induction stands. And notice, sir, what the trial of alternatives has revealed: "adopt the highest" is not one good rule among several. Any rule that could ever prefer a lower-numbered confession admits a counterexample built exactly like our disaster — the desk has you construct one — while the highest-numbered confession is the unique position in the room guaranteed to hold the truth. The rule is forced. The scar closes.

And with it, the Synod of Paxos stands complete. Say it once, end to end, in the house style, so you may hear how little of it there suddenly is. Phase one: the proposer of ballot number n writes to the parliament — prepare, ballot number n. Each acceptor, unless already promised to a higher ballot, writes the promise in his ledger and replies: promise — enclosing his highest acceptance, ballot number and value, if ever he made one. Phase two: holding a majority's promises, the proposer moves — accept, ballot number n, and the value the confessions force upon him, his own only if the past is silent. Each acceptor, unless promised meanwhile to a higher ballot, writes the acceptance in his ledger: accepted, ballot number n. And when a majority has accepted at one ballot number, the by-law is chosen — irrevocably, this time in truth, because every future ballot has just been proven a hostage of the past. That is the entire protocol, sir. Two phases, two rules of refusal, one rule of adoption, a ledger, and a crowded room. Everything else ever written about Paxos is performance notes.

And if you keep only one thing from the wiring, sir, keep the three gates — the three places where every decision in this machine is actually taken. The first is the proposer's: he passes from phase one to phase two only with a majority of promises in hand; short of a majority he does not proceed at all, but retires and tries again at a higher ballot — and that retirement, repeated, is the whole of the trouble we meet in part four. The other two are each acceptor's, and they differ by a hair that decides everything: he opens his promise-gate to any ballot standing strictly above his last promise, and his accept-gate to any ballot standing no lower than it — so a gentleman may always accept the very ballot he has this moment promised, and never one beneath it. Three gates, sir. The entire minuet of Paxos, in every weather the adversary can send, is nothing but gentlemen arriving at those three gates and being let through or turned away.

Let us hear it performed once, clean, at full correspondence, because you have earned a run where nothing goes wrong and every letter now has a reason. The house of three; the past holds our old fossil — Gussie, from some abandoned early skirmish, has in his ledger: accepted, ballot number two, the surcharge; nothing was ever chosen. Bingo, holding the odd numbers and fresh ambitions for free delivery, opens ballot number five. Bingo writes to the house: prepare, ballot number five. Tuppy replies: promise, ballot number five — nothing ever accepted. Gussie replies, in his own good time: promise, ballot number five — and confesses, accepted, ballot number two, the surcharge. Bingo's own ledger answers likewise: promise, nothing accepted. Two promises and his own: a majority; phase one closes. Now the adoption rule reads the room: highest confession, ballot number two, the surcharge. Bingo's free-delivery ambitions go in the drawer — note it well, sir: nothing was ever chosen, the fossil was one acceptance in an abandoned ballot, and the rule binds him to it anyway. The rule cannot see whether a confessed value was chosen — that is the whole epistemic predicament — so it must treat any confessed value as possibly chosen and defer to the highest. Cautious to the point of superstition, and correct precisely because of it: cheap deference, sir, is the premium on an insurance policy against a catastrophe no one can rule out from inside the room. Bingo writes to the house: accept, ballot number five, the surcharge. Tuppy writes it in his ledger and replies: accepted. Gussie likewise: accepted. A majority has accepted at ballot number five — the surcharge is chosen, and Bertie, whose delivery notes have haunted three episodes, may at last be told something that will never afterwards be untrue. And observe the manner of that becoming, sir, for it is the temper of the whole machine: the surcharge turned from motion into law in the silent instant Gussie's acceptance completed the majority — no bell, no herald, no moment any gentleman in the room could point to. The word chosen names that fact in the ledgers, never the announcement of it; being chosen and anyone's knowing it are different events entirely, and the by-law was law before ever it was told.

I shall spend the wonder budget here, as scheduled, and briefly, on the shape of the thing rather than any one rule. We asked three attempts ago for a parliament that could not disagree with itself, and every failure retaught us the same word: the wall of attempt one, the fog of attempt two, the overwrite of attempt three — each was the past failing to bind the future. And the finished machine is nothing but that binding, built twice: promises bind the past against revision from below; adoption binds the future against invention from above. Once chosen, always chosen — not because any gentleman remembers the choosing, sir; no gentleman need ever know a choice occurred; the free delivery can be chosen while every member of the house believes the matter still open. The choice is held by the structure — lodged in the intersection of clubs, carried forward by witnesses who do not know they are witnesses, adopted by proposers who may believe they are merely being cautious. It is the first machine we have built in this course that is wiser than every one of its parts, and it will not be the last.

Part Four — The Fine Print

Now the contract's small type, in three clauses, each planted earlier and flowering now.

Clause one: what exactly is guaranteed, and against what. The safety argument you just heard — the induction, the intersection, the adoption — invoked no clocks, no timeouts, no probabilities, and no luck. Read it back and check, sir: it is pure bookkeeping about ballots and majorities, valid under any schedule the adversary cares to inflict, any delays, any interleaving of duelling ballots, any pattern of crashes and recoveries the ledgers survive. At most one value is ever chosen, in any weather. That is what unconditional safety means, and Paxos delivers it in the asynchronous model where last week's theorem lives. The theorem is not contradicted — attend to what was quietly never promised: that any ballot ever finishes.

Clause two, therefore: liveness, purchased separately — and the purchase is compulsory. Stage the failure honestly, with the two gentlemen it belongs to. Bingo prepares ballot number three; the house promises. Before his accept letters land, Tuppy — hearing nothing and growing civil-servant anxious — prepares ballot number four; the house, obedient to its promises, is now closed below four; Bingo's accepts arrive and are politely refused. Bingo, discovering himself superseded, prepares ballot number five; Tuppy's accepts arrive into a house promised to five; refused. Tuppy prepares ballot number six — you perceive the rhythm, sir. Two flawlessly polite gentlemen, each forever asking the room's permission and each thereby cancelling the other's, an infinite minuet of prepares in which no accept ever lands on open ground. Nothing in the protocol forbids it: this is last week's forever-on-the-fence, no longer an abstract adversary construction but a plain operational hazard with names attached — the duelling proposers. Last week I told you the theorem's production shadow was the election storm, term numbers climbing while no term completes; you have now been introduced to the storm's two dancers, and have watched the ballots do the climbing. When your monitoring some night shows a coordination cluster's epoch counter ratcheting upward at a steady, fruitless clip, you will know precisely which minuet is playing, and precisely which nineteen eighty-five theorem is calling the tune. And the escape is exactly the one gate one licenses: let the timing assumptions of the real world elect a distinguished proposer — a single gentleman whom the others, for a while, defer to; today, as the casting has it, Bingo has the chair. One chairman, no duel; ballots issue from one desk; phase one need succeed only once in calm weather, and matters proceed. The chair is won by timeout and held by heartbeat — which is to say, the throne episode two promised you is rented from partial synchrony, and last week's weakest-detector result now cashes its cheque with some ceremony: the minimum oracle that makes consensus solvable, you will recall, is exactly an eventual-leader service. Not a coincidence, sir — a design specification, discovered by theorists and implemented by every timeout-driven election in your fleet. And the division of labour must be said in one line, because it is the single most quoted moral of tonight: the leader is a liveness optimization; safety lives in the quorums. A second chairman arising in confusion — the deposed Bingo wandering back mid-election — threatens nothing but progress: his stale ballots die against promises, by the same machinery that kills any stale ballot. The throne may wobble freely. The ledgers do not.

Clause three: the ledgers themselves. Every load-bearing word tonight has been written — the promise recorded before the reply, the acceptance recorded before the acknowledgment — and this is not implementation fussiness; it is the failure model's bill. Our legislators are part-time: they crash and return. A gentleman who promises ballot number four, naps, and wakes with an empty head is a gentleman who will cheerfully accept ballot number three — the very acceptance his promise forbade — and with two such nappers the parliament manufactures two chosen values in an afternoon. The desk stages this precisely — a sabotage problem in which the promise table is kept in memory because it is rebuilt by traffic anyway, a sentence I have heard in production reviews, sir, spoken by persons of otherwise sound judgment — and the disaster is certified fatal in the solutions, double-choice written out to the last letter. Amnesia is not a degraded mode of the protocol. It is a different protocol, and a wrong one.

Two footnotes to the contract before Raft, both of which the folklore habitually loses. First: the learners. A value being chosen and anyone knowing it are different events — you glimpsed this in the derivation, where free delivery was law while every gentleman believed the matter open. Choice lives in the ledgers collectively; knowledge requires collation. The mechanics are undramatic — acceptors post their acceptances to the learners, or to the chair, who tallies and announces; a majority of acceptances at one ballot number is the certificate — but one consequence deserves its own sentence. A latecomer — Bertie's aunt, arriving cold — cannot simply poll the house and trust the tally, for she may catch the ledgers mid-ballot, half-updated, exactly as the naive auditors of episode two caught the bank mid-transfer. The clean way for a latecomer to learn the truth is to run a ballot of her own: prepare, adopt the highest, propose it, and read what the house confirms — the act of reading the parliament is a ballot, sir. File that sentence carefully; next week, when we ask what it costs to serve a merely read through a consensus system, and what shortcuts leases can lawfully buy, it will be the entire crux.

Second: the invoice. Count the letters in the clean run you heard: one round trip of prepares and promises, one round trip of accepts and acceptances — two round trips of the Post per decision, each touching a majority, plus a ledger write on every gentleman at each step. That is the sticker price of one by-law, and for a parliament deciding by-laws all day — which is what a replicated database is — it is plainly extortionate. The remedy is the chairman we have now lawfully earned: a stable leader runs phase one once, for all future decisions at a stroke — one standing prepare, a majority's standing promises — and thereafter each decision costs a single accept round. Phase one amortized away; the common case, one round trip. That arrangement — the Synod with a standing chairman and a numbered docket of decisions — is called multi-Paxos, it is what every production deployment actually runs, and it is precisely the door at which next week's episode knocks. Tonight we merely note the price and that it can be paid.

Part Five — Raft, or Understandability as an Engineering Requirement

Twenty-odd years pass. Paxos wins in production — inside lock services and databases you have operated, usually via the multi-decree extension we take up next week — and simultaneously acquires an academic folklore: that it is fiendish; that nobody implements it correctly from the papers; that every real deployment is a nest of undocumented amendments. Some of this was costume, as established. Some was real: the single-decree Synod is small, but composing it into a practical replicated log leaves many load-bearing decisions as exercises for the implementer, and implementers' exercises become outages. In two thousand fourteen, Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout of Stanford published, at the USENIX technical conference, a protocol called Raft, built on a premise the field found mildly scandalous: that understandability is an engineering requirement — not a courtesy, a requirement, on the grounds that operators debug at three in the morning what they can hold in their heads, and safety on paper is not safety in a fleet run by the confused. They did something almost unheard of, sir: they measured it — a formal user study, students taught both protocols and quizzed, and the quiz scores published in the paper as evidence, as though comprehensibility were a latency figure. I have rarely seen the industry so quietly shamed into agreement.

The paper's method deserves one more sentence before its machinery, because the method is transferable and the machinery is not. Ongaro and Ousterhout designed for explainability the way one designs for latency: decomposing the problem into nearly independent concerns — leader election, log replication, safety, membership — so each fits in a head separately; preferring one strong leader through whom all data flows, not because symmetry is impossible but because asymmetry is narratable; and shrinking the state space wherever a rule could be simplified at modest cost. Then the study: two lecture videos of equal polish, one protocol each; students quizzed on both, order counterbalanced like a proper trial; Raft's comprehension scores materially higher, and the authors published the quizzes so the skeptical could re-run the experiment. One may quibble with any single method — I have heard the quibbles, sir, and delivered a few — but the stance is the contribution: if operators cannot re-derive your invariants at three in the morning, your invariants are decorative.

Raft's furniture, then — most of it tonight's machinery in working clothes, and I propose to set it out one stick at a time rather than gesture at the room. The protocol's whole claim on your attention is that it can be held in a head at three in the morning; a description delivered at speed would refute the claim in the act of making it.

Begin with the cast — not the gentlemen, whom you know, but the offices. At any moment, every member of a Raft house is exactly one of three things: a follower, a candidate, or the leader. Three offices and no fourth. A follower is passive: he answers letters and never initiates; the great majority of the house is in this condition the great majority of the time, and a healthy house is one leader and four followers going quietly about their filing. A candidate is a follower who has run out of patience with the silence from above and is soliciting votes for himself. The leader is a candidate who won, and while he reigns, all business flows through him — the house takes no motion from anybody else. Fix the three in your ear before anything moves, sir: follower, candidate, leader. Everything that follows is the rules for passing between those three offices, and the letters exchanged while holding them.

Now the calendar. Time in Raft divides into terms, numbered one, two, three, and onward without end — and a term is a ballot number wearing a calendar; the Lamport clock takes yet another salary. Two facts about a term, and they are worth taking separately. The first: each term begins with an election, and each term has at most one leader — sometimes none at all, for an election can fail, and a term that elects nobody is simply a term in which nothing happened. The second, and the one that surprises people: a term is not an interval of clock time. It is a reign. It lasts precisely as long as its leader lasts — a few milliseconds if he is unlucky, a fortnight if he is not — and the numbers march upward not with the hours but with the failures.

And now a rule that governs every letter in the protocol, which I shall state before any letter is sent and restate once it is. Every Raft letter, in both directions — solicitation and reply, replication and acknowledgement, the lot — carries its sender's current term number. On receiving any letter, before he so much as reads its contents, a gentleman compares the term it carries against his own. If the letter's term is higher, he adopts it as his own on the spot and becomes a follower that instant, whatever he was a moment before: a candidate abandons his candidacy, a leader abandons the gavel, and neither is consulted in the matter. If the letter's term is lower, he refuses the letter unread and replies with his own term, so that the sender may discover he is behind and stand down of his own accord. Higher term: adopt it and submit. Lower term: refuse it and correct the sender. That single comparison, performed on every letter that crosses the island, is how Raft retires a stale chairman without anybody having to establish that he died — the deposed leader deposes himself the moment somebody addresses him in the language of a later reign.

What each gentleman keeps in his ledger, then — durably, in ink, written before any reply leaves the desk. Three items, no more. First, his current term: the latest term he knows to exist. Second, his vote in that term: the name of the gentleman he voted for, or nobody-yet. Third, his log, of which much more shortly. All three are written down before the corresponding reply is posted — amnesia forbidden, precisely as with the Synod's promises and for precisely the same reason: a gentleman who forgets his vote across a crash and votes a second time in the same term has, with his own hand, manufactured two leaders in one term. The ink is not bookkeeping. The ink is the safety property.

An election, then, one move per beat. Bingo's patience expires — we shall come to how patience is measured in a moment. First move: he increments the term, from term four, let us say, to term five. Second move: he votes for himself in term five, and writes that vote in his ledger. Third move: he ceases to be a follower and becomes a candidate. Fourth move: he writes to every other gentleman a letter whose name is worth keeping, because it is the paper's own — request vote — and that letter carries two things: the new term number, and the ending of Bingo's own log, meaning the number of the last slot he holds and the term stamped in that last slot. Hold on to the ending, sir. In ten minutes it decides everything.

Now cross the island and sit at Tuppy's desk as the letter lands, and take his decision as he takes it — three questions, asked in that order, and the candidate must survive all three. Not the Synod's three gates, sir, which policed ballots; these are Tuppy's three questions, and they police a vote. The first: what term does the letter carry? Here the term rule you have just had twice — below Tuppy's own, he refuses at once and tells the candidate his term; above it, Tuppy adopts the new term and, mark this, clears his vote, because a fresh term is a fresh slate and one vote per term means one vote per that term. The second: has Tuppy already voted in this term, for somebody else? Then he refuses; his vote is spent. The third: is the candidate's log at least as up to date as Tuppy's own — a comparison I shall define precisely when we have the log properly on the table? If not, he refuses, and that refusal is the single most important veto in the protocol. Three questions survived, and Tuppy writes the vote into his ledger first, then posts his reply: granted.

An election ends in exactly one of three ways, and it repays having all three in the ear. One: the candidate collects grants from a majority of the house, counting the vote he cast for himself, and is leader of that term forthwith; he announces himself by writing to everybody immediately, which stops the others from standing. Two: while he is still soliciting, a letter arrives from a leader whose term is his own or later — somebody else got there first, or a later reign has already begun — whereupon the term rule does its work, and our candidate becomes a follower again without a fight. Three: nobody wins. Three candidates in a house of five, the votes scattered, no majority anywhere — a split vote. Nothing is broken and nothing is decided; the candidates' patience simply expires again, the term is incremented again, and the house tries once more in term six. Note the shape of that third outcome, sir: a failed election costs a term and a round of letters, and nothing else. Raft would sooner waste a term than risk two leaders in one.

Which brings the crowded club to collect its first Raft dividend. Could two gentlemen both be leader of term five? To lead term five, each must hold grants for term five from a majority of the house. But any two majorities of five share a member — the crowded club again, an hour older and still earning its keep — so some gentleman granted his vote twice in term five. And he cannot have: he grants at most one vote per term, and he writes that vote in his ledger before the reply leaves the desk, so not even a crash can talk him into a second. Contradiction; two leaders in one term is impossible. At most one leader per term, proven in the time it took to say it, and that is election safety, the first of the five clauses in the paper's warranty.

Now the reign, and the letter that constitutes it. A Raft leader replicates by sending a letter called append entries — the paper's name, and worth keeping — and a heartbeat is simply an append-entries letter with nothing to append: an empty envelope posted at a steady cadence to say merely the chair is occupied. Do not let the emptiness fool you, sir; the empty letter does two jobs and both are load-bearing. It suppresses elections, because a follower who hears from the chair has no cause to stand. And it carries the leader's term, which by the term rule quietly deposes any stale claimant who happens to hear it, and quietly informs any gentleman who has been asleep that the world has moved on.

And the trigger opposite the heartbeat: every follower keeps a clock called the election timeout — his allowance of patience — and a gentleman who has heard nothing from the chair for the length of his patience concludes the throne is vacant and stands for it. You will recognize the timeout for the purchased verdict it is; Raft simply owns the purchase openly. But observe the hazard the timeout drags in behind it. If every gentleman's patience were the same length, then silence from the chair would set the entire house campaigning in the same instant, five candidates each voting for himself, no majority possible, a split vote — and then, patience expiring together again, the same farce in the next term, and the next. Hence the humblest trick in the paper, and one of its most effective: randomized election timeouts. Each gentleman draws his allowance of patience at random from a range — a range a hundred or two hundred milliseconds wide, in the deployments you have operated — so no two gentlemen lose patience together, so one of them almost always stirs first and has collected his majority before his rivals have thought to move. You will recognize the shape from last week's second gate, sir — a pinch of randomness to break the symmetric minuet — deployed here not against the theorem's adversary but against ordinary bad luck. The theorem still holds; the storm is still possible; the dice merely make it embarrassingly improbable per round.

And the paper states its warranty as a contract of five named properties, which I shall read out as a contract is read out, one clause at a time. Election safety: at most one leader per term — proved a minute ago, and yours now. Leader append-only: a leader never deletes or overwrites an entry in his own log; he only appends to it. Log matching: any two ledgers that agree on one slot agree on the whole history behind it — the clause we take up next, since it is the engine of the thing. Leader completeness: any law the house has committed is present in the ledger of every future leader, forever. And, resting on the other four, state-machine safety: no two gentlemen ever apply different laws at the same slot — which is the promise the customer actually bought, the other four being the joinery that holds it up. Five clauses, each with its own page and its own argument — understandability, sir, practised as promised, one insurable clause at a time.

But Raft's real subject is the part we have deferred all evening: not one law but the log — the numbered ledger of laws, slot after slot, that next week's episode makes the star. Raft is log-native, and before we watch the log move, let us open it and look at a single line of it. A log is a numbered row of slots, slot one, slot two, slot three, running rightward without end, and what sits in a slot is called an entry. An entry carries the motion itself and two stamps, and both stamps are load-bearing, so take them one at a time. The first is the slot number — the entry's position in the row, which is what makes the log a log rather than a heap, since the state machines apply the slots strictly in order. The second is the entry's term — the term of the leader who created it. Position says where; term says under whose reign. Very nearly every rule in Raft is a comparison of one stamp or the other, and the trap we are walking towards is a rule that compares the wrong one.

The happy case first, one move per beat, with Bingo in the chair. Bertie the client sends a motion to the leader — to Bingo, and to nobody else; a follower approached directly merely points at the chair. First: Bingo appends the motion to his own log, in the next free slot, stamped with his own current term. Second: he writes to every follower an append-entries letter carrying that new entry. Third: each follower who accepts it writes the entry into the same numbered slot of his own ledger and acknowledges. Fourth: when the entry sits on a majority of desks, Bingo's own included, the entry is committed — chosen, in tonight's vocabulary — and Bingo may apply it to his state machine and tell Bertie the by-law is law. Fifth: the followers find out afterwards, and here is the small piece of plumbing that does it — every append-entries letter also carries the leader's commit index, the number of the highest slot he knows to be committed, so the very next letter to arrive, even an empty heartbeat, tells each follower how far down his own ledger it is now safe to apply. Note the asymmetry, sir, because it is permanent and it is not a defect: the leader knows a thing is committed one letter before the house does.

So an append-entries letter carries four things, and I shall name all four, because the third does the work of the protocol. One: the leader's term. Two: the entries being sent, if any. Three: the stamp of the slot immediately preceding the ones being sent — that slot's number, and the term written in it. Four: the leader's commit index. It is item three we care about, and it has a name in the paper: the consistency check. The follower's rule upon receiving the letter is this. Before writing anything, look up the named predecessor slot in your own ledger. If you hold an entry there, of that number and bearing that term, then you and the leader agree at least that far back: accept, write, acknowledge. If you hold nothing there, or hold something bearing a different term, refuse — and refuse without arguing, without explaining, without proposing amendments.

Watch the repair that follows a refusal, because it is prettier in operation than it sounds in description. Bingo, newly in the chair, believes Tuppy's ledger matches his own through slot seven, and writes accordingly: here is slot eight; the slot before it, slot seven, bears term four. Tuppy consults his own slot seven, finds term two sitting there instead — the residue of some earlier reign — and refuses. Bingo, so corrected, steps back one slot and tries again: here are slots seven and eight; the slot before them, slot six, bears term four. Tuppy consults slot six, finds term four, and this time the check passes — whereupon he deletes the conflicting entry he was holding in slot seven, and writes the leader's slots seven and eight in its place. Had the check failed again, Bingo would have stepped back again, and again, until a letter passed. That is the whole repair mechanism: the leader walks backwards until the two ledgers meet, and then ships his own version forward over whatever the follower had. Followers never negotiate; the leader's ledger simply wins, and the finding of the meeting point is done with the leader's feet.

And that one-line check buys the property with the honest name — log matching: if two gentlemen's ledgers agree on the term stamped in a given slot, then they hold the identical entry in that slot and identical entries in every slot before it. Consider what an enormous claim that is to get from so small a handshake, sir. It says that to establish that two ledgers agree across their entire history, it suffices to compare one slot's term. Not the contents; not the length; one stamp. And it is true because nothing ever enters a ledger without its predecessor having already been agreed — the check sees to that at every single write — so agreement at any point implies agreement all the way down. An induction that never gets to start wrong.

Elegant, effective — and the source of the trap, because followers' unreplicated tails can be overwritten, and an election can crown a gentleman whose ledger lacks another's recent scribbles. To keep committed law safe, Raft adds the voting restriction — the third of Tuppy's three questions, deferred until now: a gentleman refuses his vote to any candidate whose ledger is less up to date than his own. And up to date has a precise meaning which I shall give twice, because everything hangs on it and it is not the meaning one would guess. It is judged by the ending of the ledger, and by the term before the length. Compare the terms stamped in the two final slots. The ledger whose final slot bears the later term is the more up to date — however short it may be. Only when the two final terms are equal does length decide anything, and then the longer ledger wins. Term first, length second: a short ledger ending in a recent reign beats a long ledger ending in an ancient one, every time.

Now run the safety argument aloud, because it is the Synod's argument wearing electoral dress and you should hear the seams. A committed slot lives on a majority — that is what committed means. Any winning candidate persuaded a majority — that is what winning means. The clubs intersect: some voter holds the committed slot. And that voter's vote was conditional: he compared endings, and he granted his vote only to a ledger at least as up to date as his own — which, the desk shows with the care the claim deserves, a ledger missing his committed slot cannot be, for it must end either in an older term or in the same term at lesser length. The witness's veto, sir: the crowded club supplies the witness, the up-to-date rule arms him, and no gentleman can reach the gavel without carrying every law the house has passed. Where the Synod's proposer reads the past out of a majority and adopts it, Raft's electorate simply refuses any future that has not already done its reading. Leader completeness — the desk proves it properly, by induction over terms, structured after Ongaro's thesis.

And yet — one trap remains, subtle enough that the paper devotes its most famous figure to it: the eighth, which is why you will hear engineers speak of the figure-eight problem and expect to be understood. It is our sabotage-run, staged now with the full company of five. The rule it justifies: a leader may count replicas toward commitment only for slots of his own term. Never for an inherited slot, however widely replicated. Watch what happens to a leader who counts the old term's parcels — and mark the debut, sir: Barmy takes the stage at last, and the casting notes will be honoured.

The scene: five members — Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, Barmy, and Oofy — and one thing only to watch, sir, so fix it in your ear before anybody moves: the second slot of the ledger, and which motion sits in it on each of the five desks. Everything else tonight is scenery.

Term two. Bingo has the chair. He writes a motion into his own second slot — the old motion, let us call it, since its age is the whole point — and begins replicating. The letter to Tuppy arrives, and Tuppy writes it: two desks now hold the old motion. The letter to Gussie does not arrive; Gussie being Gussie, it is dawdling somewhere in the Post. Barmy and Oofy have not seen it at all. And now Bingo goes quiet: a long pause of the garbage-collecting kind; the house hears nothing from the chair.

Term three. An election stirs, and Barmy — on his very first appearance in this series, sir, I remind you — stands for the chair. He needs three votes and holds his own. He solicits Gussie: Gussie's second slot is empty, so he sees nothing in Barmy's ledger to object to, and he grants. He solicits Oofy: empty likewise, granted likewise. Tuppy, mark it, holds the old motion and would have refused him — but Tuppy is not asked, and two votes and his own are a majority. Chairman Barmy, then. He writes his own pet motion into his second slot — a different motion, stamped term three — and, in the finest tradition of his casting, crashes on debut, having replicated it to nobody. Count the desks again: Bingo and Tuppy hold the old motion; Barmy alone holds his new one; Gussie and Oofy hold nothing there.

Term four. Bingo wakes, none the wiser, and stands. Tuppy votes for him. Gussie votes for him. With his own that is three, and his ledger is as up to date as either voter's, so Bingo has the chair again. He resumes his term-two business and replicates the old motion to Gussie at last, who writes it in. Now count, as the naive rule would: the old motion — a term-two motion, remember — sits in the second slot at Bingo, at Tuppy, and at Gussie. Three of five. A majority. Durable on a majority — surely committed? Bingo pronounces it so; Bertie is informed; the by-law is applied to somebody's Thursday.

Now watch the mathematics collect. Bingo pauses again — the chair has a gift for ill-timed baths. Term five. Barmy recovers and stands once more, and the two he solicits are Gussie and Oofy — and here is the sting, delivered by the very rule that was installed to protect committed law. Compare the endings, as the rule requires. Barmy's ledger ends with a term-three slot. Gussie's ends with a term-two slot; Oofy's ends earlier still. The later term wins the up-to-date comparison, so by the rule they must vote for him — Gussie and Oofy have no lawful grounds for refusal, because in the mathematics of Raft the slot Bingo counted was never committed, and the electoral test compares terms, not Bingo's announcements. Chairman Barmy, restored to the gavel he barely touched, replicates his term-three second slot to the whole house; log matching does the rest — followers discover their second slots disagree with the chair's, defer, and overwrite. The motion that stood on three ledgers of five, the motion a chairman counted to a majority and pronounced law, is expunged from every ledger on the island — replaced by the pet project of a gentleman whose entire tenure in office consists of one scribble and two crashes. If Bertie was told, Bertie was lied to; if the by-law was applied, it must now be unapplied, which for delivered umbrellas is a career-limiting sentence to say aloud.

The counting was the crime, sir — mark exactly where. Replication on a majority made the slot durable: it will never be lost by accident. But the election rules protect committed law by comparing terms, and an inherited slot's term is old news — it cannot outvote a fresher failure. Durability is a fact about copies; commitment is a claim about elections yet to come, and only the sitting term's replication makes that claim good, because only the sitting term is what future voters will be comparing. Hence the actual rule of Raft, indirect and, once you have watched Barmy, obviously forced: a new leader never counts an inherited slot; he commits a slot of his own term on top of it, and log matching drags the inheritance into safety underneath. Replay the staging under the lawful rule and watch the trap fail to spring: Bingo, in term four, first replicates a fresh term-four motion — a no-operation will do; dignity is not required — to Tuppy and Gussie. Only then does he pronounce: the term-four slot is committed by its own term's count, and the term-two slot beneath it by log matching. Now let Barmy rise a second time and solicit: Gussie's ledger ends at term four — later than Barmy's term three; the veto falls; the candidacy dies in the post; the by-law stands forever. One rule, one figure, one resurrected Barmy, and the deepest sentence in the paper: replication makes a slot durable; only the sitting term's replication makes it committed.

Part Six — The Family Restored to Order

Set the two protocols side by side for a moment, because the differences are smaller than the war of pamphlets suggests. Terms are ballots; votes are promises; the up-to-date veto is the confession-and-adoption rule turned inside out — where a Synod proposer reads the past from a majority and adopts it, a Raft electorate refuses any future that has not already read it. Heidi Howard and Richard Mortier, in a tidy comparison published in twenty twenty, put it near enough thus: strip the presentation and the two are one algorithm wearing two management philosophies — Paxos trusts any proposer to learn the past on demand; Raft insists the past be pre-installed in the leader before the gavel. Multi-Paxos in sensible tweeds, sir; next week, when the log takes the stage on its own account, the family resemblance will be complete.

And one paragraph of justice, overdue by decades. In nineteen eighty-eight — before the island manuscript began circulating, and a decade before it saw print — Brian Oki and Barbara Liskov published, at the ACM's symposium on the principles of distributed computing, a protocol called Viewstamped Replication: a primary per numbered view, view changes on suspicion of failure, a majority carrying the state forward across the change, the new primary obliged to adopt the survivors' latest state before proceeding. Read the last sentence again slowly, sir. Views are terms are ballots; the view change is the election is phase one; the adoption obligation is the rule we spent ten minutes proving forced. The same machine, sir, discovered independently and published first, in the open literature, while the rival manuscript rested in its drawer being witty. History gave the island the naming rights and Liskov's group the priority, and the field, characteristically, remembers the island. The course hereby enters Viewstamped Replication into the record at its rightful seniority.

Why, then, did the island conquer anyway? Partly the drawer's own legend — nothing advertises a paper like eight years of samizdat circulation among people who insist it is unreadable — but mostly a decade of timing: when the great industrial deployments were built, in the early two thousands, the island paper had just surfaced, its author was writing exasperated simplifications, and the engineering war stories that followed — a celebrated Google account is titled, with feeling, "Paxos Made Live" — cemented the vocabulary: ballots, proposers, acceptors. Vocabulary is destiny in this trade, sir; we shall meet a fourth member of the family next week wearing ZooKeeper livery under the name Zab — born industrial, optimized for its landlord's workload, and described by its own authors in careful contrast to the island's dialect. The census, then, as the course will keep it: one theorem-shaped idea; independent discoveries and re-tailorings across four decades — Viewstamped Replication first in print, Paxos first in folklore, Zab first in your production fleet, Raft first in the textbooks your juniors will learn from. When a colleague speaks of them as rival algorithms, you may now perform the family reunion in four sentences, and I recommend doing it gently; people grow attached to their vocabularies, having paid tuition in outages for each.

Part Seven — The Universal Machine

Last movement, and it widens the lens on why tonight was worth two acts of derivation. In nineteen ninety-one, Maurice Herlihy published the result that organizes this entire territory — the field calls the paper "Wait-Free Synchronization," and its centerpiece is a hierarchy: every shared object, every synchronization primitive, every concurrent gadget can be ranked by a single number — the size of the parliament for which that object can solve consensus, with even one member free to fail. The rankings startle on first hearing. Reads and writes on shared memory — the very substrate a lifetime of single-machine programming teaches you to trust — carry consensus number one: two gentlemen sharing any quantity of registers cannot solve consensus between just the two of them. And the proof, sir, is an old friend in new rooms: bivalence. Start the two on the fence; the adversary schedules their register operations exactly as it scheduled our letters; writes at different registers are merely-elsewhere events and commute; a read learns nothing the adversary did not permit; and the fence, it turns out, can be held in shared memory precisely as it was held in the Post. Last week's machinery was never about message passing — it was about asynchrony and one possible absentee, and it follows the pair wherever they go. Climb the hierarchy: test-and-set, the humble queue, the stack — consensus number two, able to settle a pair, helpless before three. And certain objects sit at infinity — compare-and-swap, wearing silicon in every processor you own, and consensus itself: they settle any house whatsoever. The hierarchy is strict, sir, and it is a theorem: no amount of ingenuity composes registers into a two-gentleman agreement, any more than ingenuity repeals FLP — the fence is the same fence.

Now the theorem that earns the movement its title: consensus is universal. Given an object that solves consensus for a parliament, that parliament can construct a fault-tolerant, linearizable implementation of any object with a sequential specification whatsoever — any data structure, any state machine, anything you can define one step at a time. The construction has a name of its own — Herlihy's universal construction — and you have met its shape already: it is precisely the acorn from episode two grown one ring wider, the replicated state machine. Agree, repeatedly, on the next operation — a log of settled bits — and feed the agreed sequence through identical deterministic copies, which then cannot help but remain identical, forever, having the same reason. Own the bit, sir, and you own the world: every replicated database, every coordination service, every fault-tolerant anything is, by this theorem, an application of tonight — the entire upper floors of the season are furniture arranged on this one joist. Which is why the architecture funnels here and why next week is already written: one value must become a log; the log must become a service; and the services — Chubby, ZooKeeper, the captive parliaments of industry — must learn to live with tenants. Consensus in captivity, sir. Bring a lease.

Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, four items, for the review you will sooner or later chair.

Item one. Never hand-roll consensus — the derivation you heard tonight is the short version, and every corner it turned is a production incident in some company's private history. Rent the machinery: a maintained Raft library, a coordination service, a database that has done its Jepsen penance — Jepsen, to hand you the name ahead of its formal debut, being the trade's independent auditor: a test harness that partitions and crashes databases on purpose and then publishes what their brochures' promises were worth. Episode seven receives the auditor with due ceremony; for tonight, penance simply means the vendor submitted to the ordeal and printed the findings. The parliament is infrastructure, not a weekend project.

Item two. But recognize the skeleton anywhere, because you will meet it disguised weekly: ballots or terms or views or epochs — a total order of attempts; two phases whose quorums overlap — a read of the past that freezes it, a write of the future bound by it; and adopt-the-highest in some costume. When a design shows you "version numbers plus a majority write," ask where the read-and-adopt phase is; if the answer is a shrug, you have found this week's stale-ballot overwrite in embryo. And practise the recognition on machinery you already run, sir: ZooKeeper stamps every transaction with an identifier whose upper half is an epoch — a ballot number — precisely so a deposed leader's letters die on arrival; your etcd cluster campaigns in terms; even the humble fencing token you will meet properly next week is the skeleton's smallest bone — a number that only rises, presented with each request, so that a storage system may say stale to a resurrected chairman. Once you have seen the skeleton, sir, you will find the industry has been drawing it from memory for forty years, occasionally forgetting a bone, and the forgotten bone is always billed later, at incident rates.

Item three. The leader is a liveness optimization; safety lives in the quorums. Interrogate any leader-based design at exactly that joint: what, precisely, breaks when two leaders exist at once? The correct answer is "throughput, briefly." Any answer involving the word "corruption" means the ledgers are trusting the throne, and thrones, as the season keeps finding, wobble.

Item four. Persistence is part of the protocol, not an implementation detail. The promise written before the reply, the vote written before the ballot returns — these are load-bearing beams. In review, ask what is fsynced before what is acknowledged, and treat a hand-wave there exactly as you would treat one about the quorum size.

The reading, two texts, both short, both pleasures. First: Lamport, "Paxos Made Simple," SIGACT News, two thousand one — the island retold without the island, written, one senses, in a spirit of magnificent exasperation. Its abstract is a single sentence, and I shall read it to you in full, with relish, as promised in the founding plan: "The Paxos algorithm, when presented in plain English, is very simple." Every word of that sentence is doing work, sir, and two of them are doing penance. Read the paper against tonight's derivation and notice it is the same walk in formal dress. Second: Ongaro and Ousterhout, "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm," the USENIX technical conference, two thousand fourteen — read it for the protocol, but linger on the figure our Barmy just performed, and on the user study, which is the paper's real dare: correctness arguments graded by whether working engineers can retell them. And for the ambitious shelf, unassigned but unlocked: the original, "The Part-Time Parliament," Transactions on Computer Systems, nineteen ninety-eight — read it after the others, for pleasure, the way one reads a founding document once the constitutional law is already understood; and Oki and Liskov, nineteen eighty-eight, for the pleasure of watching the future arrive early, unheralded.

The desk awaits with chapter four. The crowded-club lemma, formally admitted at last, one line of proof and four episodes of consequences. The Synod boxed and checked, rule by rule, against "Paxos Made Simple." The safety theorem — at most one value ever chosen — proved in full through the invariant whose induction you heard tonight. Raft's essentials boxed against the paper's own summary figure; the leader-completeness theorem structured after Ongaro's thesis, the voting argument done properly. Exhibits: the three-way wall, the stale-ballot overwrite, the duelling proposers' minuet, and a clean Synod run for your tracing pleasure. And the problems: quorum arithmetic to limber up; the two-values-impossible theorem with your name on the hard case; two sabotage problems this week, both certified fatal per the standing rules, both with the disasters written out in the solutions — the forgetful acceptor, who keeps his promises in memory because traffic will rebuild them, and the counting chairman, who commits an inherited slot by majority and loses it by election, Barmy's whole performance reconstructed ledger by ledger. One starred door: flexible quorums — the two thousand sixteen observation of Howard and colleagues that the Synod's two phases need not each use majorities at all, so long as any phase-one club intersects any phase-two club; derive it, and feel which intersections were truly load-bearing all along.

And the ledger, sir — a rich night for the ceremony. Paid: a total order may be rented from a leader — issued at episode two's mutual-exclusion protocol, and paid tonight in full: the throne stands, rented from partial synchrony, defended by ballots, and correctly priced as an optimization rather than a foundation. Paid besides: partial synchrony, cashed by the Synod the moment the distinguished proposer took the chair. Issued, three fresh entries: one value must become a log — the Synod decides once; industry decides forever — payable next week; the phrase "behaves as a single machine," used loosely tonight of replicated ledgers, is owed its proper name and its formal definition; and the parliament's own membership — who sits in the house, and how the house votes to change the house — was quietly assumed fixed all evening; reconfiguration is deferred, with a respectful shudder, to next week's session on consensus in production captivity.

Next week, then: episode five, and the parliament goes to work for a living. A published postmortem from Cloudflare, November two thousand twenty, in which one deceived etcd cluster demonstrates that a theorem from nineteen eighty draws a production salary; the log born properly as a character; Chubby and ZooKeeper, the coordination landlords; the resurrection problem — the most dangerous machine in your fleet is one declared dead that disagrees — and its exorcists, the lease and the fencing token; and at the hour's end, the Post finally learns to forge, Aunt Agatha receives her long-promised introduction, and a three-node impossibility of terrible beauty is proven entirely in the ear. The doppelgänger returns from the stalls to the stage, sir, and this time it brings forged letters.

Until then, sir.

— End of Episode Four.
