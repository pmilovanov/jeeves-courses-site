The Parliament of Machines

An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Ten: The Log and the Dataflow

The Text Without Page Numbers

Good day, sir.

In two thousand thirteen, an engineer at LinkedIn named Jay Kreps published a blog post. I ask sir to weigh the medium before we weigh the message: not a journal paper, not a conference talk with proceedings — a company blog post, titled "The Log: What every software engineer should know about real-time data's unifying abstraction," some twenty-odd screens of plain prose with hand-drawn rectangles. It proceeded, over the following decade, to outread the journals. It is cited in papers, assigned in graduate courses, pressed on new hires by architects with the fervour of missionaries; and the field's most influential text of its decade, I note with professional appreciation, carried no page numbers. The thesis fits in a sentence, and the sentence is the evening: the humble log — an append-only sequence of records, numbered in order — is the unifying abstraction of distributed systems, the structure through which data flows between machines, the seam along which every estate can be taken apart and reassembled.

Pin the object precisely before the tour, because its power is exactly its poverty. A log supports two operations: append a record at the tail, receiving the next number; and read forward from any number. No update, no delete, no insertion in the middle; the past is immutable and the order is total — within the log — and every reader everywhere, replaying from the top, witnesses the identical sequence and arrives at the identical conclusion. That last clause is why this course keeps meeting the structure: a log is agreement, serialized — episode five built parliaments precisely to decree one; and the structure's three properties — appended only, totally ordered, durably replayable — are, respectively, why writers never conflict, why readers never disagree, and why the past can always be revisited. Everything tonight is compound interest on those three clauses.

The post was written from a particular misery, and the misery is worth a paragraph because half the estates on earth still live in it. LinkedIn, like every grown company, had accumulated systems the way a house accumulates drawers: databases, caches, search indexes, warehouses, monitoring, recommendation engines — each needing data from the others, each integrated with the others by bespoke pipelines, point to point. With a dozen producers of data and a dozen consumers, the point-to-point estate needs on the order of a dozen dozens of connections — each with its own format, its own failure modes, its own owner who has since left the company. Kreps's operational thesis was that the integration hairball dissolves if every system, instead of calling every other, reads from and writes to one central, durable, ordered, replayable structure: producers write once to the log, consumers read at leisure from it, and the dozen-dozens collapse to a dozen plus a dozen. The log as the hub was the business case, sir; the log as the theory — commit logs, replication, the database's own cellar machinery promoted to civic architecture — was the insight that made the post outlive its trigger.

Sir has, of course, already met the log — this course introduced it in episode five with billing, as the structure a parliament decrees into existence one entry at a time, and it has been loitering in the wings of every episode since: the court of record in episode eight was a log; the directory church of episode nine kept its map as one. Tonight the understudy takes the stage it was born for, and the evening is the story of how the industry's data machinery — batch processing, message queues, stream processors, the entire apparatus by which computation is fed — reorganized itself, over two decades, around that one structure. The itinerary: the lineage, from a file system that made failure boring, through a paper that made computation a pair of pure functions, to a framework that made recovery a matter of bookkeeping; the log ascendant — Kafka, and the database turned inside out; batch and stream dissolved into one another, and the return of this course's oldest ghosts, wearing lag; exactly-once summoned to the dock and cross-examined, with the Two Generals subpoenaed from episode one; the set piece — a photograph taken of a running river, walked step by step with the company; and then the reveal, for which I have been holding a debt since episode two, and which I shall not spoil in the overture beyond saying that a theorem from nineteen eighty-five is humming, at this moment, inside every serious stream processor on earth. Three debts fall due tonight, sir — the oldest items on the register among them. The photograph, the log, and the question of where computation lives: all presented for payment within the hour.

Part One — The Lineage: Failure Made Boring, Computation Made Pure

The story begins, as so many of this course's stories do, at Google in the early two thousands, with a problem of unreasonable size: index the web, on hardware chosen by the accountants. The Google File System, two thousand three — Ghemawat, Gobioff, and Leung — made three decisions that read, after episode nine, like a systems course answer key. Files are huge and append-mostly, so cut them into large chunks — shards of the file, in last week's vocabulary — replicated threefold across the fleet. Failure is not an event but a climate — with thousands of commodity machines, something is always dead — so the system's posture is not to prevent failure but to metabolize it: a chunk's death is detected, its replicas re-spread, automatically, continuously, as background digestion; failure made boring is the paper's real invention, and the temperament survives in every storage system since. And the map — which chunks, which machines — lives with a single master, episode nine's directory church founded in one stroke: the master holds megabytes of map for petabytes of data, stays off the data path — clients fetch addresses from it and then speak to chunkservers directly, so the singular component does plural work — and is itself recovered from — a log. The honest postscript came with a decade's operation: the single master aged into the design's one regret — metadata growing past one machine's comfort, failover manual in the early years — and the successors sharded the map and put parliaments under it; the church was right, sir, but even directories, episode nine taught, eventually need partitioning, and the lineage's own history obliged by demonstrating it.

Two mechanisms inside GFS deserve their sentences, both echoes forward. The master's map — files to chunks, chunks to servers — is kept current by heartbeats from the chunkservers, and the master's own survival is a checkpoint plus an operations log, replayed on recovery: episode nine's directory church and tonight's protagonist, already cohabiting in the founding document. And the paper's most quietly radical sentence is about its own arithmetic: at the fleet sizes contemplated, the normal state of the system includes failures in progress — the design begins from episode one's documentary premise rather than arriving at it through grief, which is why the course reads it as the moment the industry's storage stopped being surprised.

Then, two thousand four, the paper that taught a generation: MapReduce, Dean and Ghemawat. Its deep move is embarrassingly easy to state and was revolutionary to commit to: restrict the programmer to two pure functions — a map, applied record by record, and a reduce, applied per key to grouped intermediates — with a shuffle between them, in which the estate regroups every intermediate record by key: map, shuffle, reduce; nothing else on offer. Run the canonical job once with the company, so the shape is in the hands: count every word's occurrences across the library. Bingo, Tuppy, and Gussie each hold chunks of the library; each runs the map over his own chunk — for every word encountered, emit the pair: that word, one — a purely local pass, no conversation. Then the shuffle, the one estate-wide movement: every pair travels to the machine responsible for its word — and mark how the destination is chosen, sir: by hashing the word across the reducers, last week's placement arithmetic, employed here to partition not stored data but work in flight; debt twenty-one's payment begins in this very clause. Then each reducer, holding now every pair for its words, runs the reduce: sum the ones, emit the totals. Map, shuffle, reduce; the library counted; and no step in the description mentioned failure, which is the point about to be made. Note for the household accounts, too, where the money goes: the maps and reduces are cheap and local; the shuffle is the pattern's entire freight bill — every intermediate record crossing the network once, landing on disk at both ends — and two decades of engineering in this lineage are, from the accountant's chair, a sustained campaign against the shuffle's invoice: combine locally before shipping — the paper's combiner, a reduce run early, on one machine's own output, so that less of it travels; compress in flight; and, in the successors, keep the regrouped data in memory rather than paying the disk twice.

What did the restriction buy? Everything, sir, and by now you can derive it yourself. Pure functions commute with re-execution — run a map task twice and the world is the same, which is episode seven's idempotence arriving by another staircase. So a failed task is simply run again; a lost machine's tasks are re-dealt like cards; fault tolerance stops being a protocol and becomes a scheduler's habit. And because the functions are pure and the data is huge, the scheduler performs the inversion that pays debt number twenty-one tonight: move the computation to the data. The map task is dispatched to the machine that already holds its chunk — shipping a kilobyte of code to a gigabyte of data rather than the reverse — and where things live, last week's entire subject, becomes where things run: partitioning data was always partitioning the work it attracts, and MapReduce merely said so aloud, with a scheduler.

One mechanism in that paper deserves its own paragraph, because the course has business with it in the finale. The authors observed that jobs were routinely delayed not by failures but by stragglers — machines that had not crashed, merely slowed: a failing disk retrying, a noisy neighbour, the doppelgänger's crashed-versus-slow in overalls yet again. Waiting for the slowest of ten thousand tasks meant every job ran at the speed of its unluckiest machine. Their remedy, called backup tasks, is two sentences of pure this-course: when a task lingers near the end of a job, launch a duplicate on another machine and take whichever finishes first. Purity makes the race safe — both runners compute the same answer, the loser is discarded, no harm done — and the tail of the job's latency is cut dramatically for a few percent of wasted work. Do the arithmetic that makes the remedy compulsory, because it is the tail arithmetic of episode six's percentile commandment, resurfacing: if each task independently runs slow one time in a hundred, a job of ten tasks usually escapes — but a job of ten thousand tasks contains, in expectation, a hundred slow ones, and its finish line waits for the slowest of them; at scale, the rare event is a certainty and the job's latency is set by the tail, not the mean. The paper reports the fix's price and prize with engineering candour: a few percent of duplicated work, nearly a third of the job's latency returned. Racing duplicates against the slow, sir: hold the technique close, because in the finale ten thousand accelerators will face the same unluckiest-machine arithmetic every few seconds, and the register opens a fresh entry tonight to make sure the appointment is kept.

The bureaucratic descendants followed — query languages compiling to chains of map-reduces, so the analysts could write in a dialect of the familiar while the fleet did the shuffling; whole data warehouses rebuilt on the pattern; an ecosystem with a menagerie of project names sir mercifully need not memorize, since their common anatomy was always the same two functions and a shuffle — and with the descendants the pattern's cost became visible: every stage wrote its entire output to disk, replicated, before the next stage read it back; a ten-stage pipeline paid the storage tax ten times, and iterative computations — the machine-learning workloads sharpening their appetite in the wings — paid it every lap. The tax bit hardest exactly where the industry's appetite was growing fastest: iterative work — the clustering algorithm refining its centres, the ranking computation passing scores around a graph, the model fitted by repeated passes — where the same data is revisited lap after lap, and the pattern's write-everything-between-stages discipline turned every lap into a disk pilgrimage. The generalization arrived in two thousand twelve from Berkeley: Spark, Zaharia and colleagues, and its idea earns the careful statement because it is the lineage's second inversion. A Spark dataset is a resilient distributed dataset: a collection, partitioned across the fleet, defined not by its bytes but by its lineage — the paper's own word for it — the recipe of pure transformations that produced it from stable inputs. Datasets live in memory, uncommitted, unreplicated; and when a machine dies, taking its partitions with it, the system does not restore from a copy — there is no copy — it recomputes: reads the lost partitions' lineage backward to the last stable ancestor and replays the transformations, for those partitions alone. Recompute, don't replicate: fault tolerance as bookkeeping about provenance rather than as redundant bytes; the lineage is kilobytes where the data is terabytes, and purity — always purity, sir — guarantees the second cooking tastes exactly as the first. Two honest footnotes complete the idea, and both are in the paper. Lineages are not equally kind, and Spark's own vocabulary marks the difference: a transformation whose every output partition depends on one input partition — a map, a filter — is a narrow dependency, and recomputes a lost partition from one ancestor, cheaply; but a transformation that shuffles — a grouping, a join — is a wide dependency, making every output partition depend on all input partitions, so that a single lost partition downstream of a shuffle drags the whole wide ancestry back into the kitchen. Spark's answer is to let the engineer checkpoint — materialize a dataset durably, truncating the lineage — precisely at the expensive waists; recompute-versus-replicate is thereby a dial, not a dogma, turned per stage against the shuffle boundaries. And memory is a lease, not a promise: datasets are cached as advice, evicted under pressure, recomputed on demand — the recipe always the fallback, which is what lets the promise be cheap. The course files the whole idea beside episode five's determinism diet and episode eight's Calvin: agreement on inputs and recipe, with the outputs derived rather than negotiated, keeps proving to be the cheapest agreement on the menu.

Part Two — The Log Ascendant

Now the evening's title character, and payment on debt number fifteen. Kreps's post distilled what LinkedIn had built in Kafka: take episode five's log — the replicated, append-only, numbered sequence — strip away the parliament's other furniture, and offer the log itself, alone, as a public utility. A Kafka topic is a set of partitions; each partition is a log: producers append records to it, the records receive consecutive offsets, and the partition is replicated across brokers by a leader-and-followers arrangement whose ancestry sir can trace blindfolded. That is the whole product, and before the refusals, the physical insight that makes it economical, because it is delightfully unfashionable: the log is the disk's favourite data structure. Random access is where disks — and, differently, their solid-state successors — go to suffer; sequential appends and sequential reads are where they excel, by orders of magnitude; and a structure that only ever appends at the tail and reads in runs lives entirely on the hardware's happy path. Kafka's designers observed that a pedestrian disk, used sequentially, outruns a fashionable memory grid used badly — and that retention therefore costs shelf space rather than performance: keeping a week of the estate's events is not an extravagance but a line item, and the replayability that the week buys is, as we shall see, the recovery story of the entire modern stack. The replication beneath, sir, you may reconstruct from episodes five and six without my help: each partition has a leader taking appends, followers fetching in order, and a committed offset that advances as the in-sync replicas confirm — the parliament's furniture, rearranged for throughput, with the fine print episode six taught you to read still printed on the tin.

And the discipline of what the product refuses to do is the design.

First refusal: global order. Order is guaranteed within one partition and nowhere else — and here episode nine cashes in directly, because a partition is a shard of the stream, placed and keyed by last week's arithmetic: choose the partition key so that the records whose order matters share a partition — all of one customer's events together, ordered; different customers interleaved arbitrarily, and lawfully. Order, sir, is a per-partition commodity: within the shard it is free, across shards it costs a parliament, and Kafka's entire scalability rests on declining to buy what most workloads do not need. Interrogate a global-ordering demand once, the way the review should: Bertie's order events and the aunt's order events interleave differently at two consumers — who is injured? Bertie's own events arriving out of order would corrupt his story: deposit before account-opening, refund before purchase — so his events share a key and a partition, and his story is ordered. But whether his Tuesday purchase is filed before or after the aunt's Tuesday purchase is a fact no customer, no invariant, and no auditor consults — the only observers who could notice are cross-customer aggregates, and those group by window, not by sequence. Most global-order requirements dissolve under the question ordered for whom; the residue that survives — a true global sequence, an auction's book — is episode four's business and priced accordingly. The whiteboard will say it plainly: buy only the order you need, and key your partitions to need less of it.

Second refusal: delivery bookkeeping. The queues of the older tradition tracked, per message, per consumer, whether it had been delivered, acknowledged, redelivered — the broker as anxious postmaster, its state growing with its conscience. Kafka's log stores nothing of the kind: records sit at their offsets, immutably, for a retention period measured in days; and each consumer holds merely its own offset — the consumer offset, in the field's own speech: the position up to which it has read, a bookmark, a cursor, nothing grander. A committed offset, note well, is not a receipt: the broker does not know or care what has been processed, only the consumer knows, by where its bookmark stands; consuming is reading, not taking; ten consumer groups read the same log at ten different offsets without disturbing one another or the records. And the bookmark is the recovery story entire: a crashed consumer resumes from its last committed offset and re-reads — replayable input, the first leg of a trinity the closing whiteboard will assemble. The rewind is not a failure mode, sir; it is the product's finest feature: yesterday's bug in the consumer is repaired by moving the bookmark back to yesterday and reading again, an operation the anxious-postmaster tradition simply cannot offer, having thrown the letters away upon delivery.

And one Friday from the trade's collective memory, staged briefly, because it is the rewind's finest hour. A deploy at four o'clock — the hour has form, sir — ships a consumer with a subtle bug: it processes every record and quietly writes rubbish. The rubbish flows all weekend; Monday's dashboards confess. In the anxious-postmaster tradition this is a catastrophe of the first order — the letters were consumed, acknowledged, destroyed; the weekend's truth is gone, and the recovery plan is called the backup, may it exist. In the log tradition it is a chore: fix the bug, reset the group's committed offsets to Friday afternoon, replay the weekend through the corrected code into a fresh view, cut over. The estate's memory was never in the consumer, sir; the consumer was always disposable. Teams that have performed this manoeuvre once do not return to the postmaster.

Stage the reading arrangements briefly, because two episode-nine cameos are hiding in them. Consumers organize into groups — a group is one logical reader, its members dividing the topic's partitions among themselves: with twelve partitions and three members, each member reads four; the assignment is a little placement problem, and when a member joins or fails, the group rebalances — partitions reassigned, committed offsets handed over — with all of last week's craft applicable in miniature, including the storms: a flapping member that triggers rebalance after rebalance can stall a group's reading entirely, and the remedies are last week's dampers, verbatim. And the parallelism ceiling is set at the keyboard: a topic's partition count bounds its group's useful width — twelve partitions feed at most twelve readers — so the partitioning decision of episode nine is also the concurrency budget of every downstream consumer, decided, as usual, earlier than anyone realized it mattered.

And observe what the refusal purchased operationally, sir, because it is the part the anxious postmaster never could: the broker's work per record is constant — append, replicate, serve reads by offset — regardless of how many consumers exist, how far behind they lag, or whether they ever return. A consumer group down for maintenance costs the broker nothing but shelf space; a new analytics team subscribing to a year-old topic is merely a new bookmark at offset zero; the log scales its readership the way a library does and a courier service cannot.

Third: compaction, the refinement that makes the log a database's memoir. For topics whose records are keyed updates — customer seventeen's address is now such-and-such — retention by time discards history the business may not miss, but a compacted topic instead retains, per key, at least the latest record, discarding superseded ancestors at leisure — and deletion, sir will be pleased to hear, is handled by an old acquaintance: a record with a key and a null body is a tombstone, retained long enough for every reader to witness the death before the stone itself is cleared; episode six's prop, reprised without alteration, because resurrection-by-replay is the same disease as resurrection-by-anti-entropy. The compacted log is then a full snapshot of current state and a subscription to its changes, in one structure — replay it from the top and you have rebuilt the table; keep reading and you stay current. Whence the phrase that organized a decade of architecture, Kreps's own: the database turned inside out. A classical database is a current-state table with a log hidden in the cellar, feeding replicas episode six taught you about; invert it — publish the log as the primary artifact, and let every table, cache, index, and search engine in the estate be a materialized view, a consumer group with a cursor, rebuilding itself by replay whenever its logic changes. The log becomes the estate's spinal cord: systems communicate not by calling one another — a coupling of availabilities, episode one's oldest tax — but by writing to and reading from the shared, durable, replayable sequence, each at its own pace, each rewindable on its own schedule. Stage the payoff once at Bertie's shop, because the inside-out doctrine earns its keep on an ordinary Tuesday: the item topic, compacted, feeds the search index, the price cache, the recommendations table, and the warehouse's analytics — four views, four consumer groups, four cursors. The search team ships a better analyzer: they do not migrate a database — they start a fresh index from offset zero, let it chew through the compacted history at its own pace while the old index serves, and cut over when the new cursor reaches the head; rollback is the same trick backward. The cache is suspected of corruption: discard it entirely and replay — the cache was never the truth, merely a view of the log, and views are disposable by construction. That operational lightness — rebuild anything by rereading, trust nothing but the sequence — is what the inside-out slogan actually purchases, sir, and why its converts speak of it with the fervour previously reserved for version control, which is, one notes, the same idea applied to code.

And a wry beat to close the part, because the coordination dividend of episode five has compounded once more. Kafka's brokers, for their metadata — which brokers live, who leads each partition — leaned for years on ZooKeeper, the consensus kernel in its standard cameo. In its maturity the project retired the external kernel and folded the machinery inside: KRaft — the name is a pun on Raft, sir, and pronounced, the project insists, simply as craft — in which the brokers' metadata is itself a Raft-replicated log managed by the brokers themselves. Attend to the shape of the moment: the log-as-a-service, having grown up under the consensus kernel's guardianship, came of age by becoming its own parliament — the metadata log and the data logs, one species at last, and episode five's dividend collected by the very system that popularized collecting it. The operational motives were the usual honest ones — one system to run instead of two, faster failover, a metadata capacity ceiling lifted — but the course savours the taxonomy: the estate's map, episode nine insisted, wants a court of record; Kafka's answer, in the end, was that the finest court of record it knew was the product it already sold. The course notes, with a valet's satisfaction, that the field's plumbing is slowly agreeing with its theory.

Part Three — The River and the Table, and Time Redux

The old industry org chart put batch — nightly jobs over finished files — in one department and streams — messages, reactions, dashboards — in another, with different tools, different teams, and different bugs. The log dissolves the department wall, and the dissolution is one sentence: a stream is an unbounded table being read in motion; a table is a stream compacted to now. The nightly batch job is merely a consumer whose cursor jumps a day at a time; the dashboard is a consumer whose cursor rides the head of the log; the same log serves both, and the distinction that organized buildings full of engineers reduces to a bookmark's velocity. This is debt twenty-one's second instalment, if sir is keeping score: computation partitioned not only across space — the shuffle's business — but across time, batch and stream as two speeds of one dataflow over one structure.

But the dissolution revives this course's oldest ghosts, and they arrive, as promised in episode two, wearing lag. A batch job over yesterday's file knows the day is over; a stream processor computing "orders per hour" must answer a question the file never posed: when is an hour finished? The records flowing in carry two different times, and confusing them is the beginner's injury in this trade. Event time is when the thing happened — the timestamp in the record, stamped at the source. Processing time is when the record reaches the processor — the clock on the wall as it arrives. The windows the business asks for come in a small taxonomy the desk formalizes — tumbling windows, back to back like paving stones, one per hour; sliding windows, overlapping, the last hour as of every minute; session windows, bounded by gaps in a key's activity, the shopper's visit ending when the shopper does — and every one of them is a grouping by event time, which is precisely the grouping the network declines to deliver in order. And between them lies everything episode one and two taught: delays, retries, a phone in a tunnel replaying its buffered afternoon at six o'clock, a partition healing and disgorging an hour of a datacentre's backlog in one minute. Stage the classic offender, because every mobile estate meets him weekly: Bertie rides the four o'clock train through the long tunnel, tapping purchases into his telephone the while; the application, sensibly, buffers what it cannot send. At six, the train emerges; the telephone disgorges two hours of commerce in four seconds; and the processor receives, at six, a burst of records truthfully stamped four-oh-five, four-twelve, four-forty. The four o'clock window closed its books an hour ago — or did it, and on whose authority? Sort the ledger by arrival and the afternoon's history is fiction; hold every window open forever against possible tunnels and no total is ever final. Every honest computation over "when things happened" must group by event time while living in processing time — which returns us, inevitably, to the question this course has never been allowed to escape: how long must one wait to be sure the past has fully arrived? Episode three's answer stands — in an asynchronous world, forever — and so the trade adopted, from the MillWheel and Dataflow lineage at Google, Akidau and colleagues, the instrument this course respects most: the honest confession. A watermark is the system's declared belief, flowing through the dataflow as a marker in the stream: no further records with event time earlier than such-and-such are expected. When the watermark for two o'clock passes an operator, the operator may close the two o'clock window, emit its total, and release its state — acting not on certainty, which episode three forbids, but on a stated, tunable, monitorable assumption; episode four's partial synchrony, wearing a timestamp. Two mechanics complete the instrument. Watermarks propagate: an operator fed by several upstream channels holds its own watermark at the minimum of its inputs' — it may not declare two o'clock closed while any tributary still owes two o'clock records — so one slow partition holds back the whole dataflow's sense of time. That detention has a name the trade uses at every layer of the stack, and sir should leave the evening carrying it: head-of-line blocking, one laggard at the front of a queue detaining everything ready behind it — practised here upon time itself, and the doppelgänger's oldest trick given yet another stage. The monitoring graph every streaming operator watches first is precisely watermark lag, per input. And watermarks come in two honesties: the perfect watermark, available when the source itself can promise completeness — a log partition read in order can — and the heuristic watermark, estimated from observed lateness distributions everywhere else, wrong at a measured rate. The instrument has its own failure mode, and the operators know it by heart: a watermark that stalls — one idle source partition, one stuck consumer — freezes every downstream window in amber, totals never finalizing, state never released, the pipeline healthy by every throughput metric and dead by the only one that matters; whence the standard furniture of idle-source timeouts and watermark alarms, and the reviewer's question that goes with them: what advances your watermark when nothing arrives, and who is paged when nothing does? And because assumptions are sometimes wrong, the machinery is honest twice over: records arriving behind the watermark — the late data — are counted, surfaced, and handled by declared policy: dropped with a metric, or admitted within an allowed lateness that reopens the window and emits a correction — and the correction itself is a design decision with downstream manners: a refined total supersedes its predecessor, so the sink must treat window results as keyed updates rather than immutable pronouncements, which is to say the output wants a compacted log too; honesty about time, once admitted anywhere in the pipeline, propagates its paperwork all the way to the end. Sir will recognize the entire pattern: the watermark is a failure detector for time — eventually accurate in the happy climate, explicitly fallible always, with the cost of its mistakes priced and the response to them written down. The ghosts of episode two are not exorcised, sir; they are employed, with job descriptions: the clock that cannot be trusted now files a confessed estimate; the message that may be late now has a declared budget for its lateness; the "no now" that haunted the season is answered, in production, by a portfolio of nows — event time for truth, processing time for freshness, the watermark brokering between them — each labelled, each monitored, none pretending to be the one true clock the world declined to provide.

Part Four — Exactly-Once, Prosecuted

Now the courtroom, and let the injury be exhibited before the law, because the law is dry and the injury is not. Bertie's pipeline processes payment instructions from the log: read the record, execute the wire transfer, commit the offset. At half past four, the process executes a transfer — the money moves — and crashes before the offset is committed. The supervisor restarts it; it resumes from the last committed offset; the same record is read again, and the machinery, blameless and obedient, executes the transfer again. The customer has paid twice; the pipeline reports perfect health; and the postmortem will contain a sentence sir can write from here: "the system provides at-least-once delivery, and the sink was not idempotent." That double payment — not lost data, but repeated effect — is the entire subject of this part, and no phrase in this industry's brochures has caused more confusion per syllable than the one that claims to prevent it: exactly-once. Tonight it is cross-examined properly. The charge sheet distinguishes two defendants that marketing insists on conflating. The first: exactly-once delivery — the claim that a message will arrive at its recipient once, no more, no fewer, as a property of the channel. The prosecution calls its oldest witness — the Two Generals, subpoenaed from episode one. The final acknowledgment of any conversation may be lost; the sender who has not heard it cannot distinguish a delivered message from a drowned one — and must choose, forever, between resending, which risks twice, and desisting, which risks never. Exactly-once delivery, over a fair-loss network, in the presence of crashes, is not difficult, sir: it is impossible in exactly the sense episodes one and three made precise, and every vendor claiming it as a transport property is selling a phrase the mathematics does not underwrite. Case dismissed.

The second defendant walks free, and the distinction is the entire engineering discipline of this part: exactly-once effect — the guarantee that however many times the message arrives, the state of the world advances as if it had been processed once. This is achievable, routinely achieved, and built from exactly three materials, all of them already on the course's shelf. Idempotence: make the operation absorb repetition — set the balance to a stated value rather than add to it; upsert the row keyed by the record's identity rather than insert a fresh one; add the tag to the set, where the set ignores duplicates — episode seven's algebra hired as plumbing; then at-least-once delivery, the honest and cheap contract, becomes indistinguishable from the impossible one. The craft is in the rewrite: "increment the count" resists idempotence, but "record that event seventeen occurred, and let the count be the size of the recorded set" embraces it — the same re-drafting-toward-accumulation that CALM licensed in episode seven, practised here as a plumbing discipline; many an operation is non-idempotent only because it was phrased as a verb when it wanted to be a fact. Deduplication: when the operation cannot be made idempotent, make the processing remember — stamp each record with a unique identifier of its own, an idempotency key in the trade's usual phrase, keep a ledger of keys already applied, and check before applying; the duplicate arrives and is recognized, greeted, and discarded. Three clauses of fine print, each a production scar: the ledger must persist across crashes atomically with the state it protects — a dedup table that dies with the process protects nothing, and one updated in a separate transaction merely relocates the double-payment window; the check-then-apply must itself be atomic against concurrent duplicates — two copies of the record arriving at two workers must not both pass the check, which puts a uniqueness constraint or a single-writer partition under the ledger; and the ledger's window must outlast the retry horizon — remember keys for an hour while the upstream retries for a day, and the day's tail replays as fresh business. The sizing is honest arithmetic — message rate, times key size, times horizon — and the desk sets it with numbers; the answers are usually gigabytes, which is to say: affordable, and cheaper than the apology. Transactional sinks: when the effect and the bookmark can be committed together, atomically — the output rows and the new offset in one transaction, episode eight's machinery at last earning its keep in this hall — then a crash between the two, the classic double-processing window, is closed by construction. Replay Bertie's disaster under the repair: Gussie's sink writes the transfer's ledger row and advances the stored offset, one local transaction, one commit. Crash before the commit: neither happened; replay re-reads the record and performs both — once. Crash after: both happened; replay resumes beyond the record and touches nothing. The window has no interior, because the two effects were never two — the entire pattern is episode eight's oldest advice, co-locate what commits together, applied to the bookmark and its consequences.

Kafka's own transactional machinery deserves the outline the plan requests, because it assembles all three materials and one old friend. A producer enrolls with a persistent identity and receives an epoch — and sir smiles already, recognizing episode five's fencing token in a new uniform: a resurrected or duplicated producer instance carries a stale epoch and is refused by the brokers, the zombie fenced at the door. Idempotent production — sequence numbers per partition, brokers discarding repeats — makes append-at-least-once into append-once. And the transaction wraps a batch of appends across partitions together with the consumer's offset commit into one atomic, all-or-nothing unit, consumers electing to read only committed records — machinery whose internals sir can name on sight: a transaction coordinator, prepare-and-commit markers written into the partitions, the two-phase ceremony of episode eight conducted entirely inside the log, with the markers playing the court of record and the epochs fencing the officiants. The season's instruments do not multiply, sir; they redeploy. The result, advertised as exactly-once semantics, is precisely exactly-once effect within the log's jurisdiction: the pipeline whose inputs, outputs, and bookmarks all live in the log gets the guarantee end to end, at a price paid in the usual currencies — a coordinator conversation per transaction, and a visibility lag for read-committed consumers, who must wait at the oldest transaction still open rather than drink from the head — one dawdling transaction holds back every record appended behind it, however committed; nothing in episode eight got cheaper by moving indoors. And the honest default deserves restating before the machinery's glamour obscures it: for the great majority of pipelines — metrics, views, caches, anything idempotent by nature or by the rewrite — plain at-least-once delivery with idempotent application is simpler, faster, and exactly as correct; the transactional apparatus is for the money paths, and knowing which of your paths are money paths is, as ever, the actual engineering. The jurisdiction's border, sir, is where tonight's sabotage lives: the moment the pipeline's effect steps outside the log — an email sent, a payment initiated, any side door the transaction cannot wrap — the guarantee stops at the threshold, and Bertie's double payment walks through exactly that door; the desk's problem this week exhibits it wire by wire. There is a general principle glinting under this border, one the course has met in fragments all season and will meet formally next week under its proper name — the end-to-end argument: a guarantee manufactured in the middle of a system extends only as far as the middle reaches, and correctness at the ends — the sink that pays, the mailer that sends — must be arranged at the ends, whatever the middle promises. File the glint, sir; next week cashes it.

Part Five — The Photograph of the River

And so to the set piece, and the payment of the register's oldest debt. The scene: a streaming job of the modern kind — Flink is the reference house, and the mechanism is from Carbone and colleagues, two thousand fifteen — arranged as a dataflow, and let sir have the whole cast before anything moves. Bingo is the source: he reads the partitioned log — two partitions, which he works as two parallel source tasks, so that two channels of records leave him. Tuppy is the counting operator: both of Bingo's channels feed him, and he keeps running totals per key — state, the accumulated memory of everything processed. Gussie is the sink: he writes results onward. Records flow continuously; the totals climb continuously; and the operators, note, live on different machines with the Post between them, carrying record streams in order per channel. Weigh the stakes of Tuppy's state before the machinery, because state is what separates this hall from Part One's. A batch job's tasks were pure and stateless — recompute anything from the inputs, and Spark's recipes made even that bookkeeping cheap. But Tuppy's totals are the accumulation of everything since the job began — running counts over millions of keys, sessions in progress, joins half-matched — and the job began weeks ago; recompute-from-genesis is a recovery plan measured in days of replay, and the log's retention may not even reach that far. Streaming state is precisely the part of the computation that purity cannot cheaply regenerate, which is why it must be photographed; and the question the machinery must answer: if Tuppy's machine dies at half past four, taking the totals with it, how does the job resume without either losing the afternoon or counting it twice?

The naive answers fail instructively, and the second deserves numbers, because its failure is the whole motivation. Pause the world and copy the state — the estate has no pause, sir; episode two settled that, and the customers would notice the attempt. Snapshot each operator on its own schedule, then: Bingo photographs his bookmark at four o'clock sharp, offset one thousand; Tuppy photographs his totals at five past, by which time he has absorbed records up to offset one thousand two hundred. Now the crash, and the restore: Bingo rewinds to offset one thousand and replays; Tuppy's restored totals already contain the two hundred records Bingo is about to send again. Two hundred records counted twice, and no amount of care at either machine alone fixes it — the defect is between them, in the photographs' disagreement about which instant they depict. What is wanted is a consistent photograph — one logical instant across machines that share no clock. The course has been here before, sir. Attend to the mechanism, and to a rising sensation of acquaintance.

The job manager, on a timer, injects into each source a barrier — a special marker record, stamped with a checkpoint number; imagine a coloured card dropped into each partition's stream. Bingo, on swallowing the card, does two things: records his own position — the offsets up to which he has read, his bookmark — and passes the card downstream, into the record stream itself, where it flows in order among the records: everything ahead of the card belongs to the old epoch, everything behind it to the new; the card is a moving frontier in the river.

Tuppy, you will recall, has two inlets — one channel from each of Bingo's two source tasks — and so two cards to wait for. Here is the one subtle rule, the rule that makes the photograph consistent: when the card arrives on his first inlet, Tuppy does not yet snapshot; he pauses that inlet — buffering or holding its subsequent records, which are new-epoch — and continues draining the other inlet's old-epoch records into his totals, until the card arrives there too. Cards on all inlets: the old epoch is entirely absorbed, the new not yet touched; now Tuppy photographs his totals — asynchronously, in the background, while resuming both inlets — and passes the card on to Gussie, who does likewise at the sink's threshold.

The alignment has a price, and the honest narrator names it: while Tuppy holds one inlet shut, that inlet's upstream fills its buffers — a deliberate, bounded application of backpressure, the healthy refusal of work that episode eleven will treat as a first-class discipline; under heavy skew the pause can lengthen, and the modern engines offer an unaligned variant that photographs the in-flight records themselves rather than waiting — trading a fatter photograph for a shorter pause, and reproducing thereby, sir, with almost comic fidelity, the original algorithm's channel recording — but I am ahead of the reveal.

When every operator's photograph has landed in stable storage, the checkpoint is complete: one coherent picture — sources' bookmarks, operators' totals, sinks' positions — of a single logical instant that no wall clock anywhere witnessed. Recovery is then almost anticlimactic: on Tuppy's death, the job resets every operator to the last complete photograph, rewinds the sources to the photographed bookmarks — replayable input, the log's gift, doing the load-bearing work — and plays on; the interval since the checkpoint is re-processed, and with the sinks transactional or idempotent per Part Four, re-processed into exactly-once effect. The river resumes as if the afternoon's misfortune had been edited out of the footage. With numbers, for the operator's intuition: checkpoints every minute; the crash at four-forty catches the last complete photograph at four-thirty-nine; recovery restores the totals of four-thirty-nine, rewinds the sources one minute, and re-drinks sixty seconds of river at replay speed — typically far faster than real time, the log serving sequential reads at the disk's happy pace — so the estate is current again within moments, having neither lost the minute nor counted it twice. The checkpoint interval is thereby a dial with an honest label: how much river you are prepared to re-drink, sir, priced against how often you photograph.

Now the reveal, sir, with the full ceremony the constitution allots it. In episode two, this course walked an algorithm for photographing a system that refuses to hold still: markers injected at a node, propagated along every channel; each process recording its state on first marker; channel contents captured as the records arriving between marker and marker; the whole yielding a consistent cut — a photograph in which no effect appears without its cause. It was published in nineteen eighty-five by Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport; the course spent an evening on it and has kept it on the shelf, under an eight-week-old promissory note, ever since. Compare, clause by clause: the barrier is the marker; the source injection is the initiation; align-then-snapshot at Tuppy is the marker rule — record on first marker, capture the straggling channel contents until the marker arrives on each remaining channel; the completed checkpoint is the consistent cut. The asynchronous barrier snapshot is the Chandy–Lamport algorithm in industrial dress. The tailoring amounts to two alterations, and the desk sets both as the correspondence's exercise: the dataflow is acyclic, so markers flow one way and terminate at the sinks, sparing the general algorithm's care with arbitrary topologies; and where the original recorded the channels — captured in-flight records as part of the photograph — the industrial edition may instead rewind the source, the log's replayability standing in for channel capture, since re-reading the river reproduces whatever was in flight (and the unaligned variant, forced to photograph in-flight records after all, restores the channel recording verbatim, closing the circle). Alterations of dress, sir; the theorem inside is the nineteen eighty-five theorem, unchanged: markers flowing with the traffic partition all events into before and after, and a photograph assembled at the partition line is consistent, no pause, no clock, no now required. And permit the historical note its half minute, for it sweetens the payment: Chandy and Lamport wrote the algorithm for the analysts of the eighties — detecting termination, catching deadlocks, debugging — the photograph as an instrument of inspection; the notion that it would one day be the beating recovery heart of planetary accounting pipelines, executed every sixty seconds by machinery its authors never imagined, appears nowhere in the paper and everywhere in the industry. Forty years from theorem to overalls, sir, and the overalls fit without alteration. The photograph's debt: presented, and paid in full. The listener operating a stream processor tonight is operating a running instance of a proof this course walked eight episodes ago, and I confess the wonder budget of the evening is spent precisely here — not on the machinery being new, but on its being old: the field's plumbing and its theory, forty years apart, humming the same tune in different registers.

The part closes by assembling the trinity the whiteboard will carry, because the photograph alone recovers nothing. Replayable input — the log, retaining and rewindable, so the past can be re-read; deterministic-enough compute — operators whose replay converges to the same totals, purity's household edition, and the "enough" earns its adjective: an operator that consults the wall clock, draws randomness, or calls an external service mid-record will replay into a different world, and the disciplines are standard — take time from the record and the watermark, never the wall; seed randomness from the record's identity; fence external calls behind Part Four's materials — with the desk pinning each caveat; snapshotted state — the consistent photograph, so replay begins from coherence rather than from the beginning of time. Name all three in a design review, sir, or own none of them: a pipeline with snapshots but unreplayable input restores a memory of a world it cannot revisit; replayable input with nondeterministic compute replays into a different world; and both without the photograph replay from genesis, which is a recovery plan measured in days. The trinity is the whole recovery doctrine of the modern dataflow estate, and each leg, sir will notice, is an episode of this course wearing overalls: the log, the determinism diet, the photograph.

Part Six — Architectures, Seams, and the Limits of the Church

Two shorter matters, and then the borders of the parish. First, a paragraph of architectural history, because sir will still meet its vocabulary in design documents. When batch was trusted and streams were new, the Lambda architecture proposed running both: a batch layer recomputing truth nightly from the master dataset, a speed layer approximating the last few hours, and a serving layer stitching the two — every metric computed twice, in two codebases, in two frameworks, by two teams, disagreeing in exactly the ways two implementations of anything disagree. The disagreement was not hypothetical, sir; it was a standing agenda item: month-end arrives, the batch number and the stream number differ by a fraction of a percent, and an engineer is dispatched to determine which is wrong — the answer being, reliably, both, in different clauses — while the business asks why the dashboard and the report cannot agree on how much money exists. Every estate that ran Lambda owns this meeting's minutes. It was fashion with a reason, and the reason deserves its due: the early stream processors genuinely could not be trusted with correctness — no consistent photographs, loose delivery discipline — and the batch layer was the apology, recomputed nightly precisely because the daytime arithmetic was known to leak. The Kappa architecture — Kreps again, coining wryly — is the observation that once the stream processor acquired tonight's machinery, the photograph and the trinity, the apology became redundant: keep one pipeline, the streaming one, and when logic changes, replay the log through the new code into a new view — reprocessing as a rewind, not a second religion; the Friday-poison manoeuvre, promoted from incident response to standing architecture. Fashion, then physics, sir: Lambda was the era when the stream could not be trusted; Kappa is the era when it can; the log's retention is what made the difference, and the audit of any proposed architecture is now one question — is this second codebase an apology for a limitation that still exists?

Second, the everyday dividend, deployed this very week in estates of every size: the outbox pattern, and its industrial sibling, change-data-capture. The sibling first, in one sentence of mechanism: every serious database already keeps a log in the cellar — the write-ahead log that episode six's replicas drink from — and change-data-capture simply taps it, reading the committed changes in commit order and publishing them outward; the database's most private structure, promoted to its most public interface, with an initial snapshot stitched to the tail of changes so a new consumer can build the whole table and then stay current — the compacted topic's trick, performed at the source. The oldest bug in service architecture: a service commits a row to its database and announces the event to the estate — two effects, two systems, no transaction spanning them; crash between, and the estate's story diverges from the database's, the dual-write anomaly, episode eight's lesson ignored at small scale. Stage the injury first, thirty seconds, since sir's estates contain it somewhere tonight: Tuppy's order service commits the order row, then calls the message broker to announce order-placed — and crashes between the two. The order exists; the announcement never fired; the warehouse, the email service, and the ledger, all listening for the event, will never hear of an order that the customer can see in their account. The mirror-image crash — announce first, commit second — manufactures the opposite ghost: an event for an order that does not exist, and a warehouse packing a phantom. No retry discipline fixes either, because the two effects live in two systems with no transaction spanning them. The outbox repairs it with the evening's whole philosophy in miniature: write the announcement into the database itself, an outbox row committed atomically with the business row — one transaction, one system, atomicity where it is cheap — and let a relay tail the database's own log, the change-data-capture stream, carrying outbox rows into Kafka at its leisure, at-least-once, deduplicated downstream by Part Four's materials. The database's cellar log and the estate's public log, joined by a seam — the log as the boundary object between every system and every other, which is Kreps's thesis operating at the scale of a single feature ticket.

And the borders, stated plainly lest the church of the log acquire converts beyond its writ, for tonight has the makings of a theology. The log does not serve queries: finding, filtering, joining the current state is the business of tables, indexes, and episode seven's stores — the log feeds them; it does not replace them; and the estate that answers "where is the customer's address" with "replay the customer topic" has confused the spinal cord with the filing cabinet: the log's read pattern is sequential and total, a query's is selective and immediate, and no amount of enthusiasm converts the one into the other — the inside-out doctrine says derive the filing cabinet from the cord, never abolish it. The log does not arbitrate: appending is not deciding, and two producers racing to append conflicting claims have not resolved their conflict, merely recorded it in order — uniqueness, locks, the race test's whole domain remain episode four's business; a log with a parliament beneath it orders entries, it does not adjudicate their meaning. And the log does not span multi-key invariants: the doctors of episode seven and the officiant of episode eight are not dismissed by publishing their rows as events — an invariant across two streams needs the machinery of eight, or a redesign per seven, exactly as before. The log, sir, is the estate's circulatory system — magnificent at moving truth around the body, no substitute for the organs.

Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, three items, as the plan prescribes.

Item one. Order is a per-partition commodity. Within a partition it is free; across partitions it costs a parliament; so key your partitions to put must-be-ordered records together, and buy no more order than the business requires. Most "we need global ordering" claims dissolve under the question: ordered for whom, and would they notice? And remember that the partition count chosen at the keyboard is also the consumer parallelism ceiling and the ordering granularity, all in one number — the most consequential integer in the deployment file, set, as usual, on day one.

Item two. Exactly-once is idempotence engineering, not delivery magic. Delivery is at-least-once, by theorem — the Two Generals hold the copyright. Effect is exactly-once by materials: idempotent operations, deduplication with a persistent ledger and a sized window, transactional sinks binding effects to committed offsets — and fencing tokens on the producers, because the doppelgänger publishes too. Any brochure claiming the delivery version is selling around a theorem; ask which material they mean — and ask, in the same breath, where the jurisdiction's border runs: every guarantee of this kind holds within a boundary, the money paths cross boundaries by profession, and the double payment lives precisely at the crossing. The end-to-end argument arrives next week to make the point with full honours.

Item three. Recovery is a trinity: replayable input, deterministic-enough compute, snapshotted state. Name all three or own none — and audit the retention against the outage: a log kept for three days and a bug discovered on the fourth is a trinity with a missing leg, discovered at the worst possible hour. The snapshot is Chandy–Lamport in overalls — barriers as markers, alignment as the marker rule, the checkpoint as the consistent cut — and it recovers nothing without the log's rewind and the replay's determinism. In a review, ask where each leg lives, and what breaks if the retention window is shorter than the outage.
