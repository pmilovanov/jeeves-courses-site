# The Parliament of Machines

*An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode One: The Trouble with Other Computers*

## The Computer You Did Not Know Existed

Good day, sir.

In nineteen eighty-seven, Leslie Lamport — a name that will preside over this course the way a portrait of the founder presides over a boardroom — sat down at a workstation to do some work and found that he could not. The machine in front of him was in perfect health. The difficulty lay elsewhere: somewhere in the building, a file server he had never heard of, and had certainly never asked anything of, had died — and his own machine, which depended on it in ways nobody had thought to mention, was consequently useless. He sent an electronic message to his colleagues containing what has become the field's most quoted definition. A distributed system, he wrote, is one in which the failure of a computer you did not even know existed can render your own computer unusable.

It reads as a joke, and it is one — but I invite you to notice, sir, that it is also a precise technical statement, and that every clause in it is load-bearing. *The failure*: not the malice, not the misuse — the ordinary, statistical, actuarial failure. *A computer you did not even know existed*: the system's dependencies exceed any one participant's knowledge, including yours, including its designers'. *Can render your own computer unusable*: the blast radius of a failure is not bounded by the failed component. Every production incident you have ever attended, sir, is a footnote to that sentence.

Consider, by contrast, the machine on a desk, running alone. When a single computer fails, the failure has two properties so ordinary that nobody thinks to be grateful for them. It is *total*: the processor, the memory, the half-finished computation — all of it stops together, in the same instant, as a unit. And it is *honest*: you know. The screen freezes, the fan spins down, the box reboots. There is no ambiguity about whether the machine has failed, no committee is convened to determine its status, and nothing keeps running on the mistaken assumption that everything is fine. A single machine dies the way characters die in opera — visibly, audibly, and with the whole stage aware of it.

Distribution revokes both properties. In a fleet of machines cooperating over a network, failure becomes *partial* — some components die while others carry on, mid-sentence, holding half-finished business — and it becomes *mendacious* — the survivors cannot reliably tell who has died, who is merely slow, and who has been dead for ten minutes while continuing to receive their letters. Nearly everything difficult, strange, and expensive in this field descends from those two revocations. That is tonight's subject, and in a sense it is the season's.

A word about you, sir, before we begin, because this course is built to your measurements. You have operated these systems for some fifteen years. Bigtable, Spanner, Kafka, ZooKeeper, etcd — these are not vocabulary words to you; they are colleagues, and occasionally defendants. You have carried the pager. What you have not had — because almost nobody in industry has — is the theory underneath: the formal models, the impossibility results, the correctness arguments, the taxonomy of promises. So this course inverts the usual arrangement. I shall not be introducing you to systems. I shall be introducing you to the theorems those systems are answers to — theory first, and then, deliberately, a landing on machinery you already operate, so that what you currently know as folklore becomes something you can derive. The register we are aiming for, across twelve episodes, is not "let me tell you about Spanner." It is: "so *that* is why Spanner waits."

Tonight's itinerary, then. First, the honest reasons systems become distributed, and the paradise that is given up. Second, partial failure — the defining pathology. Third, the network itself, which in this course goes by the name of the Post, and the documented case for treating it as an adversary. Fourth, the rules of engagement: the system models under which every claim in this field is made, and outside of which every claim in this field is noise. Fifth, a small staged disaster involving a counter, three machines, and a gentleman buying an umbrella. Sixth — the set piece — two generals on two hills, and the first impossibility proof of the season, conducted properly, from premise to contradiction, entirely in the ear; I shall not be waving my hands at it, tonight or ever, and where a proof is too heavy for the ear you will be told so plainly and directed to the desk. And seventh, the thesis of the entire course, which I shall state now and spend eleven further episodes honouring: nearly everything this field wants to do is impossible in principle and routine in practice, and the gap between those two facts is the syllabus.

And one thing more to fix in your mind before we begin, sir, because he will be in every episode whether or not I name him: the villain. Every good course has one, and ours is not a machine or a network but a *disposition* — the assumption, adopted on purpose and never relaxed, that the worst permitted thing will happen at the worst possible moment. Do not picture a sunny Tuesday afternoon, every machine up, every letter arriving in order. Picture three in the morning on the Saturday of a Black Friday weekend: load at its annual peak, half the on-call roster unreachable on a train, and the one disk that chooses that instant to fill is, of course, the one nobody was watching. It is easier to reason about a wilful opponent than about a fog of bad luck, so we personify all of it as a single patient antagonist — *the adversary* — and grant him three properties. He makes the worst legal move, not the average one. He has read your protocol before he chooses it. And he is never so kind as to fail in the manner you rehearsed for. A system is correct, in this course, only if it survives his *every* move; "it works unless we're unlucky" is not a design but a hope, and hope is not on the syllabus. But — and this is the clause that keeps the subject from despair — the adversary wears a leash. He may do only what the rules permit, and the rules are ours to write, out loud, in advance. A remarkable share of the disasters in this field are simply a system meeting a move it never declared the adversary couldn't make. So we shall always say, before claiming a thing works: against whom, in what weather, on a leash of what length. You will meet him first as the Post, who loses and delays our letters; then as the scheduler who can freeze a healthy machine mid-stride; and, at his worst, as a colleague at the council table who has decided to lie. The same demon, sir. Only the coat changes.

One adversary throughout, one standing cast of machines whom you will meet in part five, and one ledger of promises, which I open tonight and audit publicly. Let us begin.

## Part One — The Reasons We Scatter

Why distribute at all? It is worth asking seriously, because the correct engineering answer to a great many distributed-systems problems is: do not have one. A single modern machine is a formidable object — dozens of processor cores, terabytes of memory, storage measured in units that would have been a research grant's worth of disk not long ago. The ceiling of one box is far higher than fashion admits, and a healthy fraction of the industry's distributed architecture exists below that ceiling, distributed anyway, paying the full tax for none of the benefit. I mention this at the outset so that nothing in the next twelve episodes is mistaken for enthusiasm. We study the machinery of distribution the way one studies maritime law — because the cargo must move, not because one loves the paperwork.

But the cargo must move. There are three honest reasons, and a fourth that is honest in a different way.

The first is scale. Eventually the workload genuinely exceeds any one machine — the index will not fit, the requests will not queue, the model will not train. When the ceiling is real, the only direction remaining is sideways.

The second is geography, which is to say physics. Your users are far away, and light is slow. A signal in optical fibre travels at roughly two thirds the speed of light in vacuum, and the planet is inconveniently large: a round trip from Toronto to Sydney, under absolutely ideal conditions that do not exist, costs on the order of a sixth of a second — before a single machine has done a single unit of work. No amount of engineering budget negotiates with that. If you want the far side of the world to experience your service as responsive, some of your service must live on the far side of the world, and now you have copies, and now the copies must be kept in some kind of agreement, and you have purchased this entire course.

The third is fault tolerance, and it is the deepest, because it contains a beautiful irony that will take us three episodes to fully unfold. One machine is one fate: it shares a power supply, a kernel, a building, a fire. If the service must survive the loss of a machine — or a rack, or a datacentre, or a city — then the service must be more than one machine, in more than one place. We distribute, in other words, *because* machines fail. And the irony, which I shall state now and prove later, is that the act of distributing creates a new and more exotic family of failures — partial ones — that are strictly harder to reason about than the total failure we were fleeing. We escape death and are compensated with ghosts.

And the fourth reason, which decency requires me to include: the organization already did. Systems are frequently distributed not because the workload demanded it but because the company was — because the payments team and the inventory team ship separate services, and the services must talk, and now there is a network in the middle of what might have been a function call. The architecture, as the saying nearly goes, ships the org chart — Conway's law, in the modern and less flattering paraphrase. I pass no judgment, sir; I merely note that the Post does not care why you invited it in. Its charter, which we shall read shortly, applies equally to the noble reasons and the accidental ones.

Now — what is given up. It is worth naming the paradise precisely, because the whole field is an attempt to buy pieces of it back at retail prices. The single machine enjoys two great mercies, so ordinary they are invisible.

The first mercy is *shared memory*. On one machine there is, at any instant, one state. Two threads may squabble over it — that is its own discipline, with its own literature — but the squabble is over a single, authoritative, commonly visible object. There is a fact of the matter about what the value of x is, and everyone is looking at the same fact. Distribute, and this is gone: there are now several copies of x, on several machines, connected by messages that take time, and the question "what is the value of x" quietly stops having a single answer. It becomes — and this is not rhetoric, it is the literal technical situation — a question about *whose* x, *when*, according to *whom*.

The second mercy is *shared fate*, which we have already met: when the machine dies, everything dies together, totally and honestly. No survivors holding half a transaction. No colleague continuing to act on the assumption that you are alive.

And there is a third mercy, so humble it is almost embarrassing to name: the single machine has *one clock*. Every event that happens on it can be placed on a single timeline, and the question "which of these two things happened first" has an answer that everyone can consult. You will perhaps feel that this one, at least, survives distribution — surely the machines can simply synchronize their watches? Hold that thought very carefully, sir, because next week's entire episode is devoted to the discovery — and I use the word discovery advisedly, because it required a genuine act of intellectual imagination, with a rather celebrated precedent in physics — that it does not survive at all. In a distributed system there is, in a sense I will make precise next week, no such thing as *now*.

Shared state, shared fate, shared time. The move from one machine to two forfeits all three at once, and it forfeits them not gradually but categorically. There is no such thing as being slightly distributed. The moment there are two machines and a wire, the full charter applies.

## Part Two — The End of Honest Dying

Let us dwell properly on partial failure, because it is the defining pathology — the thing that makes this field a field, rather than a large instance of ordinary programming.

Picture the fleet: forty machines cooperating on some piece of business. Machine seventeen — mid-operation, holding locks, halfway through updating something — stops. Perhaps its power supply went; perhaps the kernel panicked; perhaps someone in the facilities department was incautious with a cable. Here is the crucial observation: *the other thirty-nine machines were not notified.* There is no bell. Failure on a single machine announces itself totally; failure in a fleet is, by default, silent, local, and discovered only by inference. The remaining thirty-nine continue at full speed, executing a plan that assumed a colleague who no longer exists, and the system as a whole enters a state that no one designed: not the state before the operation, not the state after it, but a third thing — the operation abandoned halfway, its side effects orphaned.

And now the deeper trouble, which I want to build slowly, because it is the primordial confusion of the entire subject and half the season's theorems grow out of it.

You are one of the thirty-nine. You sent machine seventeen a request some while ago. You have heard nothing. What do you know?

Let us enumerate the worlds that are consistent with your evidence — and your evidence, note, is silence, which is to say, nothing.

World one: machine seventeen is dead. It crashed before your request arrived, or just after; either way, it is gone.

World two: machine seventeen is alive but slow. It is paused — perhaps its runtime is collecting garbage, perhaps it is thrashing, perhaps it is simply overloaded — and your request sits in its queue, about to be processed the instant it wakes. It is, so to speak, in the bath. It will be quite embarrassed when it emerges.

World three: machine seventeen never received your request. It is perfectly healthy and entirely ignorant. The network lost your letter in transit. The machine would be delighted to help, had it been asked.

World four — and attend closely to this one, sir, because it is the nastiest of the litter: machine seventeen received your request, *performed it*, and replied — and the network lost the reply. The work was done. The money moved, the row was written, the umbrella was ordered. You simply were not told.

Four worlds. In world one the machine is dead; in worlds two through four it is alive; in world four your operation *has already happened*. And from where you sit, all four worlds present the identical face: silence. There is no experiment you can run from your side of the wire, no cleverness of protocol, that distinguishes them at the moment of decision. You may wait longer — but silence at ten seconds looks exactly like silence at five, and world two can outlast any patience you choose in advance.

Notice what this does to the most natural instinct in engineering: the retry. Silence, so I shall send it again. In world three, the retry is exactly right — the request was lost; sending again is the cure. In world four, the retry is a catastrophe in miniature: the operation happened, and you are now performing it a second time. One instinct; opposite consequences; and no way to know which world you are in. Every duplicate charge, every double-sent email, every twice-applied migration in your career, sir, was this fork in the road, taken blind. We shall spend a good part of episodes ten and eleven on the disciplined answer, and it carries a name I would have you take away tonight and keep: *idempotence*. An operation is idempotent when performing it twice leaves the world exactly as performing it once did — so the cure is not to prevent the second performance but to make it harmless, and the trying-to-prevent-it is provably futile for reasons that arrive within the hour. But tonight I want you to sit with the raw situation: the retry is not safe, and not retrying is not safe either, and this is not a flaw in your tooling. It is the terrain.

And notice what it does to the notion of a timeout. A timeout is dressed up, in every codebase, as a technical parameter — thirty seconds, three, three hundred milliseconds. It is nothing of the sort. A timeout is a *verdict*: at some point you stop waiting and declare a world — usually world one, "it is dead" — without a body, without a witness, on the evidence of silence alone. Sometimes the verdict is wrong, and the consequences of a wrong verdict of death, delivered against a machine that later wakes from its bath and resumes work with all its old authority, are among the most spectacular failures this field has to offer. There is a whole episode's worth of machinery — leases, fencing, epochs — devoted to surviving the wrongly condemned, and it arrives in due course. Tonight, only the diagnosis: *crashed and slow are indistinguishable from across a network.* Not hard to distinguish. Not expensive to distinguish. Indistinguishable — and hold on to that particular word, sir, because at the top of the hour I am going to promote it from an inconvenience to a weapon, give it a name, and use it to prove the first impossibility theorem of the season.

One more small observation before we move to the villain of the piece. Everything I have just described assumed the *machines* were the fragile parties and the messages mostly arrived. The truth is worse in a productive way: the messages are the more interesting failures. To those, now.

## Part Three — The Post

Here is the adversary's first and favourite disguise. For the next twelve episodes the network will go by a single name: **the Post**.

The name is chosen with intent. A network is not a wire; it is a postal service — a vast, layered bureaucracy of queues, sorting offices, and couriers, staffed by parties you have never met, subject to weather you cannot see. Letters go in; letters, mostly, come out. And like any postal service of sufficient age and size, it has a charter — a list of liberties it reserves for itself, printed, as it were, on the back of every stamp. Let me read you the charter, because the entire season is conducted under it.

Item one: the Post may *delay* any letter, for any length of time, without notice and without pattern. Not "up to five seconds." Any length of time. A letter may arrive after the reply to the letter that was sent after it. A letter may arrive next Tuesday.

Item two: the Post may *lose* any letter, silently. There is no return-to-sender. There is no notice of failure. The letter simply never existed, except in the memory of its author.

Item three: the Post may *duplicate* any letter. You wrote it once; it arrives twice, or five times, minutes apart. And note — this clause is redundant, in a sense that should worry you: even a Post that never duplicated would produce duplicates in practice, because the sender, hearing silence, re-sends, and the original was merely delayed. From the recipient's side of the counter, a duplicating Post and a retrying correspondent are the same phenomenon. We manufacture this clause ourselves, out of prudence.

Item four: the Post may *reorder* letters. Two letters, posted in a definite order by the same author to the same recipient, may arrive in the other order. Different routes, different queues, different luck.

And item five — the single mercy, and mind it well, because an entire episode turns on its revocation: the Post does not *forge*. It delivers late, it loses, it repeats, it shuffles — but every letter that arrives was genuinely written by the party whose name is on it, and says what they wrote. Our Post is negligent, not criminal. Later in the season we shall meet correspondents of lower character — letters that lie about their authorship, machines that say different things to different colleagues — and the season will acquire, at that point, both a famous theorem and a certain aunt. But not tonight. Tonight the Post's sins are entirely sins of omission.

Now, a fair question: is this charter not slander? Real networks, you may say, work rather well; TCP — the transmission control protocol — exists precisely to paper over this sort of thing; surely the charter is a theorist's caricature.

Two answers, one principled and one documentary.

The principled answer: TCP is not a counterexample to the charter; it is a *consequence* of it. TCP is what engineering under the charter looks like — sequence numbers against reordering, acknowledgments and retransmission against loss, deduplication against the duplicates the retransmission itself creates. It is a treaty negotiated with the Post, clause by clause, and a rather beautiful one. But read the treaty's fine print: it holds within one connection, while that connection lives. The moment the connection breaks — and it breaks, at every failover, every process restart, every network hiccup longer than a keepalive — you are back at the counter with the charter, holding the oldest question in the field: did my last letter arrive? TCP shrinks the problem's surface; it cannot remove it, and later tonight I shall prove that nothing can.

The documentary answer is where this field is unusually fortunate, because the evidence is published. Some of it is even funny.

Begin in the middle nineteen nineties, at Sun Microsystems, where a body of hard-won corporate scar tissue was condensed into a list known as the *fallacies of distributed computing* — the beliefs that essentially every newcomer to networked systems holds, every one of them false, every one of them expensive. The accounting of authorship varies with the telling; Peter Deutsch is the name on the deed, with the first few clauses commonly credited to colleagues before him and the final clause added later by James Gosling. Eight fallacies. Attend, sir, and as I read them, I invite you to conduct a private audit of your last few production incidents.

Fallacy the first: *the network is reliable.* The founding fallacy — the belief that the Post's charter is a caricature. Everything else tonight is its refutation, so I move on.

Fallacy the second: *latency is zero.* It is not, it is not even small, and — as we priced out with Toronto and Sydney — a floor of it is imposed by the speed of light and is not subject to procurement. Every synchronous call across a network is a bet about latency, and the bet is placed whether or not you noticed placing it.

Fallacy the third: *bandwidth is infinite.* It is finite, it is shared, and it is most finite precisely when everyone wants it — which is to say, during the incident.

Fallacy the fourth: *the network is secure.* A matter chiefly for other courses, though episode five lives on its border.

Fallacy the fifth: *topology does not change.* Machines are replaced, addresses are reassigned, routes reconverge — routinely, automatically, and with particular enthusiasm in the middle of your request.

Fallacy the sixth: *there is one administrator.* There are dozens, they work for different departments and different companies, and they do not attend the same change-review meetings. Somewhere between you and your user, at this moment, a device is being upgraded by someone who has never heard of you.

Fallacy the seventh: *transport cost is zero.* Serialization is compute, bytes are money, and the network interface is a resource with a queue like any other.

Fallacy the eighth: *the network is homogeneous.* The fleet contains every vintage of hardware ever purchased and every version of every protocol ever deployed, simultaneously, forever.

That is the folklore — the field's grandmotherly wisdom. But folklore invites the suspicion of exaggeration, so let me now produce the documentary evidence, because it exists and it is good. In two thousand fourteen, Peter Bailis and Kyle Kingsbury published a piece in the ACM's Queue magazine with the deadpan title "The Network is Reliable" — fallacy number one, hung up as a trophy. It is a catalogue: published, attributable accounts of real network partitions in real production systems — cloud providers, enterprises, research measurement studies — assembled precisely to answer the working engineer who says "come now, how often does the network *actually* fail." The answer, documented incident by documented incident, is: regularly, at every scale, for every reason you can imagine and several you cannot. Switch firmware with novel opinions. Redundant network paths that turned out to share the one component that failed — redundancy on the diagram, correlation in the wiring closet. Maintenance procedures that partitioned the very systems they were maintaining. Load balancers gone philosophical. And my personal favourite entry in the genre, from the trade press of the same era: sharks — actual sharks — developing a taste for undersea fibre-optic cable, and a major provider armouring its trans-Pacific lines in response. The Post's charter, sir, is not pessimism. It is actuarial science. Somewhere between your datacentres, right now, there is a shark.

I assign both texts as tonight's reading, and I shall say a further word about them at the close. For now, take the finding that matters most for our purposes, and it is this: *partitions happen.* The network does not merely lose individual letters; it sometimes splits into provinces, each internally healthy, each unable to reach the other, each — recall part two — unable to distinguish "the other province is dead" from "the other province is fine and the road between us is out." Whole architectures, and one extremely famous theorem that we shall prove in due course and then spend ten minutes rescuing from its own reputation, turn on what a system chooses to do during those minutes. The charter is the terrain. Now let us learn to state, precisely, which terrain a given claim is about.

## Part Four — The Rules of Engagement

Here we arrive at the single most important methodological habit this course will install — the one I would have you carry into every design review, every vendor evaluation, and every reading of every paper for the rest of your career. It is this: **in this field, every claim is conditional on a model, and a claim quoted without its model is not a fact but a noise.**

The theorems of distributed computing do not say "consensus is impossible." They say: *under such-and-such assumptions about timing, and such-and-such assumptions about failure, and such-and-such assumptions about the network*, consensus is impossible. Change the assumptions and the theorem falls silent — and something else becomes provable instead. A great deal of the mischief in our industry — most citations of a certain three-letter theorem in vendor keynotes, for instance, an abuse we shall prosecute properly when the time comes — consists of theorems quoted shorn of their models, like maritime law cited in a desert. We shall not be doing that. Every episode of this course declares its model aloud before proving anything, and every chapter of the desk volume opens with the model in a box, so that no result ever floats free of its premises. Let me now give you the vocabulary — the axes along which the models vary. There are three.

The first axis is **timing** — what the model assumes about how long things take. Three settings matter.

The *synchronous* model is the world with a metronome: there is a known bound on how long any message spends in the Post, and a known bound on how much slower one machine may run than another. Known — that is the operative word. In a synchronous world, silence is informative: if the bound is five seconds and you have heard nothing in six, the sender is provably dead, and the whole crashed-versus-slow confusion of part two dissolves. Synchrony is lovely and largely fictional; real networks offer typical delays, not guaranteed ones, and the tail of the distribution is where incidents live.

The *asynchronous* model is the honest extreme: no bounds whatsoever. The Post may hold a letter for an arbitrary finite time; a healthy machine may pause for an arbitrary finite time. Nothing is guaranteed except that things eventually happen. In an asynchronous world silence carries no information at all — every timeout is a guess, as we said — and the crashed-versus-slow confusion is not merely present but *provably permanent*. This is the model in which the great impossibility results live, including tonight's — and one more celebrated still.

And between them, the setting where real engineering actually happens: *partial synchrony*, articulated in a landmark nineteen eighty-eight paper by Cynthia Dwork, Nancy Lynch, and Larry Stockmeyer. Partial synchrony says: bounds exist, but you do not know them — or, in the alternative formulation, bounds hold, but only eventually, after some unknown period of chaos. It is the network as you actually experience it: usually well-behaved, occasionally berserk, and never willing to certify in advance which mode it is in. Mark this model well, sir. Episode three will prove that in the asynchronous world, consensus is impossible; episode four will show that in the partially synchronous world, it is not only possible but practical — and that pair of results, taken together, is the intellectual foundation on which Paxos, Raft, ZooKeeper, and every consensus system you have ever operated actually rests. The entire industry lives in the gap between those two models.

The second axis is **process failure** — what a machine is permitted to do when it goes wrong. In ascending order of depravity.

*Crash-stop*: the machine halts and never returns. Clean, total, permanent — the gentleman's failure. Much of the classical theory is stated here first, because it is the kindest assumption that is still interesting.

*Crash-recovery*: the machine halts and later returns — and here is the subtlety that generates half the correctness bugs in the wild — it returns having forgotten everything it did not deliberately write to stable storage. A machine that promised something in memory and then crashed has, upon recovery, never promised anything. Protocols that survive crash-recovery are precisely the protocols that keep diaries, and what must go in the diary, and when, is a matter of exact science, not taste. You will meet a sabotage problem on exactly this point at the desk tonight.

*Omission* failures: the machine is alive but drops some of its duties — fails to send what it should, or to receive what it could. A useful halfway house in the theory; in practice often indistinguishable from a bad network day, which is rather the point.

And *Byzantine* failure: the machine may do anything at all — including lie, including send different statements to different colleagues, including collude. The name comes from a nineteen eighty-two paper of considerable theatrical instinct, and the analysis of such failures is one of the field's crown jewels. I defer it, with a small shudder and a promise, to episode five. Until then, our machines fail honestly: they may die, they may forget, but they do not deceive.

The third axis is **the links** — the Post's own charter, formalized. The standard vocabulary, which the desk volume tonight lays out with full precision, comes in layers. At the bottom, *fair-loss* links: the Post may lose any individual letter, but it is not allowed to be *perfectly* malicious forever — if you keep re-sending a letter, unboundedly, to a correspondent who remains alive, it must get through eventually; and the Post does not invent letters from nothing, nor duplicate any single letter infinitely. Fair-loss is the formal name for negligent-but-not-criminal. Above it one *constructs* better service: a *stubborn* link, which delivers reliably because it simply never stops re-sending — brute persistence as an algorithm — and above that a *perfect* link, which delivers reliably, exactly once, without invention. And I choose the word "constructs" with care: perfect links are not found in nature, sir; they are *built*, out of fair-loss links plus acknowledgments plus sequence numbers plus deduplication, and the desk volume will have you build one tonight and prove all three of its guarantees, because the construction is the primal pattern of the field — reliability is never discovered, only manufactured, and always out of unreliable parts.

So: a model is a point on three axes. Timing: synchronous, asynchronous, or partial. Processes: crash-stop, crash-recovery, omission, or Byzantine. Links: fair-loss upward. When a paper says "we assume an asynchronous system of crash-stop processes connected by reliable links," it has told you exactly which world its theorem inhabits, and — just as importantly — which worlds it says nothing about. When a vendor says "our database is consistent and available," it has told you nothing at all, and you are entitled, politely, to ask for the model.

For the record, then, and as our standing default: unless an episode declares otherwise, this course operates in the asynchronous model, with crash-stop processes, over a fair-loss Post. It is the harshest common terrain, and results proved there travel well. Tonight's theorem needs even less — message loss alone will do it — which is part of what makes it such a satisfying opening act.

And now, sir, the theory has earned some theatre. Let me introduce the company.

## Part Five — The Counter That Could Not Count

A course conducted entirely in the ear needs a substitute for diagrams, and ours will be a repertory company. Allow me to present the standing cast, who will perform every protocol in this season.

Our replicas — the machines that hold copies of things — are three gentlemen named **Bingo**, **Tuppy**, and **Gussie**. For scenes requiring five, two further members, Barmy and Oofy, wait on the bench. A few notes on casting, which I shall keep scrupulously consistent, because consistency of character is consistency of memory. Gussie is not fast. He has never been fast. His letters arrive late, his replies arrive later, and we shall find this defect endlessly instructive. Tuppy is reliable but literal-minded: he does exactly what the letter in front of him says, in exactly the order the letters arrive, which sounds like a virtue and will shortly be revealed as a personality. Bingo is the most ordinary of the three, and when a scene requires someone to take the chair, it will usually be Bingo. Our client — the party outside the system, making requests of it — is **Bertie**: forever a client, forever mildly inconvenienced, and tonight, in the market for an umbrella. And carrying every letter among them: the Post, whose charter you have heard.

Why names, rather than "replica one, replica two, replica three"? Because you are listening, sir, not looking. The eye can hold a diagram of anonymous boxes; the ear cannot hold three numbered replicas across a five-message exchange — but it holds Bingo and Tuppy without the slightest effort. The whimsy is load-bearing.

Now, the scene. Our three gentlemen operate the inventory system of a small but ambitious online purveyor of umbrellas. The state they share could not be simpler: a single counter — the running tally of orders placed today. Each of the three holds his own copy, because we have read part one and we want the shop to survive the loss of a machine. The protocol, designed by someone who has not yet taken this course, is the obvious one: a client's order arrives at whichever replica answers; that replica adds one to his copy of the counter; and he writes letters to his two colleagues so that they may do the same. What could be simpler? Attend, sir, to three short performances.

**Performance the first: a letter is lost.** Bertie orders an umbrella. His order lands on Bingo. Bingo's counter reads: one. Bingo writes to Tuppy: add one to the counter. Bingo writes to Gussie: add one to the counter. Tuppy's letter arrives; Tuppy, literal as ever, adds one. Tuppy's counter reads: one. And Gussie's letter — the Post eats it. Quietly. No notice, no return-to-sender; clause two of the charter, exercised. Gussie's counter reads: nought, and will read nought forever, because nothing in this protocol ever re-sends anything.

Tally the damage: Bingo one, Tuppy one, Gussie nought. The system has *diverged* — the copies disagree — and, note well, it has diverged *silently*. No alarm has gone off, because no component considers itself in error. Bingo believes his letters were delivered; Gussie does not know a letter existed. Each machine is locally content, and the ensemble is wrong. That is the signature of distributed failure, and you have seen it, sir, at three in the morning, wearing various costumes.

**Performance the second: the retry.** A new day; the counters read nought again. Bertie orders his umbrella. This time the Post eats the *order itself*, on its way to Bingo. Bertie's app — think of it as his man of business, acting on his behalf with more diligence than insight — waits, hears silence, and does the reasonable thing: it sends the order again. The second copy arrives. Bingo's counter reads: one. So far, a happy story — the retry did its job.

Except that the first letter was not lost. It was *delayed* — clause one, not clause two; even the Post cannot always tell you which clause it is exercising — and some seconds later it ambles in. Bingo, literal in his own way, sees an order and adds one. Bingo's counter reads: two.

Follow the mail from there, one letter at a time, because this is where the damage propagates. Bingo is dutiful, and he wrote to his colleagues for each order he served. So four letters go out: add-one and add-one to Tuppy, add-one and add-one to Gussie. The Post, for once, loses none of them. Tuppy adds one, and later adds one again; Tuppy's counter reads two. Gussie adds one, and later adds one again; Gussie's counter reads two. The system is now in perfect, unanimous agreement — that Bertie bought two umbrellas.

Bertie will be charged twice, and the ledger will balance beautifully, and every machine behaved exactly as designed, and nobody misbehaved at all: the client was prudent, the replicas were faithful, the Post exercised only the liberties printed on the stamp. Hold this performance in mind at the desk tonight, because the obvious remedy — give every order an identifier of its own, and ignore any order whose identifier you have already served — is exactly right, and it too has a proper name: *deduplication*. The sabotage problem waiting for you there is such a scheme with one small, fatal economy in it.

**Performance the third: two letters race.** Same shop, different state — not the counter now, but the note attached to Bertie's order. Bertie writes to Bingo: set the delivery note to "leave with the porter." A moment later he reconsiders — the porter is unreliable — and writes again: set the delivery note to "ring the top bell." Bingo applies them in the order they reached him: porter first, then bell. His copy of the note reads: ring the top bell.

Now watch the mail again, one letter at a time. Bingo forwards both instructions onward — porter and bell to Tuppy, porter and bell to Gussie. Four letters, and this evening the Post loses none of them; its liberty tonight is clause four, reordering. Tuppy's pair arrives in the order Bingo sent it. Tuppy applies porter, then bell. Tuppy's note reads: ring the top bell. And Gussie — Gussie is slow, and Gussie's pair arrives in the other order: bell first, then porter. Gussie, as literal as anyone when he finally gets round to it, applies them as they arrive: bell, then porter. Gussie's note reads: leave with the porter. Divergence again — and this time not from loss but from *reordering*: every letter was delivered, exactly once, and the copies still disagree, because for this kind of state, *order is the whole game*. "Set the note" is not addition; the last writer wins, and the three gentlemen do not agree on who wrote last — indeed, as we shall see next week, the question of who wrote last does not currently even *mean* anything.

And here is a small, glinting observation that I want to leave half-buried tonight and dig up twice more before the season ends. Return to the counter of the first two performances and ask: would *reordering* have hurt it? Suppose Bertie's order and a cancellation — an add-one and a subtract-one — had crossed in the Post and arrived at Gussie in the wrong order. Trace it through: subtract one, then add one, or add one, then subtract one — the final value is identical. Addition does not care about order. The counter, alone among our exhibits, *forgives* clause four entirely, because its operations commute — and had we also cured loss and duplication, all three copies would have converged without any coordination at all, no matter how the Post shuffled the mail. File that away, sir, in a drawer marked *commutativity forgives disorder*. It returns in episode seven wearing formal dress, and in the finale wearing yours.

Now step back and audit the whole scene, because the accounting is the point. Three performances. Divergence by loss; double-charging by duplication-via-retry; divergence by reordering. And observe the thing I most want observed: *not one machine failed tonight.* Bingo, Tuppy, and Gussie were alive, healthy, and obedient throughout. Everything you just heard was the *weather* — the Post's ordinary charter, exercised on a protocol too innocent to have read it. We have not yet introduced crashes; that pleasure is still ahead of us. A newcomer imagines distributed systems are hard because machines die. You know better, sir, and now we have staged it: the mail alone is sufficient for ruin.

One question remains from the wreckage, and it is the deepest one. Bingo says one, Gussie says nought — *which of them is right?* Take the question seriously for a moment and feel it dissolve. Right according to what? There is no master copy; we deliberately declined to have one, back in part one, when we decided the shop must survive the loss of a machine. There is no referee. There is no fact of the matter sitting somewhere that the copies are approximating — the copies are all there is. If we want there to be an authoritative answer, someone must be *appointed* to give it — and appointed in a way that survives the appointee's death, and the Post's interference with the election, and the awkward possibility of two gentlemen each believing himself appointed — a condition the trade calls *split brain*, and one you have almost certainly met under that name. That single problem — creating and maintaining an authority out of parts that fail, over mail that lies about nothing but delivers everything badly — is called consensus, it is the summit of this course's first half, and episodes three, four, and five are its story: first the proof that in our default model it cannot be done at all, and then the beautiful, lawyerly, partially-synchronous ways in which it is done every second of every day, including by the etcd cluster keeping your Kubernetes control plane honest at this very moment.

But before any of that, sir, we owe the season its cornerstone. It is time to prove something impossible.

## Part Six — Two Generals on Two Hills

The problem I am about to state is, by common consent, the oldest impossibility result in distributed computing, and it has the distinction of being comprehensible to anyone and escapable by no one. It appears in the literature in nineteen seventy-five, in a paper by Akkoyunlu, Ekanadham, and Huber on the design of network communications, and it was christened, popularized, and — characteristically — connected to the mundane business of databases three years later by Jim Gray, in his nineteen seventy-eight notes on database operating systems, where he observed that the fable I am about to tell you and the problem of committing a transaction across two machines are the same problem in different dress. Gray's fingerprints, you will find, are on half of this field; this was among the first places he pressed them. The fable runs as follows.

Two divisions of the same army are encamped on two hills. Between them, in the valley, sits the enemy — strong enough to destroy either division alone, too weak to survive both together. Command of the eastern hill belongs to General Alexandra; the western, to General Basil. If both divisions attack at dawn, they win. If either attacks alone, it is annihilated. And the only way the generals can communicate is by messenger — a rider who must cross the valley by night, through enemy lines, and who may, on any given crossing, be captured. Captured silently, mind: no gunshot heard on the hills, no notice of failure. The messenger simply does not arrive, and neither general knows.

Alexandra has surveyed the ground and fixed the plan: attack at dawn. Her problem is not strategy; her problem is *coordination*. She must arrange matters so that both divisions attack together, or neither attacks at all — because a lone attack is worse than no attack — and every instrument she has for arranging it can vanish in the valley.

Feel the problem before we formalize it. Alexandra's first instinct: send a rider with the order — "attack at dawn" — and prepare her division. But if that rider is captured, Basil knows nothing, dawn finds Alexandra charging alone, and the campaign ends in the first minute. So prudence revises the plan: she will send the order, but *she will not commit to attacking until she knows Basil received it*. Very well: Basil, on receiving the order, sends a rider back — "message received; I shall attack at dawn." Now put yourself on the western hill, sir, in Basil's boots, watching your acknowledgment disappear into the dark valley, and think it through. If my rider is captured, Alexandra never learns that I received the order. And Alexandra — by her own announced prudence — will not attack without that knowledge. So if my rider is captured and I attack at dawn regardless, I attack alone. Therefore *I* dare not commit until I know my acknowledgment arrived. So Alexandra must acknowledge the acknowledgment. And the instant her second rider steps into the valley, the anxiety changes hills again — for if *he* is captured, Basil will not know that Alexandra knows, and by the same reasoning as before, Basil cannot rely on Alexandra attacking, and so Alexandra cannot rely on Basil... Each letter, you notice, does not discharge the doubt. It *relocates* it. The uncertainty is a hot coal passed back and forth across the valley, and no number of passes extinguishes it; whoever sent the most recent rider is holding it when dawn comes.

That is the intuition, and intuition is where most tellings stop — an infinite regress, gestured at, taken on faith. We do not stop there. You engaged this course, sir, on the explicit understanding that the proofs would be done properly, and this one fits entirely in the ear, so let us now actually prove it: *no protocol whatsoever* — not the naive one, not a clever one, nothing any general or any engineer will ever devise — can guarantee coordinated attack over a valley that captures messengers. The claim is universal, over all possible protocols; such claims cannot be established by trying protocols and watching them fail; we must argue about the *shape* of any possible solution. Here is the argument. It proceeds by contradiction, and I shall take it slowly.

Suppose, contrary to the claim, that a correct protocol exists. What do we mean by a protocol? A fixed procedure, agreed by both generals in advance, that tells each of them what letters to send, and when, and — at the end — whether to attack; and by *correct* we mean it guarantees two things in every possible execution, however the valley behaves. Agreement: both generals reach the same decision — both attack, or both stand down. And a modest usefulness condition, to bar cheap tricks: the protocol cannot simply answer "never attack" regardless — when Alexandra intends the attack and the valley happens to deliver every letter, the outcome is that both generals attack. If no protocol existed at all we could satisfy agreement by universal cowardice; the usefulness clause is what makes the problem a problem.

Now, among all correct protocols — and we are supposing there is at least one — select a *thriftiest* one: a protocol whose best-case execution, the one where every letter is delivered, uses as few letters as any correct protocol can. Some whole number of letters; call the count n, and note that the usefulness condition means this execution ends with both generals attacking. We shall derive a contradiction, and the instrument of the derivation is the *last letter* — the final rider dispatched in that best-case execution. Say, for the sake of the telling, that it is Basil who sends him, though the argument is perfectly symmetric.

Observation one, and it is the fulcrum of everything: *Basil's own decision cannot depend on what happens to that last rider.* Why? Because it is the last letter of the protocol — after dispatching it, Basil receives nothing further, from anyone, before dawn. Now hold two worlds side by side. In the first world, the rider crosses safely. In the second, he is captured in the valley. And ask: what does Basil, standing on the western hill, *observe* that differs between these two worlds? Nothing. Nothing whatsoever. He handed identical orders to an identical rider at an identical hour and then watched the same darkness. The two worlds are, to Basil, one world — and a general, however brilliant, is a procedure acting on what he has observed. Identical observations compel identical behaviour. Whatever Basil does at dawn — attack or stand down — he does it in *both* worlds. In the best-case world we know the protocol ends with both generals attacking; so Basil attacks there; so Basil attacks in the captured-rider world too. His decision is already sealed, and the valley's verdict on his last rider cannot unseal it.

Observation two: now consider Alexandra, in those same two worlds, and there are only two cases.

Case one: Alexandra's decision is *also* the same whether or not that last letter reaches her. Then, sir, consider what we have just said about the letter: neither its sender's decision nor its recipient's depends on its fate. It decides nothing. It is decoration — a ceremonial rider. Strike it from the protocol entirely, and every execution still ends exactly as it would have; we obtain a correct protocol whose best case uses n minus one letters. But we chose the thriftiest correct protocol in existence. Contradiction.

Case two: Alexandra's decision *differs* with the letter's fate — she attacks if it arrives, stands down if it does not, the letter is genuinely pivotal for her. Very well: let the valley capture it. In that world, Alexandra stands down. And Basil? Basil — by observation one, by the two worlds he cannot tell apart — does precisely what he did in the world where the rider got through: he attacks. At dawn, Basil's division charges into the valley alone, and Alexandra watches from the eastern hill. Agreement is violated. The protocol is not correct. Contradiction again.

Two cases, and each ends in absurdity. The supposition falls: no correct protocol exists. And if the minimal-counterexample dress of the argument leaves any residue of doubt, notice that the same knife peels any protocol letter by letter — the last letter is always either decoration, and may be struck, or pivotal, and may be captured — down to a protocol of no letters at all, in which Basil must somehow attack precisely when Alexandra intends it, across a valley carrying no information whatsoever, which is to say: down to the absurdity made plain. The desk volume writes this out as a clean induction tonight, and I commend it to you; it is theorem one of chapter one, and it is three paragraphs long.

Let us stand back, because something just happened that will happen at every summit of this course, and I want to name it while the fresh instance is in front of us. Notice what the proof did *not* do. It did not analyze strategies. It did not enumerate clever protocols and defeat them one by one — it could not have; there are infinitely many. It did one thing, twice: it found *two worlds that some participant cannot tell apart*, in which correctness demands that participant behave *differently* — and concluded that no such participant, and hence no such protocol, can exist. Basil cannot distinguish rider-delivered from rider-captured; correctness in case two demanded he attack in one and abstain in the other; therefore correctness is unachievable. That move — indistinguishability wielded as a weapon — is the master key of this entire field, and this course will honour it with a name: **the doppelgänger argument**. Build two executions that are doppelgängers to some process — identical in everything that process can observe — and that process is *compelled*, not persuaded but compelled, to behave identically in both. If the specification requires different behaviour in the two, the specification is dead, and no ingenuity will revive it. You have already met the doppelgänger once tonight, unnamed, in part two — crashed and slow are doppelgänger worlds to the observer across the network — and you will meet it at every major impossibility this season: it fells consensus, in the most celebrated three pages of the field; it fells the two-out-of-three dream the same hour; it sets the Byzantine bound; it convicts two-phase commit of blocking. By midseason, sir, I expect you to hear its footsteps before I announce it — to feel, when a proof begins constructing two suspiciously similar executions, the pleasant dread of knowing exactly which guest is about to arrive.

And allow me, just this once tonight, the episode's moment of open admiration, because the object in front of us has earned it. The argument you just heard uses no mathematics beyond patience. It fits, whole, in a listening ear on a single pass. It was available, in principle, to Aristotle. And it fences off — permanently, for all protocols, for all time — a guarantee that every bank, every airline, every shop with two computers appears to deliver before breakfast every day of the week. A three-paragraph argument, standing like a cliff face in front of the entire digital economy — and the economy, notice, proceeds. The remainder of this course, sir, all eleven episodes of it, lives inside that apparent contradiction, and the resolution — which I shall state properly in a moment — is the most practically useful idea I will hand you all season.

But first, precision about what was and was not just proven, because an impossibility theorem misremembered is worse than none. The theorem forbids *guaranteed* agreement — certainty, in every execution, against a Post that may capture anything. It does not forbid *likely* agreement. Send the order by twenty riders, and disagreement requires the valley to capture all twenty; if each crossing succeeds even half the time, the chance of catastrophe is below one in a million — a number an actuary, if not a logician, can love. The desk has you make this precise tonight: you will design the protocol, compute the risk, and then — the sting in the exercise — prove that no protocol makes the risk zero, so that the residue is not a flaw in your design but a permanent line item, to be priced and owned. Nor does the theorem forbid agreement in *gentler models* — restore acknowledgments-with-guaranteed-delivery, or bounded loss, and matters change, which is precisely why part four insisted that every claim declare its terrain. What it forbids, exactly, is certainty of common action, over unreliable mail, by a deadline. TCP does not evade it — TCP is a magnificent treaty for moving bytes, and at the moment of connection loss it hands you back this very question, unanswered: did my last letter arrive? And when, late in the season, we watch two machines try to commit one transaction — the officiant collecting vows from bride and groom, obliged to pronounce both married or neither — you will recognize the two hills instantly, and the theorem will be waiting there, wearing a notary's collar, exacting its price in a currency called blocking.

Impossible in principle. Routine in practice. Let us finally say, plainly, how both can be true.

## Part Seven — Impossible in Principle, Routine in Practice

Everything tonight reduces to three facts, and I shall state them now as the season's axioms — the three unpleasant truths from which every theorem, every protocol, and every incident in the next eleven episodes is compound interest.

Fact the first: machines fail — independently, partially, and without notifying anyone.

Fact the second: messages are delayed, lost, duplicated, and reordered — and the sender cannot tell which.

Fact the third: there is no shared clock — and, as next week will make precise, no shared *now* for a clock to report.

That is the entire estate, sir. Three facts. Every impossibility we prove will be a consequence of them; every system you operate is a negotiated settlement with them; and every production incident of your career has been one of them, wearing a costume.

Now the thesis. Across this season you will watch the theory prove, with complete rigour, that things are impossible — and then watch the industry do those very things, at scale, before lunch, daily. Tonight was the first instance: coordinated action over unreliable messages is impossible, and yet your last online purchase went through. Episode three will be the second and grandest. And I want to install, right now, the reflex for resolving this without either dismissing the mathematics or distrusting the machinery, because both are correct, and the reconciliation is always — always — found in one of three places.

First place: *the model differs.* The impossibility was proved for a harsher world than the one the system inhabits. The theorem assumed pure asynchrony; the system bought itself partial synchrony with timeouts and got lucky, or engineered its network until the assumption mostly holds. The theorem still stands; you are simply not standing where it points.

Second place: *the guarantee differs.* The theorem forbids certainty; the system delivers overwhelming probability, and the marketing department rounded up. Twenty riders. One-in-a-million disagreement. The word "guaranteed" on the label, doing quiet work.

Third place: *the price was paid somewhere you were not looking.* The impossible thing was made possible by spending something — availability during partitions, latency on every write, a wait of seven milliseconds, a lease, a human on call. The theorem named a cost; the system paid it; the invoice was filed where visitors do not go.

Model, guarantee, price. When a claim in this field seems to contradict a theorem, one of those three is the answer, and finding which is a skill — I would say it is *the* senior-engineer skill, the difference between reading a vendor page and auditing one. The season will sharpen this reflex into a formal triage — impossible, versus expensive, versus merely inadvisable — but the instinct begins tonight.

And with the thesis stated, the season's map, briefly, so you know the shape of the campaign. Next week: time itself — what "before" means without a shared clock, courtesy of Leslie Lamport and, of all people, a patent clerk. Episode three: the week mathematics forbids consensus — Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson's celebrated proof, done in full, all fifteen minutes of it. Episode four: the parliament of our title — a vanished Greek island called Paxos, a paper nobody would publish, and its humane successor, Raft. Episode five: consensus in captivity — ZooKeeper, Chubby, leases, fencing tokens, and the arrival of liars. Episode six: the Dynamo school of replication, in which Amazon's shopping cart founds a church. Episode seven: a field guide to the most overloaded word in computing — consistent. Episode eight: transactions at a distance, wherein Google apologizes to physics with atomic clocks. Episode nine: partitioning, or where things live. Episode ten: the log, and the dataflow it feeds. Episode eleven: the engineering of failure — retries, storms, and systems that stay broken after the cause is cured. And episode twelve, the capstone, on your own professional doorstep: the training run — ten thousand accelerators in lockstep — revealed as the largest distributed system you have ever operated, and the season's every theorem found humming inside it. Two generals on two hills at the start; ten thousand machines agreeing on a gradient at the end; and the finale's business is to show you they are the same story.

## Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

Two standing rituals close every episode of this course, and I inaugurate both now.

The first is the Whiteboard: two or three minutes of what tonight obliges a designer to carry into a design review — not the theory, but the theory's posture. Four items tonight.

Item one. Fix the model before the architecture. Before a single box is drawn, say aloud what you assume: what the network may do to a message, which failures you insure against, what timing you are renting. Every downstream decision inherits from this, and an unstated model is a disagreement waiting for an incident to reveal it.

Item two. The three-question catechism, applied to every arrow in every diagram — and I do mean every arrow, sir; the discipline is the point. What happens when this message is *lost*? What happens when it is *duplicated*? What happens when it is *delayed* beyond all patience — and arrives anyway, later, after you have acted on its absence? If the answer to any of the three is "that cannot happen," what you have is not a design but a fallacy with a diagram.

Item three. Silence is not evidence. A timeout is a verdict rendered without a body; render it — you must — but never confuse it with knowledge, and always ask what happens when the condemned walks back in.

Item four. Never say "reliable" without saying against what. Loss? Duplication? Reordering? Crash? Deceit? The word without the clause is marketing; the word with the clause is engineering.

The second ritual is the reading, for the field's true textbook is its papers, and I shall assign a little of it each week, one line apiece. Tonight, two short pieces and a standing companion. First: the fallacies of distributed computing — five minutes with the list, and I ask you to read it as an audit, counting how many of the eight your last outage embodied; my own experience of engineers performing this exercise is that the count is rarely below three. Second: Bailis and Kingsbury, "The Network is Reliable," in ACM Queue, two thousand fourteen — the documentary record behind tonight's charter; read it the way an actuary reads flood data. And standing behind the whole season, for parallel reading at your leisure: Kleppmann's *Designing Data-Intensive Applications*, chapters five through nine — the spiritual companion to this course, and the best prose the field has produced.

The desk now awaits you, sir, with chapter one: the models made formal; the Post's charter as fair-loss links, and the layered construction upward — you will build a perfect link out of fair-loss parts and prove all three of its guarantees, which is the primal manufacturing pattern of the field; the Two Generals theorem written out as the clean induction I promised; the trace-diagram conventions we shall use all season; and problems in three tiers, including the season's first *sabotage* — a deduplication scheme with one small, fatal economy in it, whose fatal execution you are to exhibit. I certify, per the standing rules of this course, that the bug is genuinely fatal; I have written out the disaster myself, and it is waiting in the solutions to see whether you find the same one.

And the ledger — opened tonight, kept faithfully hereafter, though I shall trouble your ear with its details as little as decency allows; the desk keeps the itemized books. Three debts stand issued: the Post, tonight negligent but honest, will learn to forge — with a famous theorem attached; crashed-versus-slow, tonight diagnosed as permanent, will be managed, never cured, in instalments across the season; and the missing clock — payable immediately: next week.

For next week, then. In nineteen hundred and five, a patent clerk in Bern demonstrated that simultaneity — the innocent notion that two events either happen at the same moment or do not — is not a fact about the world but a fact about the observer. In nineteen seventy-eight, Leslie Lamport delivered precisely the same news to computing, in what remains among the most cited papers our field has produced, and he has been candid that the physics is exactly where he got the idea. We shall follow him: we will dismantle "which happened first," rebuild what can be rebuilt from the only causal instrument a distributed system possesses — the message — and then, for a finale, perform a small miracle of Chandy and Lamport's devising: taking a coherent photograph of a system that refuses, even for an instant, to hold still. There is no *now*, sir — and yet there is a picture. I shall see you there.

Until then, sir.

*— End of Episode One.*
