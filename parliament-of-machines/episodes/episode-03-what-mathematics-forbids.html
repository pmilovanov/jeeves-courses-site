The Parliament of Machines

An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Three: What Mathematics Forbids

Three Pages in April

Good day, sir.

In April of nineteen eighty-five, the Journal of the ACM published a paper by Michael Fischer, Nancy Lynch, and Michael Paterson, under a title of a bluntness one rarely encounters in the academic literature: "Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process." No hedging subtitle, no "towards," no "on certain aspects of." A death certificate, filed in quadruplicate, with the cause of death in the title.

The paper is short. The whole of it runs to about nine printed pages; the argument that matters — the one we shall spend the heart of tonight performing — occupies scarcely three. I have seen dinner menus with more pages and considerably less consequence. The result had circulated for two years before the journal got it — the authors first presented it in nineteen eighty-three, at a database conference, of all venues, which tells you who was bleeding — and by the time it appeared in April it had already done its work: a decade of protocol tinkering was over.

Let me describe the decade, because the paper's force is unintelligible without it. Through the late seventies and early eighties, some of the best people in computing had been designing agreement protocols — schemes by which a collection of machines, some of which may fail, settle on a common decision: commit this transaction or abort it; accept this update or refuse it; crown this coordinator or that one. Protocols were proposed, published, deployed. And every one of them, under sufficiently malicious inspection, revealed a corner — some arrangement of delays and silences in which the protocol either hung forever or, worse, agreed on two different things at once. Each corner, when found, looked like a flaw of craftsmanship. The natural response was the engineer's response: patch it, add a round, add a timeout, be cleverer. The corners moved. They did not close.

Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson explained why. The corner is not in the protocol, sir. The corner is in the problem. Every protocol that can ever be written — patched, extended, garlanded with rounds and timeouts, designed by us or by our betters or by machines yet unbuilt — has such a corner, and the paper proves it in three pages, with no mathematics beyond careful bookkeeping and one idea of genuine genius. When the field's great prize for influential work was established — you will recall from last week that its inaugural selection, in the year two thousand, was Lamport's paper on time — the honour went, in its second year, to this one. The trade calls the result FLP, an economy I shall permit myself for the rest of the evening now that the names have been said aloud: Fischer, Lynch, Paterson. The field's habit of filing its monuments under acronyms is a small barbarism; one compensates by pronouncing the names at least once, properly, the way one reads the inscription on a statue before using it as a landmark.

Now, why this problem, and why does tonight sit at the exact centre of the season's first half? Because every road we have walked so far funnels into it. Episode one ended in the wreckage of the umbrella counter with the deepest question in the rubble: Bingo says one, Gussie says nought — which of them is right? — and we found the question had no answer until someone is appointed to give one, and that appointing an authority out of parts that fail is a problem with a name. Consensus. Episode two built order out of messages, and then confessed that the better grade of order — the total order, the single timeline all replicas apply in lockstep — must be rented from a leader, and left as a debt the question of how a leaderless rabble elects one and survives his death. An election is an agreement about who won. Consensus again. Transactions, replication, configuration, membership — lift the floorboards of any of them and you find the same joist. One bit, agreed upon by machines that fail. That is the load-bearing member of the entire edifice.

Tonight the mathematics tells us the joist cannot be manufactured — not in the world we have been honestly assuming since episode one. And here is the evening's peculiar cruelty, which I want you to hold from the start: the proof does not use the Post's malice. No letter is lost tonight. No letter is forged, none duplicated, none even delivered out of order in any way that matters. The Post delivers every single letter, and the theorem kills us anyway — with delay alone, wielded with perfect judgment. Two weeks ago, loss defeated two generals. Tonight, mere unpunctuality defeats any number of them.

The itinerary. First, the problem stated cold — consensus, in three modest clauses, and why the modesty is deceptive. Second, the adversary receives its formal commission: the scheduler, the entity we have been calling the Post's malice, now given its full legal powers and its one leash. Third — the set piece, the season's flagship, budgeted at a quarter of an hour without apology, as ruled in the founding session — the proof itself, entire, in the ear. Fourth, the fine print: what was actually proven, what was not, and the two words in the theorem that carry the whole weight. Fifth, the loopholes — all three gates in the fence, through which every working system you have ever operated makes its escape. Sixth, a second theorem — smaller, more famous, more abused — concerning one bad afternoon: the CAP theorem, proven properly in two minutes and then rescued from a decade of vendor keynotes. And seventh, the installation of a triage that will serve us for the rest of the season: impossible, versus expensive, versus merely inadvisable. The doppelgänger, as promised, has the leading role tonight. It appears in the first act, it appears in the last, and in between it performs the murder.

Part One — A Modest Ask

State the problem with no theatre at all, because its plainness is the point.

Some number of gentlemen — say our three: Bingo, Tuppy, and Gussie — must jointly make a single yes-or-no decision. To fix ideas, and to keep the shop in business: the question before the house is whether to adopt tomorrow's new price list. Each gentleman arrives with a private opinion — a ballot, nought for keep the old prices, one for adopt the new — formed from whatever local evidence he has. They may correspond through the Post as much as they please. And at some point, each gentleman must write his decision in his ledger — in ink. Once written, never revised. That is the whole setting.

Three clauses define success, and I shall give them the names the field uses.

Clause one, agreement: no two gentlemen ink different decisions. Not "rarely differ." Never — in any execution, under any weather. A shop whose branches disagree about the price list is episode one's umbrella counter with stationery.

Clause two, validity: the decision must be some gentleman's actual original opinion. In particular, if every gentleman arrives with the same ballot — all noughts, or all ones — then that unanimous opinion is the decision. You will recognize the clause's purpose from the two generals: it bars universal cowardice. A protocol that ignores all ballots and always inks nought satisfies agreement magnificently and decides nothing worth deciding. Validity is the clause that makes the committee a committee rather than a rubber stamp with a prearranged verdict.

And mark, sir, what validity conspicuously does not ask. It does not ask that the value be good. It does not ask that any gentleman like it — that Bingo find the new prices agreeable, or Gussie find them profitable. Out in the world, reaching agreement usually means precisely that sort of haggling: parties with private interests, each ratifying a proposal only where it falls favourably across his own ledger. That is negotiation, and it is a wholly different and a considerably harder science, one we are — deliberately — not practising tonight. Of the value we ask only that somebody proposed it; of its merit, nothing whatever. And the stripping is the entire point. Take away every reason to prefer one answer to another, and what remains to be arranged is coordination in the pure — the bare securing of a single common answer against crashes and silence — and it is coordination in the pure that the evening will prove cannot be guaranteed. Load the values back up with preferences and the difficulty only deepens: a committee that cannot be promised agreement on a bare bit most certainly cannot be promised it on the agreeable ones. We strip the bit naked, sir, so that when it defeats us we may know beyond argument that it was the coordinating that could not be done, and nothing else.

Clause three, termination: every gentleman who remains alive eventually writes. Not "may write," not "writes if conditions permit." The meeting ends. This is the clause to watch tonight, sir. Keep your eye on it the way one keeps an eye on the quietest man in the room.

And one more requirement, which is not a clause about the outcome but a condition of the whole exercise: the protocol must deliver these three guarantees even if one gentleman fails. Crash-stop, in the vocabulary of episode one: he halts, silently, at any moment of the adversary's choosing, and never returns. One chair, possibly empty, and nobody is told whose or when or whether.

Pause on why that last requirement changes everything, because without it the problem is a parlour game. If no chair can ever empty, the protocol writes itself: every gentleman posts his ballot to every other; every gentleman waits until all ballots are in hand; everyone applies the same fixed rule — say, adopt the new prices only if the ballots are unanimous for it — and inks the result. The Post may dawdle as it likes; every letter arrives eventually; every gentleman eventually holds the same three ballots, applies the same rule, and inks the same verdict. Agreement, validity, termination — all three, trivially. The entire difficulty of the field's central problem, sir, resides in one possibly-empty chair. Wait for all three ballots, and if a chair has emptied you wait forever — termination dies. Wait for only two, and act on a partial count — and now two different gentlemen may act on two different partial counts, and agreement is in the gravest peril. The committee is caught between waiting for a colleague who may never speak and proceeding without a colleague who may merely be clearing his throat. You have heard this dilemma before. It is crashed-versus-slow, the primordial confusion of episode one, now promoted from a nuisance of operations to the exact hinge on which the possibility of agreement turns.

One more word on the modesty. A single bit, sir — that is all we are asking. Not a database, not a transaction, not a replicated log; one solitary bit, decided once, by three machines, of which at most one may fall silent. It is difficult to imagine asking less. That is precisely why the impossibility is a monument and not a curiosity: a proof that a grand thing cannot be done invites the response that one should ask for something humbler; a proof that the humblest nontrivial thing cannot be done closes the conversation. And the humblest thing is also, by a pleasing symmetry, the mightiest: settle one bit reliably and you can settle a sequence of them; a sequence of settled bits is a log; a log driven through identical machines is — as Lamport remarked in passing in nineteen seventy-eight, in the acorn paragraph you smiled at last week — a replicated anything. The bit is the atom of coordination. Next week we shall see it earn that title in full; tonight we are told the atom cannot be split reliably at all. In the asynchronous world, that is. The qualifier is the whole story, and we come to it now.

Part Two — Opposing Counsel

Every impossibility proof in this course is a game, and it is time to introduce the opposing player properly. We have been calling it the Post's malice; episode one promised it a formal commission tonight. Here it is.

The adversary in this field is called the scheduler, and I want you to picture it not as weather but as counsel — opposing counsel of formidable diligence, who has read our protocol. Read it entire, sir: every rule, every threshold, every contingency, before the game begins. There are no secrets from the scheduler, because a protocol is a published rulebook, and we must win against every possible arrangement of delays, which is the same as losing to the cleverest one. Assuming the adversary reads our playbook is not pessimism; it is the definition of a guarantee.

Now, the powers of this counsel — and, as important, the leash.

The scheduler controls timing, and timing alone. Specifically: when several letters sit undelivered in the Post's bag, the scheduler chooses which lands next. When several gentlemen are ready to take a step — receive a letter, do their bookwork, dispatch replies — the scheduler chooses who moves and who stands idle, and for how long. It may hold any letter as long as it pleases, short of forever. It may starve any gentleman of turns as long as it pleases, short of forever. In the asynchronous model — our standing terrain since episode one — there is no bound the scheduler must respect, no clock it must answer to, no pattern it must follow. Delay is its entire arsenal, and its aim with that one weapon is perfect.

The leash is fairness, and it has two straps. Strap one: every letter posted to a living gentleman is eventually delivered. The scheduler may sit on a letter for a thousand of its turns, but not for all of them. Strap two: every living gentleman is eventually given his next turn — again and again, without end. The scheduler may bench Gussie for as long as it likes, but it must keep letting him back into the game. In addition, tonight it holds one dark privilege, already announced: it may, at most once, retire a gentleman permanently — the crash — and it never files notice. But note, and this deserves repeating: it may not lose a letter. Every letter sent to a survivor arrives. Tonight's Post has been reformed, sir — it is punctuality itself, minus the punctuality.

And now observe what this adversary's powers amount to, because they amount to something you already know intimately. The scheduler can take any healthy gentleman and, for any finite stretch of its choosing, make him indistinguishable from a corpse: withhold his letters, deny his turns, let his colleagues' timeouts fire and their suspicions harden — and then, at the exact moment maximally inconvenient to us, produce him alive, bookwork intact, entirely innocent. Crashed-versus-slow, the confusion I asked you to hold from episode one, is not merely a difficulty the scheduler exploits. It is the scheduler's stock-in-trade, its entire craft — and tonight, in the proof, you will watch that confusion promoted from an operational nuisance to the murder weapon in a mathematical argument. The debt register said an instalment on that debt falls due tonight: the formal apparatus, and it is paid in full within the hour.

The game, then, stated with the precision the desk will formalize. A position of the game — the desk says configuration — comprises the private state of every gentleman together with the bag: the set of letters posted but not yet delivered. A move: the scheduler selects a letter from the bag — or selects a gentleman for an empty turn — and the selected gentleman, applying his rulebook to what he received, updates his books and posts whatever letters the rulebook dictates. Determinism, sir: given the same delivery in the same state, a gentleman does the same thing, always. Our side's play is fixed the moment the rulebook is published; every variation in how the game unfolds is the scheduler's choice and only the scheduler's. A full unfolding — moves without end, or until everyone has inked — the desk calls an execution, and an execution respecting the leash is called fair, or admissible. Our victory condition: in every fair execution, with up to one crash, all three clauses hold. The scheduler's victory condition: one fair execution — a single one — in which some clause fails. We must win every play. Counsel needs one.

Two remarks before the curtain rises on the main performance.

First: notice how much civilization is already in this framing. We are not asking "does the protocol usually work." We are asking whether opposing counsel, with the rulebook in hand and unbounded patience, can find one legal line of play that breaks it. This is the correct standard for machinery that holds money and medical records, and it is the standard the whole season applies. When the industry says a system "works," it generally means no one has yet been paid to find the line of play. When mathematics says a protocol works, it means counsel has been defeated in advance, for all lines, forever. The distance between those two sentences is the distance between testing and proof.

Second: the fairness leash matters more than it looks, and I want its role stated before the proof rather than after. An adversary allowed to suppress letters forever is just episode one's Post, and against that Post we already know coordination dies — two generals settled it, cheaply. Tonight's theorem is played against a leashed adversary, and that is what makes it deep. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson conceded to the network everything the engineer could dream of demanding — every letter delivered, every process scheduled, forever — and showed the concession does not help. The impossibility survives the reform of the Post. When a theorem still stands after you have granted the other side everything it asked for, sir, the theorem is telling you the trouble was never where you were looking.

Part Three — The Proof on the Fence

Now the set piece. A quarter of an hour was budgeted for this proof in the founding session, at your own ruling, and I intend to spend it like a man spending someone else's ruling. Everything aloud, one honestly-labelled compression, and the complete text waiting at the desk. We proceed in four movements: a vocabulary, an opening, a small geometric lemma, and the heart.

The vocabulary first, because the proof's single greatest idea is a way of seeing, and the seeing needs a word. Take any position of the game — everyone's books, plus the bag. Freeze it. Now ask, of this frozen position, a question about its futures: among all the ways the scheduler could legally continue from here, does there exist a continuation in which the parliament ends by inking nought? Does there exist one ending in one? Three answers are possible. If only nought-endings exist ahead, the position is committed to nought — the desk says nought-valent. If only one-endings, one-valent. And if both futures remain alive — if the scheduler, standing at this position, still holds in its hands the power to steer the game to either verdict — the position is called bivalent. On the fence.

Two features of this vocabulary, and I shall lean on both with full weight.

Feature one: valence is a fact about the position, not about anybody's knowledge of it. No gentleman can in general see whether the position is committed; often no gentleman could compute it. A chess position can be lost beyond rescue while both players still believe the game is open — the loss is in the position, laid up in its futures, visible only to the tablebase. Valence is the tablebase view. And tonight, for the purposes of the proof, we grant the scheduler the tablebase: opposing counsel, who has the rulebook, is permitted unlimited analysis. That is fair play in an impossibility argument — remember, we must beat even the strongest legal adversary.

Feature two, and this is the bolt that fastens the whole proof: in a correct protocol, a position where any gentleman has already inked is committed. Necessarily. For suppose Tuppy has inked nought, and yet some legal continuation still ends at one. Follow that continuation: it ends with gentlemen inking one — and Tuppy's nought stands already dry on the page. Two different inks; agreement dead; the protocol was not correct after all. Contrapositive, in a correct protocol: ink implies commitment. And therefore — say it the other way round, because the other way round is the strategy of the entire proof — a position on the fence is a position where no one has yet inked anything. Bivalence means the books are clean. Keep the parliament on the fence, sir, and you have kept every pen off every page. The scheduler does not need to make anyone decide wrongly. It only needs to preserve, forever, the possibility of either verdict — and no one can ever have decided at all. Indecision is not a failure to reach the goal; indecision is the goal, the adversary's whole campaign. Hold the shape of that: the proof will now show the campaign can always be prosecuted.

The campaign has two obligations. Begin on the fence. Never leave it. We take them in order.

The opening. Does the game even begin on the fence? Not obviously. There are finitely many starting positions — one per assignment of ballots to gentlemen — and one might hope the protocol commits from the first whistle: perhaps every starting position is already univalent, its verdict a foregone function of the ballots, the correspondence a mere formality of counting. The first lemma kills the hope, and the doppelgänger performs its first murder of the night doing so.

Suppose, for contradiction, that every starting position is committed. Consider the position where all three ballots read nought. By validity, any deciding continuation from there inks nought — so that position is committed to nought. Symmetrically, all-ones is committed to one. Now, sir, walk from one to the other, a step at a time, flipping a single ballot per step: all noughts; then Bingo's ballot flipped to one, the others nought; then Bingo's and Tuppy's; then all three. Four positions in a row, each neighbour differing from the last in exactly one gentleman's ballot. The row begins nought-committed and ends one-committed; since every position in the row is committed by assumption, somewhere along the row two neighbours disagree — some adjacent pair where the left is committed to nought, the right to one, and the only difference between them is one ballot. Say it is Gussie's: same position, save that Gussie's private ballot reads nought on the left and one on the right.

Now the murder. The scheduler exercises its dark privilege at the opening whistle: Gussie is retired — crashed — before taking a single turn, before posting a single letter. In both worlds. And ask: to Bingo and Tuppy, what distinguishes the left world from the right? Gussie's ballot was private; it never spoke; its owner never moved. Nothing distinguishes them. Nothing ever will. The two worlds are doppelgängers to every survivor — and you know, from episode one, what that compels. The protocol tolerates a crash, so the survivors must play on to a verdict — termination does not excuse them merely because a chair is empty. They play; being deterministic, and seeing identical worlds, they play identically in both; they ink identically in both. But the left world was committed to nought and the right to one — so whichever ink the survivors choose, it contradicts one side's commitment. The supposition falls. Some starting position is on the fence.

Savour the economy for one second, sir: the adversary has not yet used its scheduling powers at all. A row of paper dolls, one silenced juror, and the game is guaranteed to open with both verdicts alive. And notice — this will rhyme all night — it was the tolerance of a crash, the constitutional requirement that the survivors carry on, that the argument turned against us. A parliament forbidden to wait for Gussie is a parliament that can be shown two worlds through the hole where Gussie used to be.

The small lemma. Second movement, one minute, and you have already proven it yourself. Two letters sit in the bag, addressed to different desks — one for Bingo, one for Tuppy. Claim: the scheduler may deliver them in either order, and the resulting position is the same. Why: Bingo's reaction — his bookwork, his outgoing letters — is a deterministic function of his own books and his own letter; Tuppy's likewise; neither observes the other's step in between; so each does the same thing in either interleaving, and the bag collects the same letters. Deliveries to different desks commute. At the desk this is a small diamond-shaped diagram — two paths around a square meeting at the same corner — and it is nothing but last week's swap lemma, the transposition of merely-elsewhere events, which you proved with your own hands in problem seven of chapter two. Last week that lemma was a philosophical instrument: it certified photographs of presents that never quite happened. Tonight the same lemma is a weapon. I did tell you, sir, that the season would ask "ancestor or cousin" until it moved money.

The heart. Third movement — and here is where I owe you the honesty clause. The argument I am about to perform has, at its centre, a case analysis with a sub-case I shall compress into a single sentence and settle by appeal to the desk, where it is written out to the last inequality as the chapter's flagship. Everything else you will hear entire.

Here is the claim that wins the war — the desk calls it the pivot lemma. The parliament stands at a position on the fence. In the bag sits some letter — any letter; say, one addressed to Tuppy. Call it the pivot. Then the scheduler can continue the game in such a way that the pivot is eventually delivered — fairness honoured, nothing withheld forever — and the position immediately after the pivot lands is still on the fence.

Feel first what the claim does. The scheduler's obligation was: never leave the fence. Its constraint was: the leash — every letter must land eventually, every gentleman must keep moving. The pivot lemma says these are compatible: whatever letter the leash is currently owed, the scheduler can pay that debt — actually deliver the thing — and remain on the fence. Fairness, it will turn out, is no protection at all. The leash constrains which letters land; the fence is defended by choosing when.

Now the proof of the claim, by contradiction. Suppose the scheduler cannot: from our fenced position, every way of playing that ends with the pivot landing produces, at the instant of landing, a committed position. Two preliminary observations, the first of which is my one compression: one can show — this is the sentence, sir, and the desk redeems it — that among these landing positions both commitments occur: some ways of steering before the landing leave the pivot committing to nought, others to one. Roughly, because the original position has both futures, and the pivot's landing positions inherit that variety; the desk does the inheritance honestly. Granting it: since we can steer to a nought-committing landing and also to a one-committing landing, and since steering is done one delivery at a time, somewhere there must be two positions one single delivery apart — call the letter between them the switch, addressed to whomever — where landing the pivot on the earlier position commits nought, and landing it on the later position, just one switch-delivery downstream, commits one. One letter, sir — the switch — separates a nought-verdict from a one-verdict. Everything now turns on one question: at whose desk does the switch land?

Case one: the switch is addressed to someone other than Tuppy — Gussie, say. Then the pivot (to Tuppy) and the switch (to Gussie) land at different desks, and by the small lemma they commute: pivot then switch reaches exactly the same position as switch then pivot. Trace the two routes around the diamond. Route one: pivot lands first — position committed to nought, by hypothesis — then the switch lands. Route two: switch first, then pivot — committed to one, by hypothesis. Same final position, sir. And that position lies downstream of a nought-committed position, so every future it has ends at nought — yet route two says it is committed to one. A committed position cannot re-open, and it certainly cannot be committed both ways. Contradiction. Case closed — and notice it was pure geometry: determinism and the diamond, nothing else.

Case two: the switch is addressed to Tuppy himself — the same desk as the pivot. The diamond is unavailable; deliveries to the same desk do not commute, for Tuppy reads the first letter and his state changes before the second. Here, sir, is where the argument reaches for its deepest resource — the same one the opening move used. The protocol claims to tolerate a crash. Tuppy's crash, in particular. So: from the earlier of our two positions, freeze Tuppy — the scheduler simply declines, lawfully for any finite stretch, to give him a turn or a letter — and let Bingo and Gussie play on alone. Must they reach a verdict? They must. If Tuppy had genuinely crashed, termination obliges the survivors to finish; and — the doppelgänger, third appearance tonight — Bingo and Gussie cannot tell a crashed Tuppy from a benched one. The letters in the bag addressed to Tuppy sit undelivered either way; his silence is identical silence in both worlds. Whatever the survivors would do with Tuppy dead, they must do with Tuppy benched, for the two worlds are one world to them. So they play to a verdict; ink dries; call the resulting position the verdict position.

And now the trap closes — watch the diamond return with a vengeance. The survivors' entire play touched neither the pivot nor the switch: both are letters addressed to Tuppy, and Tuppy was benched throughout. So their play commutes with each of them — the small lemma again, iterated. From the verdict position, let the scheduler now deliver the pivot: by commutation, the result is the same as if the pivot had landed first, back at the start, followed by the survivors' play — and the pivot landing there committed the game to nought; so the verdict position has a future ending in nought. Alternatively, from the verdict position, deliver the switch and then the pivot: commuting both across the survivors' play, this is the one-committing landing followed by their play — a future ending in one. Both futures, alive, at the verdict position. The verdict position is on the fence. But ink is dry at the verdict position, sir — and we established, as the bolt fastening this whole proof, that ink implies commitment. A position cannot be both inked and on the fence. Contradiction — final, and fatal. The supposition falls; the pivot lemma stands. From the fence, any owed letter can be paid and the fence held.

The endgame. Fourth movement, and it is short, because the war is won and this is the occupation. The scheduler's full campaign, assembled: open at a fenced position — the first lemma guarantees one exists. Now run the parliament on a rota: Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, round and round, forever. On each gentleman's turn, take the oldest letter owed him in the bag — oldest first, so nothing lingers — declare it the pivot, and play the pivot lemma: steer, deliver it, land on the fence. Then the next gentleman, his oldest letter, the fence again. Audit the leash, sir, strap by strap. Every gentleman receives turns forever — the rota sees to it. Every letter is eventually delivered — it waits at most one circuit of the rota before becoming somebody's oldest, and then the lemma lands it. The run is fair. It is fairness itself. Nobody has crashed — the dark privilege, note it well, was never exercised; every gentleman is alive, industrious, corresponding at full diligence from the first move to the — but there is no last move. The game continues forever, every position on the fence, and a fence position has clean books. No gentleman ever inks. Termination fails in a fair, crash-free, loss-free execution — and the protocol, which promised termination in every fair execution, is refuted. No protocol survives. That is the theorem, sir. That is FLP entire.

Let us stand in the ruins for a moment, as we did on the hill with the generals, because this proof has a property I want you to carry out of the room. Ask: what did the adversary actually do? It lost nothing — every letter arrived. It forged nothing, duplicated nothing. It crashed no one — the dark privilege stayed sheathed, start to finish. Its entire output, over an infinite game, was a sequence of perfectly legal decisions about when — this letter now, that gentleman next. And yet it was the crash that did the killing. Not a crash that happened: the crash that might. Twice in the proof — at the opening, and in the heart's second case — the argument reached for the same lever: the protocol is required to survive a silent chair, therefore the survivors must be prepared to finish without any given colleague, therefore the scheduler can bench that colleague and borrow the survivors' preparedness — march them down the very corridor built for the emergency, then produce the missing man alive and use the corridor's existence to spring the trap. The tolerance is the vulnerability. The fire escape is how the burglar gets in. Every fault-tolerant protocol must be willing to proceed on silence, and everything that proceeds on silence can be led by silence — hesitation, sir, wielded as a weapon; the threat sufficing where the deed is never done. I permit myself the episode's one open admiration here, as budgeted, because I know of nothing else in computing quite like this argument: three pages that took the field's fondest ambition, granted it every concession it had ever asked of the network — every letter delivered, every process scheduled — and defeated it with the one thing no message-passing system can buy at any price: an answer to the question is he dead, or merely slow? The confusion we filed in episode one as the primordial nuisance turns out to be a conservation law. The corner the decade of tinkerers kept moving was this corner. It cannot close, because it is load-bearing.

Part Four — The Fine Print

An impossibility theorem misremembered is worse than none — episode one's rule — and this theorem is misremembered for a living. So, the fine print, read aloud, clause by clause.

What exactly was proven? In the asynchronous model — no bounds on delay, none on relative speed — no deterministic protocol can guarantee agreement, validity, and termination for even a single bit, in every fair execution, while tolerating even one crash-stop failure. Each italicized word is load-bearing, and each is a hinge on which the escape gates of part five will turn.

First: termination is the casualty — the only casualty. Nothing in the proof touches agreement or validity; indeed the constructed run never violates them — it never lets anyone decide at all. This asymmetry is the single most practical sentence tonight, so let me give it the whiteboard phrasing now: safety is free; liveness is the hostage. There exist protocols — you operate several — that never, in any execution, under any weather, ink two different verdicts: safety unconditional, held through arbitrary asynchrony, through crashes, through the scheduler's worst. What no protocol can do is also promise the meeting always ends. The parliament that cannot be made to lie can still be made to filibuster. When we meet Paxos next week you will see this division of the estate made explicit in the protocol's very structure: its safety argument will invoke no clocks, no timeouts, no luck — and its liveness section will begin, in effect, "assuming the weather improves." That sentence is not an engineering apology, sir. It is FLP, cited as fine print.

Second: deterministic. The scheduler's campaign was a tablebase strategy — it steered toward the fence because, holding the rulebook, it could compute where the fence was. Every reaction predictable, every fence position calculable. Put a coin in any gentleman's pocket — let his rulebook say, in some corner, "flip" — and the tablebase clouds over: positions no longer have a determinate valence, the fence is no longer a computable line, and the whole campaign wants re-litigating. That is not yet a solution — it is a doorway, and Ben-Or is standing in it; part five.

Third: one faulty process. The title's modesty, and its menace. The theorem does not require an elaborate failure model — no lying, no forging, no Byzantine aunts; one possible crash-stop, the gentlest failure in the taxonomy, suffices to poison the well. And mind the grammar of "possible": in the killing run itself, nobody crashes. It is the protocol's obligation to survive a crash — the fire escape it is required to build — that the adversary turns. A protocol for machines guaranteed immortal escapes the theorem trivially; a protocol for the actual world, where the guarantee cannot be had, walks into it.

Fourth: the reformed Post. Say it once more, because it is the detail the folklore always drops: no loss. Two Generals was an impossibility of loss — kill a letter and knowledge cannot become common. FLP is an impossibility of delay — deliver every letter, and mere freedom about when still forbids guaranteed agreement. They are different theorems about different liberties of the Post, and the pairing brackets the field's misery from both ends: the network that loses defeats certainty of knowledge; the network that dawdles defeats certainty of termination. An engineer who remembers only "networks are unreliable" has learned half the lesson. The reliable network with no latency bound is also a theorem-grade adversary.

Now the other side of the ledger — what was not proven, because the folklore here is a standing hazard to good engineering. The theorem does not say consensus is impossible in practice. It does not say your etcd cluster is broken, or that its designers evaded mathematics by marketing. The killing schedule is a single infinite thread through the tree of executions, woven with tablebase precision; disturb it at any of infinitely many points — one letter landing a moment early, one timeout misfiring in our favour — and the parliament falls off the fence and decides. Real networks, whose delays are noisy rather than malicious, wander off the razor's edge almost immediately, which is why your clusters decide millions of times a day and why the theorem's shadow in production is not deadlock but the rarer, stranger thing: the election storm — leadership contested, contested again, term after term climbing while no term completes — livelock, the fence walked in the wild, always briefly, under weather that merely resembles the adversary for a while. FLP does not predict routine failure. It forbids a certain sentence from ever being true: "this system is guaranteed to decide, whatever the timing." Any vendor page containing that sentence about an asynchronous deterministic system is selling you a theorem violation, and you are entitled — episode one's triage — to ask which word they are quietly redefining: the model, the guarantee, or the price.

Part Five — The Loopholes

A good impossibility theorem, sir, is not a wall but a survey: it tells you precisely where the fence runs, and therefore precisely where the gates must be. The italicized words of part four were the survey markers — asynchronous, deterministic, guarantee. Three words, three gates, and the entire practising industry files through them daily. Let us tour all three, because episode after episode from here forward will be spent on the far side of one or another, and I want the gate stamped in your passport each time we cross.

Gate one: renegotiate asynchronous — partial synchrony. The fence was surveyed in the model where delay is boundless and the scheduler's aim eternal. But recall episode one's middle terrain, the one I asked you to mark well: partial synchrony, articulated by Cynthia Dwork, Nancy Lynch — the same Lynch, sir, fencing from both sides, having proven the impossibility she now helps to escape — and Larry Stockmeyer, in a paper published in nineteen eighty-eight in the Journal of the ACM under the title "Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony." Its two readings you know: bounds exist but are unknown; or bounds hold only after some unknown stabilization time. Its consensus finding is the industry's charter: in partial synchrony, consensus is solvable — with safety holding unconditionally, through the wild weather, and termination guaranteed once the weather settles. Note the shape, because it is the only shape the mathematics permits: not "the protocol terminates," but "the protocol never errs, and terminates when the network behaves for long enough." The guarantee is conditional exactly where FLP says it must be, and unconditional exactly where safety is free. Every timeout in every consensus deployment you operate is this gate in miniature — a wager that the bound, whatever it is, has probably been reached; every adaptive timeout, doubling patiently, is a protocol groping for the unknown bound from below. The industry does not defeat FLP, sir. It rents a better model by the millisecond and keeps the receipts. Episode four lives entirely inside this gate, and I shall say no more tonight.

Gate two: renegotiate deterministic — randomization. Nineteen eighty-three, a year before the partial-synchrony paper's conference debut and the same year FLP itself was first presented: Michael Ben-Or, in a conference paper of a handful of pages — this field's monuments are short, sir; suspect the long ones — titled "Another Advantage of Free Choice." The advantage in question: a coin the scheduler cannot read. Ben-Or's protocol proceeds in rounds of votes; when a round's votes are decisive, gentlemen adopt and eventually ink; when they are not, each gentleman flips his private coin for a fresh opinion and tries again. The scheduler still controls all timing — but the fence campaign required steering, and steering requires forecasting reactions, and a coin has no forecast. The tablebase is blind one move ahead, forever. The price of the gate is stated with equal honesty: termination is guaranteed only with probability one — certainty of a new and weaker currency; the meeting ends almost surely, but no fixed deadline can be promised, and with naive private coins the expected wait grows savage as the parliament grows — exponential rounds in the worst arrangements. There are repairs — coins shared among the company, cryptographic and otherwise, bring the expectation down to civilized figures — and the whole trade-off is a starred door at tonight's desk, where a guided problem walks you through Ben-Or's safety and his lovely termination argument: with probability one, some round's coins all land the same way, and unanimity, once achieved, is never surrendered. The gate is real, it is beautiful, and the industry uses it less than the theory admires it — mostly because gate one is cheaper and the tooling won. But mark it as the answer to a precise question: it is the deterministic in FLP doing exact work.

Gate three: renegotiate the interface — failure detectors. This gate is subtler and, for your trade, the most clarifying. Nineteen ninety-six, Journal of the ACM, Tushar Chandra and Sam Toueg: instead of changing the timing model wholesale, package the timing assumption behind an interface. Give each gentleman one additional instrument: a suspicion service — a little oracle at his elbow that maintains a list of colleagues it currently suspects of being dead. The service may be wrong, sir — that is the entire point. It may slander the living; it may, for a while, acquit the dead; it may change its mind hourly. Chandra and Toueg's question: how much eventual honesty must such an oracle deliver for consensus to become solvable? Their celebrated answer — one of a family, and the family's weakest useful member: it suffices that eventually, some one correct gentleman is never again suspected by any survivor — that, plus a majority of the parliament alive, and consensus is solvable. All the slander in the world, forgiven, so long as the service eventually settles on trusting somebody trustworthy. And the companion result — Chandra with Vassos Hadzilacos and Toueg, same journal, same year — closed the question from below: the weakest oracle that suffices is exactly an eventual-leader service, one that eventually points every survivor at the same living gentleman. I state the theorem with respect and decline its proof, which is genuinely beyond this course — the honesty clause, exercised. But do not let the abstraction fool you into thinking this gate theoretical: a suspicion service is a timeout with its epistemology showing. Every health check in your fleet, every phi-accrual dial, every lease expiry is an implementation of an unreliable failure detector; the theory's contribution is the specification — how little accuracy suffices, and what the protocol may lawfully do with slanderous evidence. The instalments arrive on schedule: episode five arms the detectors with leases and fences; episode eleven prices their false positives in retry storms.

Three gates, sir, and one observation to bind them, worth a pin through the whole part: all three purchase the same commodity by different contracts. Partial synchrony buys it with time — eventually the network calms and a leader's authority sticks. Randomization buys it with luck — eventually the coins align. Detectors buy it with delegated suspicion — eventually the oracle settles on a leader all can follow. In each case the purchased good is a stretch of settled asymmetry: some interval in which one distinguished gentleman is, and is believed to be, in charge for long enough to shepherd a decision through. That is the commodity FLP proves cannot be manufactured from asynchronous determinism alone — and next week, when the Synod of a vanished Greek island convenes, you will watch a protocol whose safety needs no such commodity at all, and whose liveness is precisely the art of acquiring it gracefully.

Part Six — One Bad Afternoon

We change theorems now, though not, you will notice, technique. From the profound impossibility to the famous one: from the theorem every engineer should know and few can state, to the theorem every engineer can state and few state correctly. It concerns not eternity but one bad afternoon, and it will take us two minutes to prove and rather longer to disinfect.

The history, dates exact. In July of the year two thousand, at the ACM's Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing — the field's own parliament — Eric Brewer of Berkeley delivered an invited keynote proposing a rule of thumb from a decade of building web systems: of three desirable properties — consistency, availability, and tolerance of network partitions — a system may have at most two. A conjecture, offered as engineering folk-wisdom in the honourable sense. Two years later, in two thousand two, Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch — the same Nancy Lynch a third time tonight, sir; the woman fences on every piste in this field — published a short formalization and proof in SIGACT News, and the conjecture became the CAP theorem: Brewer's initials-friendly christening, Gilbert and Lynch's mathematics.

But a theorem is its definitions, so we take the three letters slowly, each with its exact meaning, because every abuse of this result begins with a blurred letter.

C, consistency, means here something quite specific: linearizability. We devote episode seven to that word — it is a debt formally issued tonight — and until then, the honest informal reading: the replicated service behaves, observably, as if there were a single copy of the data, applying operations one at a time, respecting real time — every read returns the latest completed write, full stop. Not "consistent" as your transactions department uses it, not "the data satisfies my invariants," not eventual anything. One-copy behaviour, sir. The strictest promise on the shelf, wearing the field's most abused word.

A, availability, means: every request that arrives at any non-failed node eventually receives a meaningful response. Attend to the quantifier — every request, at every surviving node. Not "the service stays up for most clients," not "the majority side keeps answering." If one lonely node on the wrong side of a partition receives a read and cannot answer it, availability — in the theorem's exacting sense — is forfeit. The theorem's A is total; the marketing department's A is a fraction with some nines in it; the two share a letter and nothing else.

P, partition tolerance, means: the guarantee in question survives the Post severing itself — provinces of the network isolated, every letter between them lost, for as long as the outage lasts. And here mark the asymmetry that undoes most recitations of this theorem: C and A are goods you promise; P is weather. Episode one entered the documentary record — the Bailis and Kingsbury catalogue, the correlated redundancy, the sharks; partitions are not a menu item you decline, they are a thing the world does to you. "Choosing" partition intolerance is choosing to have no promise for one of the afternoons that will certainly come.

The theorem: in an asynchronous network subject to partitions, no read-write storage service provides both linearizability and total availability. Two out of the trio, with P not optional. And now the proof, aloud and entire, as budgeted at two minutes — after FLP it will feel like a stroll, and the punchline is an old friend.

Sever the shop. Bingo's branch in one province; Gussie's in the other; the Post between them dead for the afternoon — every letter across the divide, lost. Both gentlemen are healthy, their provinces internally sound. Now, Bertie — forever a client, forever inconveniently timed — conducts two items of business. First: he writes to Bingo's side — update the delivery address, from the old to the new. Bingo is a non-failed node holding a request; availability compels a meaningful response; the write completes, acknowledged, on Bingo's side of the silence. Second: Bertie — or his man of business, or any client anywhere; the theorem needs only one reader — reads the delivery address from Gussie's side. Gussie is a non-failed node holding a request; availability compels an answer. And now the doppelgänger, fourth and final appearance tonight, performing the two-minute version of the murder: what does Gussie's province know of Bertie's write? Nothing. No letter crossed; none will while the afternoon lasts. To every gentleman in Gussie's province, this world — write executed beyond the divide — is indistinguishable from the world in which no write ever happened. Identical observations compel identical behaviour: whatever Gussie would answer in the writeless world, he must answer in this one. In the writeless world he answers the old address — it is the only address his province has ever contained. So he answers the old address here: a completed write, invisible to a subsequent read. Linearizability — one-copy behaviour, latest write — is violated, publicly, in a two-line trace. The only escape: let Gussie not answer — refuse, or stall until the Post heals. And that surrenders availability, total as the theorem demands it. Choose your betrayal, sir. On the partitioned afternoon, C and A cannot both be kept, and no ingenuity is pending that changes it, for the proof used nothing but the fact that information cannot cross a silence. That is the CAP theorem — Gilbert and Lynch dressed it in registers and formality, and the desk tonight reproduces their rigour, including their further result for partially synchronous timing, which survives the trip intact. The mathematics is honestly this small. It fit in a keynote slide in two thousand; it fits in two minutes tonight.

Which makes the subsequent decade of abuse all the more instructive, and I shall now prosecute the folklore with the relish promised in episode one, where I declared — you may recall the phrasing — that most citations of a certain three-letter theorem in vendor keynotes are theorems quoted shorn of their models, like maritime law cited in a desert. Exhibit by exhibit.

Abuse the first: "choose two of three," recited as if C, A, and P were three dishes and every architect picks a pair. You now know better twice over. P is weather — it is not chosen, it arrives; a "CA system" is a system with no stated behaviour for an afternoon that is certainly coming, which is not a category but a confession, an unstated model in the sense of episode one's discipline. And in fair weather — no partition in progress — the theorem is silent entirely: nothing in Gilbert and Lynch prevents a well-built system from being consistent and available on every ordinary day, and the good ones are. The honest sentence is smaller and sharper: when a partition strikes, and only then, each affected operation must betray either consistency or availability. The theorem is a theorem about one bad afternoon. Its citation as a universal design trilemma is heraldry, not law.

Abuse the second: the labels. An industry of taxonomists arose, stamping each database CP or AP, as though the theorem issued licence plates. Mr. Kleppmann — our standing companion's author — published in two thousand fifteen a critique, and a plea whose title-sentiment I endorse and assign tonight: please stop calling databases CP or AP. The letters, remember, are extremes: C is full linearizability, A is every-node-answers. Real systems are neither pole: they are configurable per operation, quorum-tunable, differently-behaved for reads and writes; a system may serve linearizable writes and stale-tolerant reads in the same breath, and most do — the label compresses a contract worth reading into a syllable worth nothing. When a vendor page says "CP," sir, the diligent question is episode one's question: which failures, which guarantee, at which price — show me the afternoon in the documentation.

Abuse the third, subtler, and the one whose correction earns its keep in design reviews: treating the partition dichotomy as binary when it is a dial. Nineteen ninety-nine, before the conjecture was even conjectured publicly: Armando Fox and the same Brewer published "Harvest, Yield, and Scalable Tolerant Systems," which offers the grown-up vocabulary for degradation. Yield: the fraction of requests answered. Harvest: the fraction of the relevant data each answer reflects. The partitioned search service that answers every query from the reachable half of its index has kept its yield and halved its harvest — every client served, every answer honest about being partial. The banking service that refuses writes on the minority side has spent yield to keep harvest whole. Between "consistent" and "available" there runs a whole negotiated frontier — answer with a bound on staleness, answer with an explicit partial-results flag, accept writes locally with declared reconciliation debts, the shopping cart's whole future lives out here — and systems that degrade well are precisely systems whose designers chose a point on the frontier deliberately, in peacetime, and wrote it down. CAP names the frontier's endpoints. Harvest and yield survey the middle, where the actual engineering lives.

And abuse the fourth — the omission, the one that costs the most money: partition-fixation. The CAP framing lets the unwary conclude that consistency's price is paid only on the bad afternoon, so a sturdy network makes strong consistency free. It does not, and the corrective is the grown-up formulation the plan promised: PACELC, proposed by Daniel Abadi of Yale in a column in IEEE Computer in two thousand twelve. The scheme reads: if Partition, then the trade is Availability versus Consistency — CAP, conceded; Else — in ordinary weather, which is most weather — the trade is Latency versus Consistency. For consider what one-copy behaviour costs on a perfectly healthy network: coordination. The write that must be seen by every subsequent read everywhere must reach agreement machinery — a quorum's round trip, a leader's confirmation — before acknowledgment; the toll is collected on every write, on the best afternoon, and it is collected in the currency your users actually feel. Latency collects even in peacetime, sir — that is PACELC's sentence, and it explains at a stroke the entire respectable half of the eventual-consistency industry: geo-replicated systems relax consistency not mainly because partitions terrify them but because the speed of light bills them daily; episode one priced Toronto to Sydney at a sixth of a second, and no theorem is required to find that expensive, merely a profit-and-loss statement. You will, before the season is out, see the invoice paid in the other direction — a company purchasing consistency across continents by buying better physics, seven milliseconds of deliberate waiting at a time. Both sides of that ledger are PACELC's E-clause in action. CAP is the afternoon the network betrays you. PACELC is every day, including that one.

There. The famous theorem, proven and disinfected. Two minutes of mathematics, ten years of misquotation, one afternoon of weather — and one more entry for a pattern you should now be noticing: the doppelgänger felled consensus in the grand theorem and felled the two-out-of-three dream in the small one, with the identical thrust — two worlds, one observer, no telling them apart. The master key, as advertised. By episode eight, when it walks in wearing a notary's collar to convict two-phase commit, I expect you to hear the footsteps in the first paragraph.

Part Seven — The Triage

Two theorems tonight, of very different weights, both routinely miscited — which makes tonight the correct evening to install, as promised in episode one, the course's standing triage. When any claim of the form "that cannot be done" crosses your desk — in a design review, a vendor negotiation, a postmortem, an architecture council — it goes into one of three bins, and the sorting is, I would submit, the most consequential single habit this course will leave you with.

Bin one: impossible. A theorem applies — this model, this guarantee, this exact ask — and the answer is no, permanently, for everyone, including the vendor whose deck says otherwise. Guaranteed-terminating deterministic consensus in pure asynchrony with a possible crash: bin one, by FLP. Linearizability with every-node availability through a partition: bin one, by Gilbert and Lynch. Certainty of common action over a channel that may lose the last letter: bin one, by the generals. The correct response to bin one is never effort; it is renegotiation — change the model (buy synchrony, buy detectors), change the guarantee (probability one, eventual, bounded-stale), or change the ask. The bin's discipline cuts both ways, mind: to place a claim here you must produce the theorem and match its model to your world, clause by clause — episode one's rule that a theorem quoted without its model is a noise. Most things called impossible in industry are not in this bin. Which brings us to —

Bin two: expensive. Possible, proven so, shipping in production — and carrying a price tag that arrives on schedule, forever. Consensus under partial synchrony: possible, at the price of timeout tuning, election storms in bad weather, and a liveness clause that reads "when the network settles." Linearizability across a continent: possible, at a round trip — or at seven milliseconds of purchased physics — per write, every write, peacetime included; PACELC's E-clause is bin two's ledger. Exactly-once effect: possible — episode ten — at the price of idempotence engineering throughout. The correct response to bin two is neither refusal nor heroism; it is an invoice: name the recurring cost, decide who pays it, and write both down. Most of the season from episode four onward, sir, is a walking tour of bin two — the honest accounting of how the impossible's neighbours are made routine, at what price, in which model.

Bin three: merely inadvisable. Possible, affordable — and unwise, for your workload, this year. Consensus on the critical path of every read when a lease would do. Strong consistency for a cache whose staleness nobody can observe. A cross-region transaction where a schema change would dissolve the need — we shall make that one a proverb in due course. Bin three is the bin of judgment, and it has a diagnostic all its own: bins one and two are properties of mathematics and markets, but bin three is a property of your requirements, and it is the only bin whose contents change when the business does. The senior failure mode is misfiling between two and three — paying bin-two invoices out of habit for guarantees the product stopped needing, or declaring bin-three inconveniences "impossible" to end a meeting. You have seen both, sir. So have I, and I was not always the one who caught it.

Impossible, expensive, merely inadvisable. When the claim crosses your desk: which bin, on whose theorem or whose invoice or whose judgment — and the conversation that follows is, in my experience, shorter and considerably more honest than the one it replaces.

Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, then. Four items, for the designer walking into a review.

Item one. State the model before citing the impossibility — either impossibility, any impossibility. FLP names its terms: asynchronous, deterministic, one crash tolerated, guaranteed termination. CAP names its terms: a partition in progress, total availability, linearizability. Quote the theorem with its terms attached or not at all; a three-letter incantation without its model is heraldry, and design reviews are not the College of Arms.

Item two. Safety is free; liveness is the hostage. FLP forbids guarantees of termination — it does not forbid success, and it never touches correctness. So interrogate any consensus-bearing design at exactly the joint the theorem names: what does this system do while it cannot decide? What do clients see during the election storm, and what is the story when the storm outlives the deadline? A design with a crisp answer has read its FLP; a design that answers "that won't happen" has not, and the scheduler is patient.

Item three. CAP is a theorem about one bad afternoon; PACELC is about every day. Budget both, separately, in writing: the partition posture — which operations betray consistency and which betray availability when the Post severs, chosen in peacetime, tested on schedule — and the peacetime toll — what every write pays for the consistency level you have selected, in milliseconds, on the invoice where the users can feel it. A review that discusses only the afternoon has priced the flood and forgotten the rent. And between the poles, remember the dials with names: harvest and yield — degrade deliberately, not accidentally.

Item four. Triage every impossibility claim into its bin — impossible, expensive, merely inadvisable — and demand the paperwork appropriate to the bin: a theorem with matching model for the first; a recurring invoice with a named payer for the second; a requirements argument, dated this fiscal year, for the third.

The reading, three pieces, all short — this field's monuments are short.

First, the text itself: Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson, "Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process," Journal of the ACM, April nineteen eighty-five. I assign it whole, and I assign the experience of it as much as the content: put an evening aside expecting a fortress and find nine pages, of which the fatal argument is three. Read their third lemma twice — it is tonight's pivot lemma, the fence held with the pen of a working mathematician, and the compression I performed aloud is redeemed there in full. When you finish, look up from the page and recall that this fenced off a corner of the possible for every machine that will ever be built. Three pages, sir.

Second: Gilbert and Lynch, "Brewer's Conjecture and the Feasibility of Consistent, Available, Partition-Tolerant Web Services," SIGACT News, two thousand two. Equally short. Read it for the definitions above all — what "available" totally means, what "consistent" precisely is — and note their second theorem, the partially-synchronous variant, which the keynotes never mention. And after it, as disinfectant, Mr. Kleppmann's critique from two thousand fifteen — the plea about the licence plates.

Third: Abadi's PACELC column, IEEE Computer, two thousand twelve. One evening, one acronym more euphonious than most, and a permanent upgrade to your vocabulary for exactly the conversation item three demands.

The desk now awaits with chapter three, and tonight it is the volume's flagship, stated as such with relish. The consensus problem formal; the game, the configurations, the valence apparatus; the commuting square as a lemma with last week's fingerprints on it; the pivot lemma with the compressed case written out to the last inequality — my debt from the set piece, redeemed in full; the endgame construction with its fairness audit; and the whole assembled as the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson theorem, entire, the proof this volume was built to hold. Gilbert and Lynch formalized alongside, both timing models. The problems in three tiers, as framed tonight: valence drills on a toy parliament small enough to see whole; the harvest-and-yield vocabulary made formal; the distinction between agreement among survivors and agreement binding even the dead — uniform agreement, a subtlety with a body count; and a sabotage problem I commend to you with particular warmth — the impatient tribunal, a rotating-chairman protocol of entirely plausible construction, the kind of thing a strong engineer designs on a Tuesday whiteboard, whose timeout-based suspicions can be arranged, by a scheduler of tonight's calibre, into two chairmen inking two different verdicts in the same afternoon. Per the standing rules: I certify the bug fatal; the split-brain execution is written out in the solutions, ink and all. Two starred doors for the ambitious: Ben-Or's coin walked through with a guide — safety, validity, and the lovely almost-sure termination argument — and the reduction that drags atomic broadcast into FLP's grave with consensus, so you may see how an impossibility, once proven for the atom, propagates up the periodic table.

And the ledger. An instalment falls due tonight, and I am pleased to pay it before the audience: crashed-versus-slow, issued in episode one — managed, never cured — tonight received its formal apparatus: the confusion was commissioned as the scheduler, and then promoted to murder weapon — the proof of the season's flagship theorem turns, at both of its critical junctures, on precisely the two worlds no survivor can tell apart. The debt is not retired, sir — it cannot be retired; that is rather the theorem — but it is now managed by mathematics instead of by anecdote, with further instalments to come. Three fresh debts issued tonight: partial synchrony, surveyed as gate one, is cashed next week, when protocols move in and furnish it; the suspicion services of gate three await their machinery and their price; and the C of CAP — linearizability, tonight defined by gesture and promissory note — will in due course receive its full legal definition, when the most overloaded word in computing is finally read its rights.

Next week, then: the parliament of our title. In nineteen ninety, Leslie Lamport — the third time the portrait has presided over an episode, and not the last — submits to the field a paper describing the fault-tolerant legislature of a vanished Greek island called Paxos, its protocol reconstructed from archaeological fragments, its correctness argued in the voice of an amateur classicist. The reviewers request the removal of the jokes. The author declines. The paper enters a drawer, and remains there for eight years, while the industry outside the drawer builds, badly, the very thing inside it — published at last in nineteen ninety-eight, by which time the field had learned to need it. My sympathies in the affair, sir, are theatrically divided, and I shall itemize them at the proper moment. We will not recite the protocol; recitation is how Paxos acquired its reputation. We will derive it — begin with naive majority voting, watch it deadlock, repair it, watch the repair lose a chosen value, repair the repair — every rule a scar with a story, until the Synod stands before you as the only shape it could ever have taken, and the fence of tonight is escaped through gate one with the toll paid in full view. The doppelgänger may take a well-earned week in the stalls. The crowded-club lemma makes its debut on the boards.

Until then, sir.
