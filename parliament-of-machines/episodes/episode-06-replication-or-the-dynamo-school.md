# The Parliament of Machines

*An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Six: Replication, or the Dynamo School*

## The Decembers of Amazon

Good day, sir.

In the early years of this century, the retailer Amazon had a seasonal ailment. Every December, as the shopping public converged upon it in their gift-buying millions, the load on its systems climbed toward the year's summit — and the systems in question were, in their deep tissue, relational databases: magnificent instruments, rigorously transactional, and constitutionally inclined, when overwhelmed or partially failed, to do the safe thing. The safe thing, for a database of that upbringing, is to *stop answering* — to refuse the write it cannot certify rather than accept the write it cannot guarantee. An honourable instinct, sir, bred from decades of banking. And catastrophically wrong for the business in question, because the write being refused was a customer placing an item in a shopping cart, and a shopping cart that says *no* in December is not a database exercising caution; it is a store turning away money at the door while the queue stretches round the block.

And there was a second commandment in the house culture, less quoted but equally structural: Amazon measured its services not at the average but at the *far tail* — service agreements written at the ninety-ninth-point-ninth percentile, the experience of the unluckiest customer in a thousand, on the reasoning that the unluckiest customers are disproportionately the heaviest ones. A database that answers beautifully on average and stalls its worst percentile during failover fails that covenant on exactly the days that matter. Hold both commandments — never refuse a write, and mind the slowest customer in a thousand — and much of tonight's architecture designs itself.

Beneath both commandments lay arithmetic of a frankness the trade has quoted ever since, because the retailer had done what retailers can and measured the price of its own sluggishness: experiments in which pages were artificially delayed found that every additional hundred milliseconds of latency cost a measurable slice of completed sales — the delayed customer does not complain, sir, or write to the management; he simply drifts away mid-purchase, and the tills record his absence — and an unavailable cart was priced dearer still, since it forfeits not only the abandoned basket but some fraction of the shopper's future custom with it. I ask you to hold that pricing alongside the theorem of episode three, because it is the missing premise that makes tonight's heresies rational: when the cost of refusing a write is denominated in December revenue, and the cost of accepting it in a little reconciliation labour later, the mathematics of the betrayal writes itself, and what looks from the banking pew like recklessness is, from the shop floor, merely a ledger read correctly.

Out of some years of this came a paper, presented at the Symposium on Operating Systems Principles in October two thousand seven: "Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-Value Store" — DeCandia and eight colleagues, with Werner Vogels, the company's chief technology officer, closing the author list. The paper described the system Amazon had built for its most commercially sacred workloads, and its founding commandment inverted the databases' instinct wholesale: the cart must *always be writeable*. Always. During partitions, during failures, during the strange weather of December — a customer with an item in hand is never told no. Everything else in the design — and tonight we shall tour everything else — follows from that single commercial sentence, including the price, which the paper states with a candour that made it famous: the system would sometimes hold *several* versions of your cart at once, and something, somewhere, would have to sort that out later. You will recognize, sir, from four episodes' distance, what was really being announced: Amazon had chosen its betrayal. Faced with the theorem we proved in episode three, on the afternoon the network severs, Dynamo betrays consistency, keeps availability, and — this is the part the industry took a decade to absorb — does so *on purpose, with receipts*, engineering the reconciliation rather than suffering it.

The paper founded a church, as I put it in the founding plan, and the church had denominations within the decade — open-source systems built in its image, several of which you have operated, one of which now carries its name with the ordinal filed off; the paper's own fate is a minor comedy of the trade, being among the most cited systems papers of its era for an architecture its authors' employer subsequently moved beyond in favour of tidier managed descendants. Churches outlive their founders' houses, sir; that is rather what makes them churches. But I shall keep the sermon for the movements. Tonight is the episode this course has owed the practising engineer from the start: replication *as a menu* — every honest way to keep copies of data on machines that fail, with every price tag turned face up. First, the classical column: one leader, and the recovery-point ledger that asynchrony quietly runs. Second, several leaders, and the bill called conflict — with a published disaster walked in full. Third, the Dynamo column: no leader at all — quorums loosened until they slosh, parcels held by neighbours, libraries compared by fingerprint. Fourth, the set piece at the fine-print counter: what a quorum overlap actually purchases — the answer will genuinely surprise, in both directions. Fifth, the two kettles, at last, in the same cart. And sixth, the menu folded into a decision procedure fit for a design review. The doppelgänger rests this week, sir, save one operational cameo; the crowded club, as noted, works Sundays.

## Part One — The Classical Column: One Leader

Begin with the arrangement your intuition already owns, because half its costs hide in plain sight. **Primary-backup**: one replica — the primary, the leader; today Bingo has the chair — accepts every write; the others hold copies and follow. The write path is the whole design: Bertie's order lands at Bingo; Bingo applies it; Bingo forwards it to Tuppy and Gussie.

A preliminary word on *what*, exactly, travels down those links, because a whole class of production ghosts lives in the choice. Forward the client's *statement* — the command as issued, **statement-based replication** in the manuals — and every follower re-executes it, which is cheap on the wire and haunted in exactly the way episode five's determinism diet warned: a command that consults the clock, draws a random number, or walks an unordered collection executes *differently* at every desk, and the copies drift while all machinery reports health. Forward the *effect* instead — the rows as changed, the bytes as written, which the trade calls **row-based** or *logical* replication — and determinism is moot because nothing is re-decided; the wire carries more and the ghosts carry nothing. The industry's mature systems ship effects, or statements laundered through a determinism filter, and the lesson is the log's lesson from last week wearing database clothes: *decide once, upstream; replicate the decision, not the deciding.*

And now the single most consequential checkbox in replication, the one that prices everything downstream: does Bingo *wait*?

**Synchronous** replication: Bingo acknowledges Bertie only after Tuppy — perhaps Tuppy and Gussie both — confirm the write is durably theirs. The purchase: at the moment of acknowledgment, the write exists in two or three places, and the death of Bingo loses nothing that was ever confirmed. The price arrives on two invoices. The daily one: every write pays the round trip to the slowest waiter — PACELC's peacetime toll, which you met in episode three. And the coupling one, staged in a sentence: Tuppy — synchronously required — has his afternoon pause, and observe what follows at Bingo's desk: *the primary cannot acknowledge anything*; every client write in the estate now queues behind one follower's garbage collector, and a machine bought for durability has become a machine that halts commerce. Wait on everybody and you have coupled your availability to your *least* available member — which is why production systems that wait usually wait for *one* of several followers, the semi-synchronous compromise: the write provably exists somewhere beyond the primary before the client hears yes, though nobody undertakes to say where. The disaster-recovery ledger names both columns, and both deserve their number in a review: the recovery *point* — how much confirmed history can vanish — and the recovery *time* — how long the estate stands dark while the failover machinery of the next paragraph deliberates. Synchronous replication buys the first number down to zero and pays for it in the second currency, daily.

**Asynchronous** replication: Bingo acknowledges at once and forwards at leisure. The purchase: the primary runs at local speed, followers may lag without harm. The price, sir, is a ledger the industry chronically declines to read aloud, so we shall — staged, with dates left blank for your own incident reports. Bertie places an order at half past the hour; Bingo applies it, acknowledges it, and queues the letter to Tuppy; the confirmation reaches Bertie's screen; the order is, by every promise the interface made, *placed*. At thirty-one minutes past, Bingo's power supply performs its actuarial duty. The letter to Tuppy is still in the queue — was still in the queue; the queue was memory. Tuppy is promoted, healthy, and *has never heard of Bertie's order*. No component misbehaved: acknowledgment was defined as Bingo's word, and Bingo's word died with Bingo. At any instant, an asynchronous estate carries a window of confirmed-but-unreplicated writes — everything acknowledged to clients that lives, as yet, only in the primary's ledger — and when the primary dies unrecoverably, *that window is the data you lose*. The disaster-recovery trade has a name for its width: the recovery point. Asynchronous replication *is* the decision to lose a bounded recent slice of confirmed history in exchange for latency — a perfectly defensible decision, sir, made every day by sensible people for workloads that can absorb it, and made indefensibly whenever it is made without the number attached. Seconds of exposure, times writes per second, times the worth of a write: the arithmetic fits on an index card, and the whiteboard will demand it aloud.

A word on the followers while they are still mere followers, because the classical column's second product besides durability is *read capacity*, and it ships with fine print. Tuppy and Gussie, holding copies, can serve reads — and every read they serve is a read of the past, aged by whatever their replication lag happens to be. Mostly milliseconds; under load, or after a hiccup, seconds or worse — and the estate begins exhibiting the small hauntings every operator recognizes: Bertie updates his address, refreshes the page, and sees the old one, because the write went to Bingo and the refresh was served by a lagging Gussie. He has written; he cannot read his own writing. There are named, purchasable remedies — pin a user's reads to the primary briefly after his writes, or route by session — and the honest generalization of those remedies is a family of contracts called the session guarantees, which next week's field guide will name and price properly. Tonight, the classical column's summary: follower reads are cheap, plentiful, and *stale by an unstated amount* — and "unstated" is, as ever in this course, the actionable defect.

Now failover, where the classical column meets episode one. Bingo goes silent. The runbook — or the automation; the distinction matters less than the industry pretends — must decide: is the primary dead, or merely slow? You know this question, sir. It has no answer from across a network; every failover system on earth resolves it with a purchased verdict — a timeout — and the wrong verdict has a specific, spectacular failure mode, so let it be staged rather than gestured at. Bingo, mid-afternoon, enters a garbage-collection pause of distinction. The monitor's three probes go unanswered; the verdict is rendered; Tuppy is promoted and the estate's writes are rerouted to him. Forty seconds later Bingo returns — from his own perspective, nothing has happened at all — and resumes accepting writes from every client and every stale routing table that has not yet heard the news. Two primaries, sir: *split brain*, in the trade's own word, arrived by way of the *false death* — the doppelgänger's operational cameo. The wrongly condemned, like last week's resurrected lock-holder, retains all his old authority and none of the news, and every write he accepts from this moment is a fork in the estate's history. The disciplined arrangements you already own from episodes four and five: promotion through consensus or a coordination kernel, so that at most one promotion can ever be chosen; and an epoch stamped on all replication traffic, so the deposed primary's letters *bounce* off followers who have seen a higher number — the skeleton's bones, doing their eternal work, and the fencing token's elder siblings. Undisciplined arrangements meet part two's postmortem.

One more entry in the classical column, less famous than it deserves and included per the founding plan precisely because it rearranges intuitions: **chain replication**, published by Robbert van Renesse and Fred Schneider — the tutorial's Schneider, sir; a small parish — in two thousand four. Arrange the replicas not in a star but in a *chain*: Bingo the head, Tuppy the middle, Gussie the tail. Run it once as correspondence. Bertie's write enters at the head: Bingo applies it and forwards down the link to Tuppy; Tuppy applies and forwards to Gussie; Gussie, the tail, applies — and *Gussie* acknowledges Bertie: the write is confirmed only on reaching the end of the chain. Bertie's aunt now reads — and her read goes *only to the tail*: Gussie answers from his own ledger, locally, no quorum consulted. Feel what the topology bought with no arithmetic at all: anything the tail has, the whole chain has — a write standing in Gussie's ledger has, by construction, passed through every desk above him — and anything the tail *lacks* is by definition unacknowledged. So the tail's answers never expose an in-flight write and never miss a confirmed one: strong reads, served locally, by geometry alone. The costs are equally legible: a write pays the full length of the chain, hop by hop, before confirmation — latency proportional to the chain; and a broken link demands a reconfiguration dance (a master, typically rented from last week's landlords, splices the chain: head failure promotes the second; tail failure promotes the second-from-last; a middle failure sutures its neighbours together with a small state reconciliation). An unlikely shape delivering strong guarantees cheaply; it resurfaces inside storage systems to this day, and the desk has you prove its central property tonight — a proof, I note with approval, that runs on the chain's geometry rather than on any club.

## Part Two — Several Leaders, and the Bill

Geography sings its siren song: why should a customer in Tokyo write to a primary in Virginia? Put a leader in every region — **multi-leader** replication — each accepting writes locally, each forwarding to the others, and every region enjoys local write latency. The purchase is real and the bill arrives immediately, because two leaders accepting writes to the same object, concurrently — merely elsewhere, in our exact and earned vocabulary — will conflict, and something must adjudicate.

Before the adjudicator takes the stand, a word on the postal map among the leaders themselves, because with more than two thrones the letters need routes, and the routes have failure modes of their own. The tidy geometries come first to any whiteboard: a circle, each leader forwarding what it learns to the next around the ring; or a star, one hub relaying among the spokes. Both economize on connections, and both harbour the same defect — a single stopped desk breaks the relay for everyone downstream of it, and the estate's convergence now waits upon a repair. The robust choice is all-to-all, every leader posting to every other, so that no single desk can dam the flow. But observe what the robustness purchases, sir: with many routes come races. A customer's update leaves Tokyo; a correction to that update leaves Tokyo a breath later; the two travel to Virginia by different intermediaries, and the correction, catching the faster packet, arrives first — an effect presenting itself before its cause, a child arriving at the party ahead of its parent. The cure you already own from episode two: the letters must carry causal paperwork, so the receiving desk can hold the correction in the anteroom until the update it corrects has arrived. And I note the pattern for the season's ledger, since it recurs tonight in grander dress: every time the trade widens a topology in the name of availability, the ordering bill arrives by return post.

The industry's default adjudicator deserves its arraignment by name: **last-writer-wins**. Attach a timestamp to every write; on conflict, keep the larger; discard the smaller. Silently. Stage the arraignment's evidence in four sentences. The Tokyo leader accepts a customer's corrected shipping address at what its clock calls two seconds past the minute; the Virginia leader accepts the *same* customer's phone-number update at what its clock calls three seconds past; the two writes touch the same contact record, cross in replication, and the timestamps adjudicate: Virginia's row — carrying the *old* address alongside the new phone number — wins whole, and the corrected address ceases to exist. Nobody is told. The customer's parcel ships to the old address three weeks later, and the investigation finds every system healthy and every log clean, because nothing malfunctioned: a policy executed. I shall say this with the precision the plan demands, sir: last-writer-wins is not a conflict *resolution* strategy; it is a conflict *denial* strategy — institutionalized data loss, wearing a tie-breaking rule — and its verdicts are rendered by comparison of cross-machine wall clocks, instruments episode two convicted of perjury four evenings ago; the "last" in the name is an opinion wearing digits. There are workloads where the policy is honestly fine — where any discarded write was genuinely superseded by the survivor: telemetry samples, caches, the last position of a cursor. There is no workload where it is fine *by accident*, and the desk's drills include auditing a schema, column by column, for which fields can lawfully board this particular vessel.

Between the throne and the timestamps sits a third posture worth naming, because much of the industry quietly lives there: **conflict avoidance**. Keep multiple leaders for latency, but route all writes *to a given key* through one designated home — this customer's records always to Tokyo, that one's always to Virginia — so that per key, the system is single-leader and conflicts cannot form. It works precisely until the routing must change — the customer moves, the home region fails — and in the changeover window the design discovers it was multi-leader all along, with the adjudication debt merely deferred and now due at the worst hour. A lawful economy, sir, provided the changeover plan is written before it is needed.

And because the postmortem clause licenses only the published record, here is the published record — GitHub, the twenty-first and second of October, two thousand eighteen, from the company's own account. During routine maintenance replacing network equipment, the connection between GitHub's East Coast datacentre and its other facilities failed — for *forty-three seconds*, sir; hold the number; it is the whole homily. In those seconds, the automated topology manager — a tool whose entire commission is to keep the databases writable by promoting replicas when primaries vanish — observed the East Coast primaries unreachable from where it stood, and did its duty: replicas on the West Coast were promoted; applications, rerouted, kept writing. The connection healed, forty-three seconds old, and the estate now held what multi-leader holds by design and single-leader holds only by accident: *two* recent histories — writes committed on the East Coast in the final seconds that the West had never received, and writes committed on the West during the interregnum that the East had never seen. Neither side could be discarded; both contained customers' truth; and the timestamps could not adjudicate because, as you have known since episode two, there was no fact of the matter for them to report. What followed, per the postmortem, was more than twenty-four hours of deliberately degraded service — failing over traffic, restoring from backups, replaying the orphaned writes, reconciling colliding records by hand and by script — a full day of engineering labour purchasing back the consistency that forty-three seconds of split authority had spent. The exchange rate is the lesson: partitions are cheap to *enter* and ruinous to *leave*, and any design that can produce two primaries, a split brain in one word, owes its reviewers, in writing, the reconciliation plan — before the maintenance window, not during.

Fairness obliges me to add: multi-leader has honest homes. Clients that go *offline* and write locally — the laptop on the aeroplane, the telephone in the tunnel — are each, structurally, a leader of one, and no throne can abolish them; and collaborative editing, where several hands amend one document live, is multi-leader by its very product definition. Both homes force the same conclusion the shop reached: conflicts must be *merged*, not adjudicated by clock — a thread that runs straight into next week's algebra.

And the honest ancestor in this column, named in the plan for the receipt-keeping: **Bayou**, from Xerox PARC, nineteen ninety-five — Douglas Terry and colleagues; a system built for exactly those disconnected laptops, decades early. Bayou accepted writes anywhere, expected conflict as the *normal case*, and demanded of every write two accompaniments, supplied by the application at write time: a *dependency check* — a predicate detecting that the world has changed under this write — and a *merge procedure* — what to do about it when it has. Stage its house example: two colleagues, disconnected, each book the same meeting room for the same hour; when the laptops resynchronize, the dependency check on the later-ordered booking finds the slot taken, and its merge procedure — written by the calendar's own author — books the adjacent hour instead, rather than either double-booking the room or silently deleting a colleague's meeting. Applications, not timestamps, resolved conflicts, because applications know what the data *means*. Nothing about the mechanism scaled gracefully to the modern fleet, and its total-ordering machinery had a primary hiding in it besides; but the moral survived intact and Dynamo inherited it whole: if you accept concurrent writes, the conflict is *application state*, and the system's duty is to *keep the evidence* — both versions, with their causal papers in order — not to eat one quietly and call the ledger balanced.

## Part Three — The Leaderless School

Now the Dynamo column proper, and let me set the stage at the width it deserves: five gentlemen tonight — Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, Barmy, and Oofy — because the choreography needs neighbours.

Dynamo's first move abolishes the throne entirely. No primary, no elections, no terms: any replica may accept any operation, and correctness is arranged, where it is arranged, by arithmetic. Each key has a **preference list**: the ordered set of replicas responsible for it — the list read off a hashed ring whose full geometry is episode nine's subject; tonight it suffices that for Bertie's cart the list reads: Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, then Barmy and Oofy as understudies. The top three — the replication factor N — are the home trio; a coordinator (any of them; whoever the request reaches) drives each operation. Three numbers govern everything, spoken as the season's templates demand: N, the copies kept — for us, three; the write quorum W — how many acknowledgments a write collects before the client is told yes; and the read quorum R — how many replicas a read consults before answering. Tuning the pair is tuning the temperament: W of one is the fastest, most fragile yes the school can utter; R of one is the cheapest, staleness-happiest read; and the school's central arithmetic — which you have already proven in your sleep — is that R plus W greater than N buys the overlap: any read quorum intersects any write quorum in at least one member. The count is the entire proof, and it fits in a breath: the write lodged with W gentlemen, the read canvasses R, and were the two parties disjoint the house would have to seat R plus W distinct members — more desks than the N it possesses, by the tuning's own premise. The crowded club, working its advertised Sunday. Two-and-two-of-three is the classic tuning: every read overlaps every acknowledged write in at least one gentleman, so the newest acknowledged version is always *somewhere in the room* when you ask — a sentence whose precise strength, and precise weakness, is the whole of part four.

But the founding commandment was *always writeable*, and strict quorums are not always writeable — if Gussie is down and Tuppy unreachable, a two-of-three write to the home trio must refuse, and refusal is the one forbidden act. So Dynamo loosens its grip: the **sloppy quorum**. Stage it in December weather. Bertie adds a kettle; the coordinator, Bingo, writes locally — one — and posts to Tuppy and Gussie; Gussie's rack has lost power and Tuppy's link is having a Cloudflare afternoon. A strict system waits, times out, and says the forbidden word. Bingo instead slides down the preference list: the letter meant for Gussie goes to Barmy, marked with a note — a *hint* — reading, in effect, *this parcel belongs at Gussie's desk; hold it and deliver when he returns.* Barmy stores the kettle-write in a separate hinted larder, acknowledges, and Bingo has his two: the client is told yes, the founding commandment honoured, in a storm, without a throne. **Hinted handoff**, sir: the neighbours hold the parcels. When Gussie's rack revives, Barmy's larder streams its hinted holdings home — the handoff proper, the second word of the name earning its keep: one short conversation, kettle delivered, hint discharged, larder emptied — and the estate converges to its intended addresses. The write was never refused; the durability arithmetic was honoured in *count* — the agreed number of copies exist on healthy machines — though not, temporarily, in *address*. Note what was quietly traded, because the paper is honest about it and its cheaper imitators sometimes are not: the crowded club's theorem spoke of overlapping clubs drawn from a *fixed* roster, and a sloppy write may land entirely on understudies while a later read consults the recovered home trio — overlap by arithmetic has become overlap by *high likelihood*. The school's whole posture is that December's revenue is worth a probabilistic corner, and the safety nets below exist precisely to sweep the corners.

Net the first: **read repair**. Every read at quorum R sees up to R versions of the object, with their causal paperwork (part five's subject). Stage it once: after the storm, Bertie's cart is read at two-of-three — Bingo answers with the kettle-bearing cart, Tuppy with the elderly kettleless one; the paperwork ranks Bingo's newer; the client is answered with the kettle. And then, in the same breath, the reading coordinator posts Bingo's version back to Tuppy, whose stale copy is quietly retired. Reads become janitors, sir: every act of asking tidies the room asked, so the estate's hottest data — the data most read — is also its best-repaired, by construction and for free. Attend to this mechanism; it returns in the set piece with its significance considerably promoted.

Net the second, and the episode's designated moment of wonder, so allow me the held breath now. Read repair tidies only what is read; cold data drifts — the entries nobody has asked for since the storm are exactly the entries nobody has repaired. The deep net is **anti-entropy**: periodically, pairs of replicas reconcile their entire holdings, so that divergence has a half-life even in the unvisited stacks. Said naively, that is absurd — two gentlemen comparing millions of entries over the Post to find the dozen that differ, a nightly postal audit of the whole estate. The instrument that makes it civilized is the **Merkle tree**, and I want you to feel the trick whole, with the numbers spelled as the house demands. Each replica builds, over its key range, a tree of fingerprints: at the leaves, a compact hash of each small range of entries; at each parent, a hash *of its children's hashes*; and so upward to a single root fingerprint summarizing the entire library. A million entries, a branching factor of a few dozen, and the tree stands perhaps four levels deep — hold that. Now Bingo and Barmy run anti-entropy after the December storm. They exchange *one* fingerprint each: the roots. Roots equal? Then — with confidence bounded only by the hash's honesty, which is cryptographic — the libraries are identical, and the reconciliation of a million entries has cost *one comparison*. Roots differ? Descend a level: a few dozen child fingerprints cross the Post; all but one pair match; every matching branch — with its hundreds of thousands of entries — is dismissed unexamined, and the descent recurses into the one disagreeing subtree. Four levels down, the differing leaf range stands isolated: a few hundred entries, of which the actual divergence — Barmy's storm-week hints, say — finally crosses the wire, and nothing else ever does. Two vast libraries, compared to the leaf in a handful of postal exchanges, shipping only their difference. The desk proves the cost lemma tonight — traffic proportional to the disagreement times the tree's depth, not to the library — and I permit myself the once-per-episode admiration here: the Merkle tree is the rare instrument that makes *verification* exponentially cheaper than *transfer*, which is why you will find it, sir, everywhere honesty must be checked at scale — in Dynamo's janitorial closet; in the version control system holding this very course's scripts, where every commit you have merged this fortnight was named by its fingerprint; and in the lottery-parliaments of last week's raised eyebrow, where the chain of blocks is nothing but this tree unrolled in time. One idea, three industries.

Net the third: membership itself travels by **gossip**. No coordination kernel holds the roster; each gentleman, at a steady modest cadence, rings up one colleague chosen at random and the two exchange everything they believe about the ring — who is present, who suspected, who newly joined — each keeping the fresher account wherever they differ. Feel the epidemiology, because the arithmetic is the charm: one gentleman learns a fact; after one round two know it; then four, then eight — rumour doubles per round, and a fleet of a thousand is fully informed in about ten, with no coordinator, no broadcast storm, and magnificent indifference to the loss of any particular messenger, since the rumour has no spine to cut. The lineage is Demers and colleagues at Xerox, nineteen eighty-seven — epidemic algorithms for replicated database maintenance, one of the field's loveliest papers, which took its mathematics quite literally from the epidemiology of infection. The modern refinement is SWIM, name-checked for the interested, and its improvement is a courtesy this course can now name precisely: before condemning a silent colleague, ask a few third parties to probe him *on your behalf* — indirect confirmation — so that one bad link between two healthy machines cannot slander either; suspicion is published as a state distinct from death, and the accused is given a gossip round to protest. You will notice, sir, that this is a failure detector — episode three's third gate, rebuilt without a landlord — and that its verdicts are exactly as purchasable, and as fallible, as every other timeout in this course.

Two mechanical notes complete the anatomy. The coordinator, first: I have spoken of one as though appointed, and none is — any member of the preference list may coordinate any request, and in several of the school's descendants the *client library itself* takes the chair, posting directly to all replicas and counting acknowledgments at the edge; the school is leaderless all the way out to the caller when it wishes to be, and the latency saved is one hop, which at the ninety-ninth-point-ninth percentile is real money. And sibling arithmetic, second, as an operational honesty: under heavy concurrent writing to one hot key, siblings do not merely occur — they *accumulate*, each unmerged rival breeding further rivals with every write that descends from a partial read, and the school's estates cap the family size and complain to their operators; a key with fifty siblings is not a database problem but an application refusing its merge duty, and the pager, for once, points at the right team.

That is the school entire: no throne to defend, no election to storm, every component symmetric, every corner swept by a net rather than barred by a guarantee. The operational temperament follows — Dynamo-school systems degrade gracefully and diffusely where leader systems degrade sharply and legibly — and the reckoning of which temperament your workload wants is part six's business. But first, the fine-print counter, because there is a widespread belief about what R plus W greater than N *purchases*, and it is false in an instructive direction.

## Part Four — The Fine Print: What the Overlap Buys

Here is the belief, stated as the industry holds it: *with R plus W greater than N, reads always see the latest write — that's linearizability, near enough.* Tonight's set piece dismantles the near-enough, then rebuilds it properly, and the rebuilt version — a linearizable register with *no consensus anywhere in it* — is among the most clarifying objects in the field.

First, honesty about the dials at their other settings, so the belief is met at its strongest rather than at a straw tuning. W of one, R of one — the school at its fastest — promises only *eventual* anything: a write acknowledged by a single gentleman may be missed by every read for as long as the nets take; nobody claims otherwise, and workloads that choose it know what they bought. The belief under examination is about the *respectable* tuning — R plus W strictly greater than N, the overlap real, every read quorum provably touching every acknowledged write's quorum. That tuning is routinely sold, and bought, as "strong consistency." Now the staged run, with the home trio and honest weather — no crashes, no partitions, merely the Post being the Post. Take the cast first, before anyone moves, because three desks and two readers is precisely the traffic that loses an ear. The object is Bertie's account balance. It is held at three desks — Bingo, Tuppy, and Gussie — and all three, at the outset, hold the same old value; call it version one. Three people will act, in this order: Bertie, who writes; then his aunt, who reads; then his uncle, who reads after her. The tuning throughout is the respectable one, two and two of three.

Bertie writes first. The new balance is version two, and W is two, so the coordinator — whichever desk took his request — posts version two to all three and waits for two acknowledgments. Bingo's acknowledgment comes back at once: version two is now durably at Bingo. Tuppy's copy is still in the Post. Gussie's copy is still in the Post. So the write stands, at this instant, *in flight* — lodged at one desk, acknowledged to nobody, the client still waiting.

The aunt reads next, R of two, and the two desks her read happens to reach are Bingo and Tuppy. Bingo answers with version two. Tuppy answers with version one. The causal papers rank version two the newer of the pair, and she is answered with it: *the new balance*. Perfectly true, fresher than required — no complaint yet.

The uncle reads last — after the aunt's answer has arrived and been acted upon, in strict real time. His R of two reaches the other pair: Tuppy and Gussie. Tuppy answers version one. Gussie answers version one. Concurring, stale, and served with confidence: *the old balance*.

And there, sir, is the crime scene: the aunt read the new value; the uncle, reading *afterwards*, read the old — the estate's observed history ran *backwards*. If the aunt telephoned the uncle in between — and next week this exact telephone call becomes the formal test — the family has watched money flicker. No quorum was violated: every read overlapped the write set; the overlap simply *delivered* the new version to one reader and not the other, because "the write set" was still a moving target. The belief dies on the distinction it never drew: R plus W greater than N guarantees the newest acknowledged version is *visible in the room* — it does not guarantee that *having been seen once, it stays seen*. Visibility is not linearizability. The overlap buys durability and freshness-on-offer; it does not, alone, buy an ordered world.

For the collectors of precise labels — and the desk files this formally — the object the bare overlap builds has a name in the register taxonomy: it is, in the well-behaved cases, a *regular* register: a read that overlaps no write in progress returns the latest completed value, full stop; a read that overlaps an in-flight write may lawfully return either the old value or the new. Both of tonight's family reads overlapped the dawdling write, so each was individually lawful under regularity — the crime was only visible *across* them, in the new-then-old order. Linearizability is exactly regularity plus the no-flicker clause: once any reader has been shown the new world, no later reader may be shown the old one. One clause, sir; the whole price of the summit is one clause about the past staying seen.

Now the repair, and its provenance deserves the naming ceremony: Hagit Attiya, Amotz Bar-Noy, and Danny Dolev, in work from nineteen ninety published in final form in ninety-five — the field says **ABD** — solved exactly this, years before Dynamo made the question commercial. The repair is one clause, and you have already met it wearing overalls: *the reader who has seen a version must make it durable before repeating it.* Formally: a read queries R, selects the newest version, and — before answering the client — **writes that version back** to enough replicas that a quorum holds it. Read repair, sir, promoted from janitor to load-bearing wall: not an optimization that tidies eventually, but a *blocking obligation* — the answer does not leave the building until the evidence is lodged with a majority. Run the crime scene again under the clause: the aunt's read finds version two at Bingo; before she is answered, version two is written back until a quorum — say Bingo and Tuppy — holds it; *now* she is answered. The uncle's subsequent read, any quorum of two, must intersect that majority: he cannot miss version two. The flicker is dead. Once seen, forever seen — monotonic, ordered, lawful.

Notice, before the theorem, two elegant consequences hiding in the clause. First: the reader has become a *helper* — if the original writer crashed mid-write, version two lodged at Bingo alone, the write is stranded neither committed nor gone; the first reader who encounters it *finishes it*, write-back completing what the fallen writer began, and the estate never needs to hold an inquest into whether the write "really happened" — whoever sees it first makes it official. Every doubt is resolved in the direction of durability, by whoever stumbles on it. And second, notice the tax and where it falls: reads now pay a write's postage — a second round trip, to lodge before answering. Practical descendants soften this exactly where softening is lawful — if the read's quorum already unanimously holds the newest version, there is nothing to lodge and the second trip vanishes; the toll is collected only when the room actually disagreed, which in a well-swept estate is rare. The guarantee is bought retail, sir, and mostly on the days it was needed anyway.

And the theorem this builds, stated with the care it has earned: for a single-writer register, the ABD protocol — writes stamped by a rising sequence from the one writer, lodged at a majority; reads that query a majority, adopt the newest, and write back before answering — implements a **linearizable register** in a fully asynchronous system, tolerating a minority of crashes. Full proof at the desk tonight, single-writer form; the multi-writer extension — where a writer must first ask the room for the highest stamp before writing one higher — is the starred door. Pause on what is *absent* from that sentence: no leader. No election. No consensus. No FLP shadow — the protocol *terminates*, always, weather regardless, and the reason bears saying precisely: FLP's fence stood on the adversary's power to keep two futures alive, and a register protocol has no fork to hold open — nothing in ABD ever *decides between* anything; it only lodges copies with majorities and adopts maxima, operations no schedule can leave on a fence. Patience assembles a majority; nothing further need be agreed. And the CAP invoice is paid exactly where episode three said it must be: during a partition, ABD's majority side proceeds and its minority side *waits* — consistency kept, availability surrendered on the small side of the severance, the betrayal chosen and signed.

And one clause of fairness to the school itself, lest the set piece read as indictment: Dynamo *knows* this fine print. The paper does not claim linearizability; it claims eventual consistency with causal paperwork, and its descendants that bolted the write-back clause on — several offer it, under names like "quorum reads with repair," at a documented latency premium — did so as a *setting*, because the school's founding judgment was precisely that the cart does not need once-seen-forever-seen, and should not pay for it. The set piece is not a scandal, sir; it is a *boundary marker*: the exact line where the school's arithmetic ends and ABD's clause must be purchased, drawn so that you may check, per system and per setting, which side of the line your keys live on — and so that when a vendor's page waves "R plus W greater than N" as a consistency claim, you may ask the one diagnostic question: *where does the read-back live, and does it block?*

And yet the guarantee delivered — linearizability — is the very consistency badge next week will crown as strongest. How do both stand — the strongest badge, without the expensive parliament? You met the answer in episode four's final movement, and here it pays its first invoice: *registers carry consensus number one* — the bottom rung of Herlihy's ladder. Reading and writing, however strongly consistent, cannot decide anything — cannot elect, cannot lock, cannot compare-and-swap; two gentlemen with a linearizable register still cannot agree on one bit against a single crash — and everything below consensus's rank is purchasable without consensus's machinery. There, precisely, runs the border, sir, and it is the practical discovery that surprises nearly everyone: linearizable *storage* is cheap — majorities and diligence; it is linearizable *decision* that costs a parliament. When a design review conflates the two — buying etcd to hold a value nobody ever mutates conditionally, or hand-rolling quorum reads and calling the result a lock — you now own the map that sorts it, and the map has a single test: *does any operation's outcome depend on winning a race?* If yes, parliament. If no, majorities and diligence suffice, whatever the consistency badge required.

## Part Five — The Two Kettles

Multi-writer reality now, and the payment of a debt planted in episode two's rival kettles. In the leaderless school, two writes to the same key from different clients, landing at different coordinators, are the normal case — and after episode two you own the honest description: if neither write's history includes the other, they are not "simultaneous," they are **concurrent — merely elsewhere** — and no lawful system may pretend one superseded the other. Dynamo therefore keeps, per key, exactly the instrument episode two built: a **version vector** — causal paperwork listing, per coordinating replica, how many updates this version has absorbed. The mechanics matter, so hear the loop whole: every read returns, alongside the data, its causal *context* — the vector; every write *carries back* the context of the read it descends from, declaring its ancestry in its own hand; and the receiving coordinator compares paperwork. A write whose context covers the stored version is a lawful successor — it supersedes, the old version retires with honour. A write whose context and the stored version's are *incomparable* — each ahead somewhere, episode two's exact test — is a rival, and the system does the only honest thing on the menu: it keeps **both**.

Siblings, the school calls them, and the shop calls them Bertie's two kettles — planted, you will recall, four episodes ago. Bertie, in his study, reads his cart — one umbrella, context supplied — and adds a kettle; the write, bearing the study's context, lands at Bingo. At the club, moments later by nobody's clock in particular, Bertie reads the *same* cart on his telephone — one umbrella still; the study's kettle has not propagated — and adds a kettle of a different and, one fears, redundant model; the write, bearing the club's context, lands at Gussie. The two version vectors — the contexts — are incomparable: each descends from the umbrella-only cart, neither from the other. Two carts now exist, both real, provably concurrent — and the next read *receives both*, siblings side by side, with their paperwork. Resolution belongs to the party that knows what a cart *means* — the application: and for a cart the merge is a union, kettle and kettle and the umbrella once, because the sin of showing a customer two kettles is billed rather lower than the sin of vanishing one. The merged cart is then written back bearing *both* contexts, a descendant of each parent, and the paperwork closes over the reconciliation like a settled estate. The paper is disarmingly frank that this loop is the business logic of the entire edifice: the cart merges; the customer occasionally deletes a resurrected item and mutters; December proceeds.

And before the footnotes, one minute's dwelling on what the merge function owes, because next week hangs upon it and the shop has already been obeying the laws without naming them. The union that mended the cart answered to three masters. It could not care in which order the siblings were presented — the study's kettle merged with the club's must build the same cart as the club's merged with the study's, or two readers repairing the same rivalry would manufacture new rivals. It had to survive redundancy — the nets of part three may deliver the same version twice, and merging a cart with a sibling already absorbed must change nothing, or the janitors would breed the very dust they sweep. And it could invent nothing — every item in the merged cart traceable to some parent, or the reconciliation is a forgery. Indifference to order, indifference to repetition, no invention: hold those three, sir, for they are not etiquette but load-bearing algebra — precisely the properties that let a room full of replicas repair in any order, along any routes, meeting any duplicates, and still converge upon one cart. Notice also who does the work: the merge runs in the *reading client*, at the moment the siblings are received, which is why the school's client libraries are famously thick and its servers famously thin — the intelligence lives at the edge, where the meaning lives. Next week gives the three laws their government names and the theorem they underwrite; tonight it is enough to observe that the humble union has been keeping all three since the day the paper shipped.

Three footnotes of due diligence, one wrapped gift finally opened. Footnote one: siblings are the *honest* mode, and last-writer-wins remains on the school's menu as the dishonest default — more than one of Dynamo's open-source descendants shipped with timestamp adjudication where version-vector paperwork belonged, or made the honest mode optional and the quiet mode default, and Kingsbury's laboratory has published the resulting autopsies, lost updates reproduced under partition on demand. When operating a school system, sir, the first audit question is not the quorum tuning; it is *which mode holds your keys*.

Footnote two: deletion, the school's quietest trap. To delete a kettle, one cannot merely erase it — an erased entry looks, to every reconciliation net in part three, like an entry the eraser *never had*, and anti-entropy will helpfully restore it from a colleague: the deleted kettle rises from the cart, again and again, resurrected by machinery doing exactly its job. The lawful instrument is the **tombstone**: deletion recorded as a *write* — a marker version saying, with causal paperwork, *this kettle was removed, downstream of these ancestors* — which propagates, wins its comparisons, and suppresses resurrection. And tombstones must eventually be garbage-collected, which reopens, in miniature, every windowed-deduplication argument of episode one's desk: discard a tombstone while any replica might still reintroduce its ancestor, and the resurrection returns. The school's estates all carry tombstone-lifetime settings for exactly this reason, and the operators who did not know why have met the ghost.

Footnote three, the gift from episode two: **dotted version vectors**. The classical vector has a subtle flaw in coordinator hands — versions written through the same coordinator on behalf of *different* clients can be filed as ordered when they were in truth concurrent, or spuriously multiplied into siblings that never rivalled anyone; the coordinator's single counter cannot say *which* update of its own series a version descends from, only *how many* it has seen. The dotted refinement gives each write one precise event coordinate — the *dot* — atop the summarized past: this version is exactly the coordinator's ninth event, descending from the first eight, rather than merely a version that has seen nine of them. Exact causality at coordinator granularity; no false siblings, no lost rivals; the founding treatment is Preguica and colleagues, in the reading notes, and the machinery proper is desk material. The audio's takeaway is the pattern you have now seen thrice, sir: causal bookkeeping is an engineering *budget* — full vectors, dotted refinements, or bare timestamps — and every discount below full honesty is a specific, nameable lie you elect to tolerate. What the kettles still lack is a *general* theory of merging — union worked for carts because carts are sets that grow; counters, documents, and calendars want their own laws, and the discovery that a whole algebra of mergeable objects exists, with a convergence theorem to its name, is precisely next week's second half. The kettles are in the same cart at last. Their wedding is episode seven.

## Part Six — The Menu as Decision Procedure

Fold the menu, sir, into the form it takes at a whiteboard, because a replication scheme — the plan's phrasing, and I cannot better it — is an answer to exactly two questions. **Who may accept a write?** One appointed gentleman (classical; conflicts impossible by construction, throne must be defended); one gentleman per region (multi-leader; conflicts by design, adjudication owed in writing); anyone (leaderless; conflicts normal, causal paperwork and merges owed in code). **And who must agree before the client is told yes?** Nobody but the acceptor (asynchronous; name the recovery window aloud); a fixed set (synchronous; name the availability coupling); a quorum (name R, W, and whether the quorum is strict or sloppy). And read the season's systems back through the grid, so it earns its keep at once: primary-backup with synchronous acknowledgment — one acceptor, fixed agreement; the classical bank. Raft and multi-Paxos — one acceptor per term, quorum agreement; the throne with arithmetic underneath. Dynamo at the classic tuning — any acceptor, sloppy quorum; the December machine. Chain replication — one acceptor, agreement defined as *the whole chain*, geometry standing in for arithmetic. Every system on the market is a cell in that small grid, whatever its brochure says, and most production incidents in replicated estates are one of three category errors: a recovery window nobody priced, a second primary nobody planned the reconciliation for, or a quorum overlap mistaken for an ordering guarantee. You now hold the instruments for all three.

Choose by workload, in the order that actually decides: first the consistency need — will the business absorb siblings, or stale reads, or lost updates? — episode seven arms this question properly; then the latency budget — synchronous hops and blocking write-backs are purchases against it; then topology — one region argues for the classical column; many regions, for the Dynamo column or for next fortnight's expensively synchronized alternative.

And because a procedure unexercised is a poster, run three of your own workloads through it aloud. The shopping cart: consistency need — siblings absorbable, loss forbidden; latency — sacred; topology — global. The grid answers leaderless, sloppy, vectors, union-merge: Dynamo is not *a* choice for the cart; the cart is why Dynamo exists. The payments ledger: loss forbidden *and* siblings forbidden — a double negative no quorum tuning satisfies; the ledger wants a throne with synchronous acknowledgment, or the parliament outright; December may queue. The service-configuration store: small, hot, catastrophic to fork — last week's coordination kernel, rented, with the fine print of episode five. Three workloads, three different columns of the same menu, one company — which is the quiet point, sir: the school wars of the trade press are category errors; estates are plural, and the menu is consulted per dish.

And a fourth dish, included because the menu owes last-writer-wins its one honest home, and the audit habit demands we name the home rather than merely gesture toward it: the telemetry stream — the device reporting its position, the sensor its temperature, the dashboard its heartbeat, thousands of times an hour. Consistency need: none worth the name, because each sample supersedes its predecessors by the data's own meaning — the reading anyone wants is the latest, and a discarded older sample was genuinely never going to be consulted again. Latency: cheap writes at enormous volume. Topology: everywhere at once. The grid answers leaderless at the loosest tuning, timestamps adjudicating with a clear conscience — the one column where the tie-breaking rule discards only what the workload itself had already discarded. Mark the reasoning shape rather than the verdict, sir: last-writer-wins became lawful here not because the tuning was clever but because the *meaning of the data* contained its own ordering — supersession was in the schema before it was ever in the clock. That is the test that generalizes, and it is the same test, run in the opposite direction, that convicted the shipping address in part two.

And since the procedure will most often be exercised not upon your own designs but upon somebody's brochure, here is the whole episode folded once more, into the five questions to ask aloud at the review — a catechism, answers in writing. Who may accept a write — one desk, one per region, or anyone? Who must agree before the client hears yes — and if a quorum, is it strict, or does it slosh to understudies in weather? If any acknowledgment is asynchronous, what is the recovery point — the number, sir, on the index card: seconds of exposure, times writes per second, times the worth of a write? Where does the read-back live, and does it block — is the consistency on the label bought with lodged evidence, or with arithmetic and optimism? And which mode holds these particular keys — causal paperwork with an owned merge, or timestamps quietly eating the loser — and if tombstones are anywhere in the design, what is their lifetime, and who chose it? Five questions; every one of them has been an episode of this course or a part of one; and a vendor — or a colleague, or a past version of oneself — who cannot answer all five has not designed a replication scheme, sir; he has purchased a rumour of one.

One temperament note from the operations desk, offered as folklore honestly labelled: leader systems fail *legibly* — there is a throne to inspect, a failover to narrate, one place where the incident lives — while the Dynamo school fails *diffusely* — everything degrades a little, latency creeps, siblings multiply, and no component owns the incident because no component owns anything. Choose also by which failure your organization debugs better at three in the morning; it is a real axis, and no paper prices it for you. Stage the two incident reports side by side and the axis becomes tactile. The leader estate's report is a narrative: the primary failed at seventeen minutes past the hour; the detector fired; the failover completed after forty seconds; here is the recovery window, and here is what fell into it — a story with a protagonist, reconstructible from three logs. The school's report is a weather system: latency crept for an hour before anyone paged; sibling counts rose on a fraction of keys; hinted larders swelled on machines nobody was watching; no single moment went wrong because no single place could. The first demands an organization that can execute a runbook crisply at night; the second demands one that trusts dashboards, tolerates ambiguity, and sweeps its corners on schedule. Neither temperament is superior, sir; but a team with the first temperament operating the second architecture will spend its incidents searching for a throne that does not exist.

## Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, four items.

Item one. A replication scheme is an answer to two questions — who may accept a write, and who must agree before acknowledgment. Demand both answers of any design, and file every brochure adjective under one of the grid's cells before believing it; an adjective that fits no cell is describing the marketing, not the machine.

Item two. Asynchronous replication is choosing a data-loss window; price it aloud, on the index card: seconds of typical lag, times writes per second, times what a write is worth — and then the tail case, because the lag during the incident is not the lag on the dashboard. A design that cannot state the number has not made the choice; it has merely deferred the discovery to the postmortem, where the number states itself.

Item three. Quorum overlap buys durability and freshness-on-offer — not, by itself, linearizability. Once-seen is not forever-seen until readers lodge what they saw with a majority before repeating it; if the vendor's page says "strong consistency" over bare R plus W arithmetic, ask where the read-back lives. And remember the border the repair revealed: linearizable storage is majorities and diligence; linearizable *decision* is a parliament. Do not pay for the second when the first suffices.

Item four. If concurrent writes are possible, the honest modes are two: prevent them with a throne, or keep the evidence with causal paperwork and an owned merge. Last-writer-wins is a third mode only in the sense that losing money is a business model; where it holds your keys, know it, and be able to say why the discarded write was never truth.
