The Parliament of Machines

An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Five: Consensus in Captivity

A Byzantine Failure in the Real World

Good day, sir.

On the second of November, two thousand twenty, a portion of Cloudflare's control machinery — the systems by which the company's operators configure and observe their global network — went dark for several hours. The company published its postmortem within the week, as is its admirable habit, and the document is remarkable twice over. Once for its candour, which we have come to expect from the better postmortem shelves. And once for its title, which reads like an admission one does not expect outside the theoretical literature: "A Byzantine Failure in the Real World."

The anatomy, compressed from the published account and told in our vocabulary. At the heart of the affected system sat a small parliament — an etcd cluster, three members, running Raft, precisely the machinery of last week's episode, industrious and correct. Around it, a network built with the customary redundancy. And in the middle of that network, a switch began to fail — not honestly, not totally, but partially: some conversations through it lived, others died, and the pattern was asymmetric. Member one could hear member two; member two could not reliably answer; the third saw yet another weather entirely. Attend to what this does to last week's machinery, sir. Raft's elections, its heartbeats, its timeouts — all of them were designed against the failure models we priced in episode one: crash-stop, crash-recovery, the honest silences. The parliament was built to survive members dying. Nobody had promised it members that were half-audible — a house in which every gentleman holds a different, internally consistent, mutually contradictory account of who can hear whom. The cluster did not split its brain — the crowded club held, as mathematics said it must — but it thrashed: elections that almost completed, leaders deposed by phantom silences, terms climbing, work undone. The theorem-grade guarantee — safety — survived without a scratch. Everything around the guarantee had a very bad day, because the world had wandered outside the model the guarantees were purchased in.

And a word on the small parliament at the story's centre, because it deserves the landing this course promises: etcd — spoken et-see-dee, a pronunciation the course's lexicon defends with its life — is the coordination service keeping, among much else, every Kubernetes control plane honest; if you have operated Kubernetes, sir, you have operated a rented Raft parliament, whether or not the tenancy agreement was ever shown to you. It is the direct descendant of tonight's two landlords: the Chubby pattern, rebuilt in the open, with the Raft of last week where Chubby keeps Paxos. When tonight's machinery is on the bill, it is not seminar machinery. It is the substrate of the orchestration layer of most of the industry.

The postmortem's authors reached, correctly, for the oldest word in this corner of the field: Byzantine. The word is forty years old this year, sir, give or take a publication lag, and tonight it finally enters our course through the front door. But not immediately — and the delay is the lesson. Because the Cloudflare incident is not, in the strict sense, a story about Byzantine machines; no component lied with intent; a switch merely failed in a way that made honest machines look inconsistent to one another. The story is about what happens when the tidy failure taxonomy of episode one meets production, where failures arrive in costumes the taxonomy never fitted. And that — the gap between the parliament in the proof and the parliament in the fleet — is tonight's whole subject. Last week we built consensus in the abstract. Tonight, consensus goes into captivity: put to work, rented out, operated, resurrected wrongly, and finally — in the hour's last movement — confronted with correspondents of genuinely low character. The itinerary: first, one value becomes a log, and the log becomes the most important character remaining to be introduced in this course. Second, the parliament learns to amend its own membership, which is trickier than it sounds. Third, the industry's actual arrangement: consensus as a rented service, with two celebrated landlords. Fourth, the resurrection problem — the most dangerous machine in your fleet is one declared dead that disagrees — and its two exorcists, the lease and the fence. Fifth, the Post learns to forge at last: a certain aunt takes the stage, a theorem of terrible beauty is proven entirely in the ear, and the arithmetic of quorums acquires a new column. Sixth, a single raised eyebrow in the direction of consensus by lottery. And seventh, the senior skill: knowing when not to convene the parliament at all.

Part One — One Value Becomes a Log

Last week's Synod decides one by-law, at a price of two round trips of the Post, and then stands complete, its single page of ledger filled, forever. A shop needs a new by-law every few milliseconds. The composition is the obvious one, and its name has been waiting since episode two: number the decisions. Slot one, slot two, slot three — a docket without end, each slot decided by its own run of the Synod, independent in principle, and the whole sequence constituting the log: the ordered record of everything the parliament has ever resolved.

Done naively, this is extortionate — two round trips and a quorum's disk writes per slot, with every slot fighting its own phase-one battle for the privilege. But we earned the remedy last week, in the fine print: the chairman. Let the house elect a distinguished proposer — today, as ever in the happy case, Bingo has the chair — and let Bingo run phase one once, with a single ballot number covering every slot at a stroke: prepare, ballot number seven, for slot one and all slots thereafter. The house promises once, confesses per-slot whatever fragments it holds, and thereafter, for as long as Bingo's chairmanship endures, every new decision costs a single accept round. One round trip from proposal to durability across a majority. That arrangement — the Synod per slot, phase one amortized under a stable chair — is multi-Paxos: the thing actually deployed everywhere the word Paxos is spoken with a straight face, and, as you observed of Raft last week, sir, structurally the same animal in different tailoring: Raft simply is multi-Paxos with the chairmanship made load-bearing and the log made primary.

Let us hear the machine run once at full correspondence, fair weather first, then foul. Bertie submits two items of business in quick succession: debit account seven by ten pounds; then credit account nine by ten pounds. Both letters land at the chair. Bingo assigns slots from his docket — the debit takes slot forty-one, the credit slot forty-two — and writes to the house: accept, ballot number seven, slot forty-one, the debit; accept, ballot number seven, slot forty-two, the credit. Tuppy writes both in his ledger and replies: accepted, slot forty-one; accepted, slot forty-two. Gussie — Gussie's replies will arrive presently; the chair does not wait for unanimity, only for clubs. With his own ledger and Tuppy's acknowledgments, Bingo holds a majority for each slot: both are chosen. The state machines apply slot forty-one, then slot forty-two, in docket order, at every member as the news reaches it; Bertie is answered; the whole exchange cost one round trip per decision, and the two decisions travelled in the same envelopes as often as not.

Now the foul weather, because it is where the composition earns its subtleties. Bingo goes quiet mid-stream: the debit of slot forty-one reached a majority, but his accept letters for slot forty-two reached only Gussie before the silence fell. Tuppy, in due course, suspects, prepares ballot number eight for all slots, and the house confesses per slot: for slot forty-one, accepted at ballot seven, the debit — Tuppy adopts and re-ratifies it, business as before, the invariant of last week working slotwise. For slot forty-two, Gussie confesses the credit at ballot seven; adopt the highest; the credit survives its author's silence. But suppose the weather had been one letter crueller — suppose slot forty-two's accept had reached nobody before the fall. Then the confessions for forty-two come back empty, and Tuppy faces a docket with a hole in it: slot forty-one decided, slot forty-three perhaps already carrying fresh business, and forty-two a void. He cannot leave it void, sir, and here is the reason in one sentence: the state machines apply the log in order, and an unfilled slot dams the river — every decision downstream of the hole, however thoroughly chosen, sits inapplicable until the hole resolves. So the new chair fills it forthwith — with the lost motion if any fragment of it survives in some confession, and otherwise with a formal no-operation: a motion that moves nothing, resolves the slot, and lets the river flow. Undignified, perfectly lawful, and the direct ancestor of a rule you met last week in Raft dress: the fresh leader who must commit an entry of his own term before pronouncing on inherited ones is performing exactly this rite — assert the new reign across the docket's tail, then let the past flow through. The family resemblance, as promised, is complete.

And now the christening this course has owed since episode two. Why is the log — not the consensus, the log — the character the industry actually built its temples around? Because of the acorn, sir, now grown to full oak: the replicated state machine. Take any deterministic machine — a key-value store, a lock table, a bank ledger, anything defined one operation at a time. Run one copy on each gentleman. Feed every copy the same operations in the same order — which is precisely what the log provides — and the copies cannot help but remain identical, forever, having at every step the same state, the same input, and the same rulebook. Lamport tossed this off in a paragraph in nineteen seventy-eight; Fred Schneider wrote the definitive tutorial in nineteen ninety, in ACM Computing Surveys, under the title "Implementing Fault-Tolerant Services Using the State Machine Approach," and the tutorial's quiet thesis is the industry's entire architecture: fault tolerance is not a property you add to a service; it is a property you inherit by driving the service from a replicated log. The service does not replicate. The log replicates; the service merely reads it. Agreement is needed once, at the narrow waist — slot by slot, on the order of operations — and everything above the waist gets consistency for free, by determinism. This is Herlihy's universality theorem from last week wearing overalls: own the bit, own the world; agree on the sequence, and any machine whatsoever rides along. When we reach episode ten the log will star in its own feature; when we reach episode twelve you will find it feeding gradients to accelerators. Tonight it needed only to be born properly and named.

One trap in the constitutional theory, flagged now because production falls into it weekly: the theorem's engine is determinism, and determinism is a stricter diet than programmers assume. The same operations in the same order yield identical replicas only if each operation's effect depends on nothing but the state and the operation — no wall-clock timestamps consulted mid-apply, no random numbers, no iteration over collections whose order the language declines to promise, no reads of the local machine's environment. Every one of those is a hidden second input, different at every replica, and each is a slow leak: copies that agree on every slot and drift apart anyway, the divergence surfacing weeks later as an inexplicable audit failure three episodes of machinery cannot explain, because the machinery was never at fault. The discipline: anything nondeterministic is decided once, by the leader, and written into the log entry itself — the timestamp chosen at proposal time, the random draw made before sequencing — so that what the replicas apply is, again, pure clockwork. The desk makes this a drill; your code reviews should make it a reflex.

One practical clause before the parliament learns new tricks, because it is the clause on which the next two parts turn: notice what the replicated state machine makes of the leader. All client traffic flows to Bingo; Bingo sequences it into slots; the house ratifies; the copies apply. From the outside, the ensemble now behaves — and I choose these words with the care of a man handling a debt — as if it were a single machine, one that happens to survive the death of any minority of its parts. The phrase "as if it were a single machine" is doing enormous, undefined work in that sentence, sir; episode seven pays that debt in full, with a definition sharp enough to test systems against. Tonight we bank the engineering fact and move on.

Part Two — Amending the Parliament

A parliament that cannot change its own membership is a parliament with a countdown clock: machines age, racks retire, capacity moves. And membership, mark it well, is not configuration in the operational sense — a line in a file — it is the definition of the quorum, the very denominator of the crowded club. Change it carelessly and you do not misconfigure the system; you dissolve the mathematics.

Stage the naive disaster in one breath. The house of five — Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, Barmy, Oofy — is to become a house of seven, adding two new members. Announce the new roster by ordinary letter, and for one terrible interval some gentlemen believe the house is five while others believe it is seven. A majority of the old five is three. A majority of the new seven is four — and the four may be found entirely among the gentlemen who have not yet heard, while the three are found among those who have... arrange the letters maliciously, sir — you know by now the Post will oblige — and two disjoint clubs each lawfully consider themselves a quorum: two chosen values, two leaders, the full split brain, achieved without a single machine failing, purely by disagreement about what the parliament is. The membership is itself a piece of replicated state, and it must be changed with the same ceremony as any other — through the log — with the added subtlety that the state in question defines the machinery doing the changing. The parliament must vote on the shape of the parliament, using quorums, while the meaning of "quorum" is the thing being voted.

Two disciplined solutions, both in production. The first is joint consensus, Raft's original proposal and multi-Paxos's folklore: transition through an interregnum in which decisions require majorities of both houses — the old five and the new seven, simultaneously. No decision passes without a club from each roster, the two eras cannot contradict one another, and once the joint arrangement is itself committed, a second log entry retires the old house. Correct, general, and fiddly — two overlapping quorum disciplines live in the code at once. The second is one-server-at-a-time: permit only single additions or removals, and observe the pleasing little lemma that makes it fly — spoken in full, because it fits in a breath. Any majority of a house of five and any majority of a house of six must share a member: the first club exceeds half of five — three or more; the second exceeds half of six — four or more; together that is seven or more gentlemen drawn from a combined roster of at most six, and by the arithmetic of small rooms, somebody was counted twice. The same sum works for every n against its successor, and the shared member polices the eras exactly as the crowded club has policed everything since episode four: consecutive configurations cannot contradict each other in ignorance, so no interregnum machinery is needed at all — provided, and only provided, the changes truly come one at a time, each committed through the log before the next begins. Simpler, and this is what most modern deployments actually run — with one honest asterisk, which I flag per the standing rules of this course: the simple scheme's original published form contained a subtle interaction between a half-completed membership change and a concurrent leadership change, found after publication and corrected by the scheme's own author — the desk's reading notes give the trail. The moral is not that the scheme is unsound; patched, it is sound and standard. The moral is that this corner of the protocol is where the dragons winter, sir. Membership interacts with leadership interacts with the log's tail; the new member must be caught up before he counts, lest quorums include gentlemen who know nothing; the removed member must somehow be told to stop campaigning, lest he haunt the elections he no longer belongs to — every clause a place where review headroom goes. When a design document proposes a bespoke reconfiguration scheme, that is the page to read twice.

Part Three — The Landlords

Now the industry's actual arrangement, because almost nobody who needs consensus runs their own parliament — and this is not laziness but the correct application of last week's whiteboard. Consensus is rented, from a small number of intensively engineered coordination services, and two of them are historical monuments worth knowing properly.

The first is Chubby, from Google, described by Mike Burrows in a paper at OSDI in two thousand six — "The Chubby Lock Service for Loosely-Coupled Distributed Systems" — that I will assign tonight as literature as much as engineering, because it is one of the field's great candid documents. Chubby is, on paper, a lock service: a small filesystem-like namespace of tiny files, on which clients take locks and store small metadata, backed by five replicas running Paxos. But the paper's confessions are the treasure. Burrows reports, with the weariness of a man who has read the tickets, what the service actually became in the hands of its tenants: a name service, a rendezvous point, a place to store configuration that absolutely must not be lost, a mechanism for electing primaries among client fleets — the whole pattern this course will call the coordination kernel: locks, leases, elections, membership, and small hot metadata, rented from one intensively-guarded parliament rather than rebuilt badly in every service that needs them. He reports the outages, in a table, with causes. He reports that most outages traced not to Paxos but to everything around it — operator error, misconfiguration, client abuse, the janitorial reality this episode is named for. And he reports the design consequence Google drew, which has become doctrine: concentrate the hard agreement problem in one small, obsessively engineered service, and let ten thousand tenants inherit its guarantees through leases and locks rather than running ten thousand parliaments. The economics of consensus, sir: the machinery is expensive to operate correctly, so operate it once, well, and sell coordination retail.

The mechanics of the tenancy repay one more minute, because they are the lease of part four already in action, wholesale. A Chubby client does not take out one lease per lock; it maintains a single session with the landlord — one master lease, kept alive by periodic exchanges, under which every lock, every cached file, every open handle shelters. And caching is the reason the arrangement scales: clients cache the tiny files aggressively and consistently, because the landlord tracks who caches what and, before committing any change, first posts invalidation notices and waits for the tenants to acknowledge — the change blocks until the caches are provably clean. Master lease, tracked caches, blocking invalidations: three decisions that turn a five-machine parliament into a service ten thousand clients can lean on without flattening it — and one operational tooth worth remembering at three in the morning: when a session's lease jeopardizes and recovers, or fails outright, the client library tells the application, in effect, everything you cached is now suspect and every lock you held may be gone — and applications that ignore that callback are the resurrection problem of part four, volunteering.

The second landlord is ZooKeeper, published by the Yahoo team — Hunt, Konar, Junqueira, and Reed — at the USENIX technical conference in two thousand ten, and subsequently the open-source world's coordination kernel of record: the metadata heart of Kafka, Hadoop, and a decade of systems you have operated. The tenancy pattern is Chubby's, and its furniture deserves a minute because the furniture is the product. The namespace is a little tree of znodes — tiny files, kilobytes at most, for this is a parliament, not a warehouse. Three modest mechanisms compose into everything else. An ephemeral node lives exactly as long as the session that created it: the machine dies, or pauses past its heartbeats, and the node evaporates — presence itself, as data. A sequence node is created with a number appended — one that only rises, courtesy of the log underneath; you may smile on schedule, sir. And a watch is a subscription: tell me when this node changes, once, cheaply. Now assemble, before your ears, the smallest election protocol in production service. Every candidate creates an ephemeral sequence node in the election directory: Bingo's request comes back stamped candidate seven; Tuppy's, candidate eight; Gussie's, candidate nine. The rule: the lowest number reigns. Bingo, seeing no number beneath his own, takes the chair. And the others do not — this is the touch worth admiring — crowd around watching the throne; each watches only the candidate immediately beneath himself: Gussie watches Tuppy; Tuppy watches Bingo. When Bingo's session dies, his node evaporates; the landlord notifies exactly one tenant — Tuppy — who checks, finds nothing beneath him, and reigns. No thundering herd at the moment of succession, no polling, and the whole edifice — presence, order, notification — is three primitives deep, every one of them a rental of the log beneath. That is what a coordination kernel is, sir: not a feature list but a small set of correct atoms from which tenants compose their own protocols, mostly without ruin.

The protocol underneath, called Zab, deserves the one careful paragraph the plan budgets, because it is our fourth family member and its distinction from Raft is instructive rather than cosmetic. Its stamp first, since you already own the reading of it: every proposal carries a zxid, spoken as the ledger's own compound — epoch thirty-two, counter seven — the upper half naming the reign, the lower half the position within it; comparison is by epoch first, counter second; and a follower discards anything from a stale epoch on sight. You have now seen this stamp four times in four dialects, sir — ballot, term, view, zxid — and I shall stop announcing the salary; consider the Lamport clock tenured. Zab, like Raft, elects a primary per epoch and stamps every proposal with an epoch-and-counter pair — the zxid, one more Lamport clock drawing a salary. The distinction lies in what the protocol promises about order. Zab is engineered as a primary-order atomic broadcast: its contract is that everything a given primary proposed is delivered in exactly the order that primary proposed it, and that a new epoch begins only after the new primary has provably absorbed every delivery the old epochs committed — recovery first, then broadcast, as strictly separated phases. Raft achieves the matching end state with its interleaved machinery — the up-to-date veto and log matching, policing continuously. The engineering flavour differs: Zab was built to sit under a primary-backup database (ZooKeeper's own), where the primary's proposal order is the database's semantics and must be preserved exactly through failover; Raft was built to be one teachable algorithm. At the desk, a problem has you set the two recovery disciplines side by side and mark where each spends its guarantees. The family census from last week thus completes: Viewstamped Replication first in print, Paxos first in folklore, Zab first in your production fleet, Raft first in the textbooks — one idea, four working dialects.

And one sentence of connective tissue, planted deliberately: everything the landlords rent — every lock, every election, every ephemeral node — is ultimately a claim about time: who holds what, until when, according to whom. Which delivers us, sir, directly into the crypt.

Part Four — The Resurrection Problem

Here is the most instructive horror story in production distributed systems, and I shall tell it first as the composite it usually is — assembled from familiar parts, per the postmortem clause — and then dismantle it properly.

Bertie's firm runs a payments pipeline. To prevent double-processing, a worker must hold the lock — rented, naturally, from the coordination service — before touching an account. Bingo, a worker, acquires the lock for account seven and begins his business. And then Bingo pauses. Not crashes — pauses: a garbage collection of heroic length, a virtual machine migration, a laptop lid of the metaphorical kind; the reasons are legion and all of them are episode one's bath. The coordination service, hearing no heartbeat, does exactly what it is designed to do: declares Bingo's session dead, releases the lock, and grants it to Tuppy, who begins processing account seven. And then, sir, Bingo wakes up. His pause, from his own side, was nothing at all — a skipped beat, invisible; no exception fired, no flag was raised; his memory says, with perfect internal consistency, I hold the lock for account seven, and he resumes mid-instruction, writing to the very account Tuppy is halfway through. Two writers, one account, and every component behaved as designed: the service timed out a silent client, as it must; Bingo trusted his own memory, as programs do. The corpse walked back in — episode one's exact phrase, three episodes early — and the wrongly condemned, restored to life, carries all his old authority and none of the news.

Attend to why no tuning fixes this. Lengthen the session timeout and you slow every legitimate failover in the fleet; shorten it and you manufacture more resurrections; and no finite timeout eliminates them, because the pause is unbounded — that is what asynchrony means, and we proved in episode three that the confusion underneath, crashed-versus-slow, is a conservation law, not an engineering shortfall. And attend, too, to how this presents in production, because the presentation is designed by malice's cousin, coincidence, to mislead you: the double-write is rare, the pause that caused it left no error anywhere, and the corruption surfaces hours or days later, in reconciliation, wearing the costume of an application bug. The investigating engineer finds the lock service blameless — it was blameless — finds both workers' logs internally coherent, and files the ticket under mystery. The tell, when you learn to look: a session-expiry event on one machine, bracketing a gap in its own logs, at the exact timestamp the service granted the lock elsewhere. Every seasoned operator has one of these in the drawer, sir; tonight the drawer gets a label. The debt register said instalments fall due tonight — the confusion managed, the suspicion services practicalized — and here is the payment, in two instruments.

Instrument the first: the lease. A lock, as naively conceived, is a permission without an expiry — and a permission without an expiry, granted over the Post to mortals who may pause indefinitely, is exactly the corpse's charter. So the landlords do not grant locks; they grant leases: permission for a bounded time, renewable by heartbeat, and here is the delicate part — the part with a small, pleasing proof at tonight's desk — the bound is enforceable without synchronized clocks. Recall episode two, sir: the gentlemen's clocks do not agree, and we forswore pretending otherwise. But the lease argument never compares two clocks; it compares two durations, and it assumes only that no gentleman's clock runs at a wildly wrong rate — a bounded drift in the tick rate, a far humbler purchase than agreement on the time of day. The grantor starts his stopwatch at the grant and will not re-issue the lease until a full period has elapsed by his own watch. The holder starts his stopwatch at the request — earlier than any grant could have occurred, a deliberately pessimistic anchor — and abandons the lease when a slightly discounted period has elapsed by his watch, the discount sized to the drift bound.

Run the arithmetic aloud with small numbers, in the house tradition. The lease period is ten seconds; the drift bound, generously, one part in a hundred — no watch in the fleet gains or loses more than a hundredth. Bingo posts his request and starts his stopwatch. The landlord grants — some fraction of a second later, but later, which is the whole trick of the pessimistic anchor — and starts his own. Bingo treats his tenancy as over when his stopwatch shows ten seconds discounted twice over — say nine seconds and four-fifths, the discount covering the possibility that his watch runs slow while the landlord's runs fast. Suppose the worst on both wrists: Bingo's watch loses a hundredth — his nine and four-fifths of measured time spans just under nine seconds and nine-tenths of true time; the landlord's watch gains a hundredth — his measured ten seconds elapses after just over nine seconds and nine-tenths of true time. The two intervals kiss and do not cross, sir — and Bingo's began earlier, at the request, widening the gap further. Both watches wrong about the hour, possibly by minutes; neither wrong about a duration by more than the bargain; no overlap, ever, at the price of a sliver of tenancy sacrificed to drift. It is the first time this course has bought a real guarantee from physical clocks, and note precisely what was bought and with what: mutual exclusion in calendar time, purchased with a bound on rate error, not offset error. Episode eight will go shopping in the same market with a vastly larger budget.

But the lease alone has not exorcised the corpse — attend, because this is the step the industry forgets. The lease guarantees Bingo knows his time is up... if Bingo's stopwatch is running. A paused process reads no stopwatch, sir. Bingo wakes, checks his watch, and — if his implementation is honest — discovers the lease expired and stands down. If. The check is a line of code in the tenant, and the tenant is precisely the party we have stipulated may pause at any instruction, including between the check and the write. The window narrows; it does not close. Which is why the second instrument exists, and why it belongs to a different party altogether.

Instrument the second: the fencing token. The insight was argued with particular clarity by Mr. Kleppmann in the great distributed-locking debate of two thousand sixteen — a public exchange worth a sentence of context, because it is the rare industry quarrel conducted at theorem grade. A popular recipe called Redlock proposed taking locks by racing timestamped grants across several independent stores, its safety resting, on inspection, on bounded pauses and well-behaved clocks — assumptions you now know to be purchases, not defaults, and purchases the recipe had not priced. Kleppmann's essay, "How to Do Distributed Locking," made the case that followed and the counter-essay from the recipe's author made the debate; the desk's reading notes preserve both sides, and the durable residue is the insight before us: the last line of defence cannot live in the lock holder, because the lock holder is the party whose sanity is in question. It must live in the resource. So: every lease the service grants carries a number — and you will smile, sir, at what kind of number: one that only rises, incremented at every grant; the smallest bone of the skeleton, as last week's whiteboard had it. Bingo's lease carries token thirty-three; every write he sends to the storage system is stamped thirty-three. When his session dies and Tuppy is granted the lease, Tuppy's token is thirty-four. And the storage system — the account ledger itself — keeps one integer per resource: the highest token it has ever seen, and refuses anything lower. Bingo's posthumous write arrives stamped thirty-three against a high-water mark of thirty-four, and dies at the door with a form-letter rejection. The corpse may walk, sir; the bank teller checks the date on his letters. Note the architecture of the fix, because it is the deepest thing tonight this side of the Byzantines: the lock service alone cannot deliver this guarantee — safety migrated into the storage interface, one compare on one integer, and the system is safe even against clients that are arbitrarily late, arbitrarily confused, and arbitrarily convinced of their own authority. The store, not the lock, has the last word. You will recognize, next week and the week after, that this is a general law struggling to be named: the enforcement point must be the point of effect. And a ballot number, a term, an epoch, a fencing token — one skeleton, as promised, now with its smallest bone in production clothing.

Part Five — The Post Learns to Forge

And now, sir, the hour's last model change, four episodes in the promising. Every failure this course has yet permitted has been a failure of silence — machines that stop, letters that vanish, pauses that deceive by absence. Clause five of the Post's charter, the single mercy, has stood since episode one: the Post does not forge. Tonight we revoke it. Letters may now lie about their authorship; machines may now say different things to different colleagues; a member of the parliament may now be — and here the course pays a debt of phrasing owed since the founding session — suborned by Aunt Agatha: an intelligence of unlimited malice and excellent penmanship, coordinating her nephews toward the maximum embarrassment of the house. The name the field uses is Byzantine, from a paper we shall come to in a moment; the failure model is: a corrupted machine may do anything — lie, forge, collude, selectively participate, imitate correctness for years and defect at the worst instant. We model malice not because every fault is malicious, sir — return to the opening scene: a half-dead switch has no intentions whatever — but because malice is the closure of all misbehaviour: a guarantee that survives Aunt Agatha survives every lesser confusion, every bit-flip, every half-audible switch, as a special case. One insures against the worst tenant, and the merely careless are covered by the same policy.

The history, dates exact, because this literature founded a subfield. In nineteen eighty, in the Journal of the ACM, Marshall Pease, Robert Shostak, and Leslie Lamport — the portrait presides a fourth time — published "Reaching Agreement in the Presence of Faults": the mathematics of agreement among processors some of which may be arbitrarily faulty, containing the bound that will close this part. Two years later, in nineteen eighty-two, the same trio — reordered as Lamport, Shostak, and Pease — published "The Byzantine Generals Problem" in TOPLAS, which contributed, beyond refinements, the framing: divisions of an army ringing a city, some generals loyal, some traitorous, needing to agree on attack or retreat by messenger. Lamport has been candid that the story was chosen deliberately — after the Albanian Generals were retired for diplomatic reasons — precisely so the result would be remembered. It was, sir. It is perhaps the field's most successful act of marketing, and I say that with admiration; we have seen this author's views on whether presentation is load-bearing.

A word on how this problem stands to the consensus of episodes three and four, so the furniture is arranged before the play. There, every gentleman arrived with his own opinion and the house settled on one of them. Here, one designated voice — the commander — holds the input, and the task is to broadcast it faithfully through mud: everyone loyal must end holding the same account of what the commander said, even if the commander himself is the liar. The two problems are cousins with an exchange rate: run one faithful broadcast per member, so that every loyal gentleman holds the same vector of everyone's opinions — the literature calls this interactive consistency — and then let each apply the same rule to the same vector; private opinions become common knowledge become agreement. Impossibility for the broadcast therefore poisons consensus, and the bound we prove for three generals is a bound on the whole Byzantine estate. The staging, then.

The problem, staged. One general commands; the others are lieutenants; the commander orders attack or retreat, by letter, to each lieutenant; lieutenants may then correspond among themselves. Two clauses of success. Clause one: all loyal lieutenants end agreed on one order — the traitors' final opinions being nobody's concern. Clause two: if the commander is loyal, the loyal lieutenants' agreed order is the commander's actual order — the usefulness clause; without it the lieutenants could agree on "retreat" unconditionally and call it a day, universal cowardice in epaulettes. And the celebrated first question: with three participants, one of whom may be a traitor, can it be done? Three gentlemen, one liar, majority intact — every intuition from the crash-stop world says comfortably yes.

The answer is no, and the proof — tonight's set piece, whole in the ear, the doppelgänger returned from the stalls exactly as promised, this time carrying forged letters — takes three minutes. The company: Bingo commands; Tuppy and Gussie lieutenant. Suppose a correct protocol exists; we shall trap a loyal lieutenant between two worlds.

World one: Bingo is the traitor; both lieutenants loyal. Bingo writes to Tuppy: attack. Bingo writes to Gussie: retreat — different letters to different colleagues; that is precisely the new licence. The lieutenants then correspond honestly: Tuppy tells Gussie he was ordered to attack; Gussie tells Tuppy he was ordered to retreat — each reporting the truth about his own mail.

World two: Gussie is the traitor; Bingo and Tuppy loyal. Bingo writes to both, honestly: attack. Tuppy corresponds honestly. But Gussie, in his exchange with Tuppy, forges: he reports that Bingo ordered him to retreat — a lie about his own mail, Aunt Agatha's simplest instruction.

Now stand in Tuppy's boots, sir, and audit his evidence in the two worlds. In world one: a letter from Bingo saying attack; a report from Gussie claiming Bingo said retreat. In world two: a letter from Bingo saying attack; a report from Gussie claiming Bingo said retreat. Identical. To the letter, to the comma — the worlds are doppelgängers to Tuppy, and you know what indistinguishability compels: whatever Tuppy decides, he decides in both. Apply clause two in world two: the commander is loyal there, his order was attack; Tuppy, a loyal lieutenant, must decide attack. Therefore Tuppy decides attack in world one as well — where, note it for the record, nobody loyal ordered him anything of the kind.

Now the mirror, and I shall build its worlds as carefully as the first pair, because the symmetry is the proof. World three: Gussie's experience of world one, seen from his own boots — the traitorous Bingo sent him the letter saying retreat, and honest Tuppy's report claims Bingo said attack. So in world one, Gussie holds: a letter saying retreat; a report claiming attack. World four, the new construction: Bingo loyal, and this time he ordered retreat — writing retreat to both lieutenants — while Tuppy is the traitor, forging toward Gussie the claim that Bingo said attack. Gussie's evidence in world four: a letter saying retreat; a report claiming attack. Identical to his evidence in world one, item for item — the two are doppelgängers to Gussie. Apply clause two in world four: the commander is loyal, his order was retreat; Gussie must decide retreat. Therefore Gussie decides retreat in world one as well. And world one now stands convicted: both its lieutenants are loyal, clause one is in force — they must agree — and Tuppy has inked attack while Gussie has inked retreat, each compelled by his own private twin of a world where obedience was mandatory. Contradiction, total and permanent. Three cannot tolerate one, sir. Not with any protocol, any number of rounds of further correspondence — the desk shows the argument swallows arbitrarily chatty protocols whole, since longer transcripts merely give the forger more pages to copy — and not with any cleverness. The pigeons of indistinguishability always come home. The engine, notice, is that a lieutenant cannot audit hearsay: a report about a letter is worth only its reporter's character, and with every voice worth the same, one liar in a triangle can always place each honest member in a world where the liar is someone else.

The general bound follows the same blood type and is stated with the respect owed a founding theorem: to tolerate f traitors requires three f plus one participants — no fewer suffice, by an argument the desk structures and stars: any smaller house could be partitioned so that three "super-generals," each played by a bloc, re-enact the three-way impossibility — simulate and generalize. And it is worth pausing on what became of our arithmetic, sir, because the whiteboard will demand it, and because watching the old arithmetic fail is more instructive than memorizing the new. In the crash world, tolerating f failures cost two f plus one seats, and the entire safety argument rested on one property of the witness in the club overlap: that he exists. Existence sufficed because an honest-but-silent world has no false testimony — whatever the witness's ledger says is true; the only question was whether a witness would be present to say it. Now re-run episode four's Synod safety argument with a liar in the house of three. The choosing quorum and the preparing quorum share a member, as ever — but suppose the shared member is the nephew. His confession to the new ballot is whatever Aunt Agatha pleases: he accepted the free delivery at ballot one, and he confesses nothing. The invariant's witness stands in the box and perjures himself, the new ballot proposes fresh business over a chosen law, and every line of last week's proof holds except the one that assumed testimony is true. That is why the price rises: with three f plus one seats and working quorums of two f plus one, any two quorums overlap in at least f plus one members — count it for f equal to one: two clubs of three drawn from a house of four must share at least two gentlemen — and after deducting Aunt Agatha's full allowance of f from the overlap, at least one honest witness remains, and honest testimony can be identified by its company: the protocols are arranged so that what must be believed is what f plus one voices agree on, a threshold no coalition of f liars can counterfeit alone. In the crash world the witness needed only to exist; in the Byzantine world he must exist in sufficient numbers to outvote the liars standing next to him. Same crowded club; steeper cover charge — and spoken as the whiteboard will demand it: five seats survive two crashes but only one liar; seven seats, three crashes but two liars. When a vendor tells you their five-node cluster "tolerates two failures," the diligent question is episode one's question — failures of which kind? — with tonight's arithmetic as the audit.

I shall spend the episode's wonder budget here, briefly, before the escapes, because the object earns it on economy alone. The proof you just heard used no probability, no rounds of induction, no machinery at all beyond the construction of two afternoons of correspondence and the observation that a loyal gentleman cannot tell them apart. It is the Two Generals' knife, the fence's knife, honed now against forgery — the third great result this course has watched fall to the identical thrust — and there is something close to aesthetic law in it, sir: every fundamental limit this field possesses is, at bottom, a statement that someone, somewhere, cannot tell two worlds apart, and every protocol that works is a machine for manufacturing distinguishability — stamps, terms, tokens, certificates — faster than the adversary can erase it. I know of no cleaner organizing principle in any engineering discipline, and we are barely halfway through the season.

Can the bound be beaten? Only by changing the model — episode three's triage, applied on schedule. Give the generals unforgeable signatures — letters that carry cryptographic proof of authorship, so hearsay becomes auditable: a lieutenant relaying the commander's letter relays the sealed letter, and forgery of the seal is off the menu. Then the three-node impossibility dissolves — the desk stars the door — because the engine of the proof was precisely that reports could not be audited: Gussie's lie in world two required him to compose a letter in Bingo's name, and against seals, composition fails; Tuppy demands the sealed original, Gussie has none, and the two worlds come apart in Tuppy's hands. Signatures do not abolish Byzantine trouble — a sealed liar can still stay silent, or seal contradictory letters to different colleagues and be caught only when the seals are compared — but they re-price it: with signatures, agreement survives any number of traitors for some problems, and the three-node wall in particular falls. The desk stars that door with the honest label on the price tag: the seals themselves now carry the load, and cryptography enters the trusted base.

And with or without seals, the practical monument is the protocol Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov — the same Liskov, sir; the field is a small parish — published in nineteen ninety-nine under the title "Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance": the Synod's spirit rebuilt for the three-f-plus-one world. Hear its happy-case shape as correspondence, with the smallest interesting house: four members — Bingo in the chair, Tuppy, Gussie, and one nephew of Aunt Agatha's, name withheld by the court; f is one, the house is three-f-plus-one exactly, and working clubs are two-f-plus-one — three. Bertie's request arrives at the chair. Round one, the pre-prepare: Bingo assigns it a sequence number and announces to all — I, chairman of this view, place this request at slot nine. Round two, the prepare: every member who receives the announcement broadcasts to every other — I have seen the chair place this request at slot nine — and each gentleman now collects such statements until he holds them from three distinct members. Feel what that certificate means, sir: three statements in a house of four leave room for only one absentee or liar, so any two such certificates overlap in at least two members — of whom at most one serves Aunt Agatha — and therefore no two honest gentlemen can hold prepare-certificates for different requests at the same slot: the nephew cannot tell Tuppy one story and Gussie another and have both notarized; the arithmetic of the overlap convicts him. Round three, the commit, repeats the ceremony one level up — I hold a prepare-certificate for slot nine — collecting again to three, which is what makes the decision durable across view changes; and then the house executes and all members answer Bertie directly, who believes the outcome when f plus one — two — matching answers agree, since two answers cannot both be nephews. When the chair itself falls under suspicion, a view change — an election, with epochs; the skeleton is eternal — installs a successor who must prove, with certificates, that he carries forward everything the old view committed. Three rounds in fair weather, correspondence quadratic in the house — everyone writing to everyone, the price of trusting no single voice — and a decade of scepticism about practicality that the paper's very title was chosen to answer. The desk boxes the message pattern and declines the full proof with a clear conscience and a pointer.

One deflationary note, offered in the spirit of the opening. Between the crash world and Aunt Agatha lies most of production reality: switches that half-fail, disks that flip bits, processes that emit one garbled message and then behave for a year. The industry's honest position is that full Byzantine machinery is bought where the threat model earns it — among mutually distrustful institutions, at blockchain frontiers, in flight computers — while inside a single company's fleet one usually buys checksums, sanity checks, and crash-tolerant consensus, and accepts, eyes open, that the Cloudflare afternoon is the residual risk. That is not negligence, sir; it is the triage — expensive versus merely inadvisable — executed with a signature on the risk register. What is negligence is the middle posture: believing one's crash-tolerant parliament is protected against corruption because it has "consensus" in the name. Raft agrees on what the leader says, faithfully, even when what the leader says is corrupted nonsense. Agreement is not truth; it is only, ever, agreement.

Part Six — Consensus by Lottery

One movement, as ruled in the founding session, with the eyebrow at the altitude the ruling specified. In two thousand eight, a pseudonymous author published nine pages describing a currency, and in passing an agreement mechanism of genuine theoretical interest: Nakamoto consensus. Observe first what it discards. The parliament is unbounded and anonymous — anyone may join, at any moment, wearing any number of masks; and with no membership roll there is no denominator, and with no denominator every quorum argument this course has built evaporates — the crowded club cannot convene when nobody knows how large the room is, and Aunt Agatha, who can print nephews at will, would own any headcount. The substitution is the nine pages' genuine idea: make voting expensive rather than enumerated. A lottery weighted by computational expenditure; the winner of each round appends the next block to the log — it is always a log, sir — and the honest strategy is to extend the longest chain one has seen; influence is proportional not to identities, which are free, but to electricity, which is not. Agreement is never final, only probabilistically deepening: a block buried under six successors is expensive to unseat, under sixty prohibitive, but the mathematics never says never — a sufficiently endowed adversary re-mines history, and safety rests not on quorum intersection but on economics: the attack must cost more than it yields, and the ledger's security budget is, quite literally, a utility bill. It is a genuinely different theorem for a genuinely different model — open membership, monetary adversaries, synchrony assumptions about propagation doing quiet, load-bearing work — and within its own terms it is elegant; the eyebrow is raised at the terms, not the derivation. The reasons this course now bows and moves on are two, sir, and neither is snobbery: first, our syllabus concerns parliaments with a membership roll, where the crowded club is purchasable at five machines rather than a power grid — inside a company's fleet, identity is cheap and trustworthy, and paying Nakamoto's premium there is buying flood insurance for a submarine; and second, the interesting engineering above Nakamoto — the finality gadgets, the proof-of-stake successions, the committee samplings that quietly reintroduce the very quorums the scheme began by discarding — is a course of its own, taught in a different building, with its own aunts. And the economics clause, I should add, is not hypothetical: on the minor currencies, where the utility bill is modest, the attack has been executed in the field — computation rented by the hour, history re-mined, settled payments unsettled — which is the theorem working precisely as stated, sir, merely on a chain whose security budget a criminal could afford; the premium buys exactly as much finality as it costs, and must be paid in perpetuity merely to keep yesterday settled. A raised eyebrow, a genuine nod, and the door closed gently. Decision eleven, executed.

Part Seven — When Not to Convene the Parliament

The senior skill, last, and quickly, because it is a discipline rather than a doctrine.

Consensus is the most expensive ordinary thing your systems do, and the ledger deserves to be read aloud once, in round figures, spelled out as the conventions demand. One decision: a round trip to a quorum — within a single facility, a millisecond or so; across availability zones, a few; across regions, tens to a hundred and more — plus a synchronous disk write at every voting member before it answers, another handful of milliseconds on honest hardware, less on the exotic kind. Chain the arithmetic: a coordination service in one region can settle perhaps a few thousand to some tens of thousands of decisions per second, and every tenant in the estate shares that budget. Compare the demand side: a busy service handles hundreds of thousands of requests per second before breakfast. Three orders of magnitude, give or take, between what the parliament can decide and what the front door admits — that gap is the entire architecture of coordination, sir: it is why leases exist (amortize one decision across ten thousand requests), why leaders batch (amortize one round trip across a hundred log entries), why the kernel stores only small hot metadata, and why every read that must be current is a question with a price tag — and here tonight's last debt is issued, sir, with ceremony. The instinct says a read may simply be answered by the leader from local state — he is the leader, after all. But you know the resurrection problem now: a leader can be deposed and not yet know it, and his confident local answer can be a posthumous letter — stale in the precise, formally-defined sense that the field guide ahead will name. The disciplined options: route reads through the log (correct, and priced like a write); or serve them under a lease — the leader answers locally only while holding a majority-granted time bound, the very instrument of part four, now insuring reads — correct exactly insofar as the drift assumption holds. The fine print on that "exactly insofar" is deferred, with the debt register's blessing, to the field guide's definitions.

So the catechism, and it is short. Convene the parliament for what is genuinely constitutional: leadership, membership, locks, the small hot metadata whose loss or fork is catastrophic — and rent even that, from a landlord with a Jepsen dossier. Do not convene it for traffic: for caches, for feeds, for anything whose staleness is survivable — episodes six and seven build the honest vocabulary for survivable — and never on a per-request critical path if a lease can amortize it. I have watched the catechism fail at a design review, and the staging is instructive: a team, burned once by a stale cache, resolved that every read of the product catalogue would henceforth consult the coordination kernel for the current version — a small read, they reasoned, and correctness is correctness. The kernel, shared by the entire estate, thereafter spent the bulk of its decision budget confirming that toasters were still toasters, and the locks guarding actual money queued behind the toasters; the estate learned the constitutional distinction during an incident, which is the most expensive of the available classrooms. The parliaments of this course are constitutional monarchies at best, sir: essential, dignified, and kept well away from the day's actual commerce, which is next week's subject entire.

Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, four items, for the review you will chair.

Item one. Rent coordination; do not build it. Your parliament should be somebody's product, with somebody's on-call rotation and somebody's Jepsen dossier — not your weekend. The competence you keep in-house is the tenancy: sessions, watches, and what your application does when the landlord's callback says everything you held is suspect.

Item two. Every lock in your estate is a lease, whether it admits it or not — find its expiry, and then find its fence. A lock without a fencing check at the resource is a resurrection waiting for a garbage collector, and the check is one comparison on one rising integer: the cheapest insurance sold in this industry.

Item three. Count your f's aloud — two f plus one machines against crashes, three f plus one against lies — and say which failures your quorums actually insure. Five seats survive two crashes but only one liar. A Raft cluster is not Byzantine-tolerant because the word consensus appears in its documentation: it agrees on what the leader says, faithfully, even when what the leader says is corrupted nonsense.

Item four, the Cloudflare item. Know which failures live outside your model entirely, and buy detection there — checksums, sanity bounds, divergence alarms — because the theorem only patrols the terrain named in its premises, and the weather does not read the premises.

The reading, two assignments and a shelf. First: Burrows, "The Chubby Lock Service," OSDI two thousand six — read it as literature; it is the rare systems paper written in the first person singular of hard experience, and its section on unexpected uses and abuses is the truest portrait of tenancy in the literature. Second: Lamport, Shostak, and Pease, "The Byzantine Generals Problem," nineteen eighty-two — for the three-node argument you now own, the signed-letters escape, and prose that earns its legend. The shelf, unassigned: Schneider's state-machine tutorial, nineteen ninety, for the log's constitutional theory; "Paxos Made Live," from the Chubby engineers, two thousand seven, for what the island cost to industrialize; the Cloudflare postmortem of November two thousand twenty, read against tonight's opening scene; and Mr. Kleppmann's locking essay of two thousand sixteen, for the fencing argument in its native habitat.

The desk awaits with chapter five: the replicated state machine formalized, with the identical-replicas theorem proved in a page; the lease theorem under drift, the small pleasing proof performed in full; the three-node impossibility written out with the two worlds side by side in an exhibit; the three-f-plus-one bound structured, its simulation argument starred; the PBFT message pattern boxed and checked against Castro and Liskov; and the problems — fencing protocol design with a proof obligation, the Zab-and-Raft recovery comparison, quorum arithmetic in the new column, and a sabotage of particular contemporary relevance, certified fatal per the standing rules: a fencing scheme that stamps writes not with a rising token but with the holder's wall-clock timestamp — because fresher time must mean fresher authority, must it not, sir? — whose fatal execution, a resurrected writer with a fast clock outstamping his living successor, is written out in the solutions to the last counterfeit millisecond.

And the ledger, sir — a heavy night of settlements, the heaviest of the season so far. Paid: the Post will learn to forge — the very first entry ever made in this book, issued in the opening hour of the season with a famous theorem promised as interest. Paid tonight in full: the theorem delivered, the aunt installed, the mercy clause of the charter formally revoked; the book's founding debt is off the books, and I confess the entry gives me the satisfaction of an old bill finally receipted. Paid besides: one value becomes a log — the log born, christened, and constitutionally established as the narrow waist of the entire architecture — and reconfiguration, the parliament now lawfully amending itself, the dragons' wintering grounds marked on the map; with instalments paid on crashed-versus-slow and the suspicion services, their management upgraded from mathematics to machinery. Issued, two fresh entries: the log will star in its own feature, stepping out of the parliament's basement to become the unifying abstraction of the industrial dataflow; and the fine print of reads under leadership — what current formally means, what a lease truly buys, and where the drift assumption quietly signs the contract — falls due when the most overloaded word in computing finally faces the bench.

The season's midpoint approaches, sir, and the shape of the campaign is worth one sentence at altitude: three episodes of impossibility taught us what cannot be promised; these two of machinery taught us what can be built regardless and at what price; and the four ahead descend from the parliament into the marketplace — replication without kings, the taxonomy of promises, transactions at continental distance, and the placement of everything. The theory half closes tonight. The engineering half is already knocking.
