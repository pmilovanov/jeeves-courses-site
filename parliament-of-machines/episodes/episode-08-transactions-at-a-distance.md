# The Parliament of Machines

*An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Eight: Transactions at a Distance*

## The Officiant Faints

Good day, sir.

Picture a wedding. The officiant, a thorough man, does not trust ceremony to luck: before pronouncing anything, he turns to each party in turn and collects a binding vow. "Do you?" — "I do," says the first, and by the rules of this particular canon the vow is irrevocable: having said I do, the first party may not change their mind, whatever follows. "Do you?" — "I do," says the second, equally bound. The officiant now holds both vows. He draws breath to pronounce the marriage — and faints, cold, on the chancel steps, before a syllable escapes him.

Now attend to the couple's situation, sir, because it is one of the purest predicaments in our field. Are they married? They cannot say. Perhaps the officiant, in the instant before falling, formed the pronouncement in his mind and — by this canon's rather bureaucratic theology — inscribed it in the parish register, which lies beneath his unconscious form where nobody may read it. Perhaps he had inscribed nothing. The couple are neither married nor unmarried; canon law offers no third state; and — here is the exquisite part — *neither of them may act*. The first party cannot walk away and marry another: the vow was irrevocable, and if the register says married, walking away is bigamy. Neither can they set up house: if the register says nothing, there is no marriage to set up. They stand at the altar, holding hands, indefinitely, while the guests shift in the pews and the caterer's bill accumulates — waiting for the officiant to wake, because only the register under his body knows the truth, and only he may read it.

I concede the canon sounds contrived, sir — until one prices the alternative rules. Let the first party's vow be revocable, and the officiant's pronouncement becomes worthless: he may declare a marriage whose bride has left. Let the couple act on silence — assume married, or assume not — and two guests who observed different moments of the ceremony will testify to different marriages, which is rather worse than no wedding at all. No clause of the predicament is decoration: the irrevocable vow, the single register, the mute unconscious officiant. Change any one and you have not solved the problem; you have merely changed which party is ruined.

That, sir, is two-phase commit, and the image is not a loose analogy but an exact one: the vows are prepare votes, irrevocable by protocol; the register is the coordinator's log; the faint is a crash; and the couple's paralysis is the blocking theorem — proved this very evening, by our old friend the doppelgänger, in his largest role of the season.

And the stakes are not antiquarian, sir; no corner of the field has seen fashion swing so violently. The classical era built empires on the ceremony — every serious database of the eighties and nineties shipped it, standards bodies blessed it, and application servers made the officiant a job title. The web era, staring at the latency and the funerals, swore off distributed transactions altogether — "we simply do not do that here" was, for a decade, a respectable architecture, and last week's shopping cart was its masterpiece. Then the pendulum, having visited both walls, returned with engineering: the planet-scale ledgers of the last decade — the systems this evening tours — concluded that the business world had been right to want the transaction and the web world right about the price, and set about paying the price properly: with parliaments, with physics, with determinism, or with signed and budgeted apologies. Sir will meet architects camped at every station of that pendulum, and tonight equips you to ask each of them the only question that matters: which theorem are you paying, and in what currency?

Tonight is about the transaction that spans machines: the payment that debits one ledger and credits another, on different continents, all or nothing, though machines fail and letters drown. We shall specify the problem honestly and discover it is a cousin of consensus with a crueller constitution — every participant holds a veto. We shall anatomize the two-phase ceremony and prove it blocks — not as an implementation blemish but as mathematics. We shall tour three-phase commit, a museum piece with a placard explaining why nobody runs it. And then the modern settlements, each a different escape from the theorem: the fragile roles rebuilt on replicated parliaments; Spanner, in which a company confronted with the speed of light simply *purchased better physics* — atomic clocks in the datacentres, uncertainty confessed as an interval, and seven milliseconds of deliberate, principled waiting, the set piece of the evening; Percolator, which dissolves the coordinator into the data itself; Calvin, which abolishes the conversation entirely by agreeing on inputs rather than outcomes; and the sagas, which abandon isolation honestly, compensations in hand — the daily bread of the microservices proletariat, eaten sometimes with dignity and sometimes not. Two threads from earlier evenings are gathered up within the hour: the physical clocks of episode two return, armed and expensive, and the serializability across shards that last week left hanging is settled tonight. The itinerary is before you, sir; the officiant is already swaying.

## Part One — Atomic Commitment, Specified

Begin, as I always insist, by saying precisely what is wanted, because the want is stranger than it looks. The setting: one transaction has done its work across several **participants** — it has debited a row at Tuppy's branch, credited a row at Gussie's branch — and the work is staged, provisional, locks held. Somebody must now decide: does all of it become permanent, or none of it? This is the **atomic commitment problem**, and its specification reads as follows. Every participant casts a vote, yes or no — yes meaning "my portion is staged, durable in the staging sense, and I can make it permanent on command"; no meaning anything from "my constraint check failed" to "I am on fire." The decision must satisfy four clauses. **Agreement**: no two participants decide differently — there is no marriage in which one party is wed and the other single. *Validity, in two halves*: if *any* participant votes no, the decision is abort; if *all* vote yes and nothing fails, the decision is commit. **Stability**: a decision, once made anywhere, is forever — no annulments by later reconsideration. **Termination**, the liveness clause: every correct participant eventually learns the decision.

Now place this beside episode three's consensus, because the family resemblance deceives everybody once. Consensus asks the parties to agree on *some* proposed value, and its validity clause is generous: any proposed value will do. Atomic commitment's validity is *unanimity in one direction*: commit requires every single yes. Hear what that grants each participant, sir: *a veto*. The shyest teller in the branch, the smallest shard in the estate, can refuse the transaction and the refusal is law. Consensus is a parliament — a majority carries the day and the minority is simply outvoted. Atomic commitment is a wedding — nobody is outvoted into matrimony. And this single clause is where all the trouble lives. In a parliament, a silent member can be left behind: the majority proceeds. At a wedding, a silent party who has already said I do cannot be left behind, because their vow might already be part of a completed marriage you cannot see. The veto converts silence from an inconvenience into a paralysis, and the rest of tonight is the working-out of that sentence.

A word on the cast of the specification, because the trade has proper nouns for it. In the classical architecture the participants are resource managers — databases, queues, ledgers, each sovereign over its own durable state — and the coordinator is a transaction manager, a role the industry standardized in the late eighties so that one vendor's officiant could marry another vendor's parties. The standard bears two letters — **XA** — and sir will want them, since they are how one finds this material in any database's own documentation: the XA interface survives in every application server to this day, and so do its failure modes, which is one reason this evening's theorem has such a distinguished operational history. And a word on the vote's fine print: a yes is not an opinion, it is a certificate of staged durability — I have written everything to stable storage such that, on command, I can commit through any crash. The certificate is expensive precisely because it must survive the participant's own death and resurrection; a participant that answers yes from memory alone has counterfeited it, and counterfeits are discovered, as counterfeits are, at the worst possible audit.

One refinement of the termination clause, sir, because the theorem trades on it. Weak termination — if no failures occur, everyone decides — is cheap, and two-phase commit delivers it handsomely. What one wants is strong, non-blocking termination: every correct participant decides even while others lie dead. Notice, too, that the specification does not forbid deciding abort generously — a coordinator may abort on any sneeze, and availability-minded engineers are perennially tempted to buy liveness with a hair-trigger. The temptation has a limit, and the limit is exactly the vow: aborts are free only before the yes votes are cast. After them, generosity becomes forgery. Keep that asymmetry in view through the proof: the protocol's entire character changes at the instant the first vote is surrendered.

The founding text is Jim Gray's "Notes on Data Base Operating Systems," nineteen seventy-eight — the same years and the same milieu as the origins we met in episodes two and three, and written in Gray's unmistakable register: plain words, total authority, the tone of a man describing plumbing he has personally soldered. The two-phase ceremony appears there fully formed, along with the observation that would haunt it: the protocol has a window in which a participant's fate lies in another machine's private log. Gray called the trapped state *in-doubt*; the industry came to call it limbo; the couple at our altar call it Tuesday. And one more piece of housekeeping from the specification before the ceremony begins: notice that atomic commitment says nothing about *isolation* — nothing about what concurrent transactions may observe while the staging is in progress. Tonight's protocols decide *whether* the work becomes permanent; last week's isolation levels govern *who may peek* meanwhile. The two concerns compose — and at the end of the hour, when the sagas abandon one of them, you will want the boundary sharp: the sagas abandon isolation, never atomicity of the individual steps. Keep the clauses in separate pockets, sir; the vendors do not always.

One more preliminary, because the episode's honesty depends on it. You may ask: is atomic commitment *harder* than consensus? Formally, in the crash-stop asynchronous model, the two problems trade blows. Given consensus, one can assemble commitment machinery of real quality: collect the votes, then run consensus on the conjunction — which is exactly where tonight's parliaments are headed. Given atomic commitment, one can extract a consensus-grade decision from the unanimity machinery in return; and episode three's impossibility accordingly shadows both — no deterministic asynchronous protocol solves either against even one crash at the wrong moment, which is why every protocol tonight leans on timeouts, majorities, or luck exactly where episode four's did. But the veto changes the *failure economics* utterly. Consensus tolerates a silent minority forever: the majority decides and moves on. Atomic commitment, with its unanimous validity, cannot decide commit while even one voter is silent — the silent one might have voted no — and cannot always decide abort either, as the blocking proof will show, because the silent one might hold evidence of a commit already sealed. The veto is the couple's protection and the couple's prison, and no cleverness of protocol design removes a clause of the specification. What cleverness *can* do — and the second half of tonight is a catalogue of it — is relocate the fragile roles onto sturdier machinery, shrink the windows, or renegotiate the specification itself. Mark the three verbs, sir: relocate, shrink, renegotiate. Every modern system you will meet tonight is one of the three.

## Part Two — The Ceremony, Anatomized

Now the ceremony itself — two-phase commit, staged with the company, slowly, because every clause earns its keep in the proof that follows. The scene: Bertie wishes to move ten pounds from his account, kept at Tuppy's branch, to his aunt's account, kept at Gussie's branch. The transaction has already done its provisional work — Tuppy holds the staged debit, Gussie the staged credit, each with locks on the touched rows per last week's isolation machinery. Bingo officiates: he is the **coordinator**. What follows is two phases, and the Post carries every word of it under the ordinary charter — letters delayed, letters drowned.

*Phase one, the vows.* Bingo writes to each participant: *prepare*. The word is a formal question: can you, if commanded, make your portion permanent — through any crash, any restart, any inconvenience? Tuppy, receiving it, does the load-bearing act of the whole protocol: he *forces his staged work and his vote to durable storage* — writes it to the log, waits for the disk to confirm — and only then replies *yes*. That log has a name worth carrying, since it has been quietly holding up every protocol of the last four episodes: the *write-ahead log*, so called for the discipline it enforces — the record of an intention is made durable *before* the intention is acted upon. Write ahead, then act. Sir will note the order in Tuppy's case: durability first, promise second; a promise made before the ink dries is the inaugural bug of episode one wearing a cassock. And from the instant that yes leaves Tuppy's hands, Tuppy is **prepared** — that is the state's proper name, the one a database prints in its own catalogue of stranded transactions — and, being prepared, he is **bound**: he has surrendered his veto. He may no longer abort his portion unilaterally — not on timeout, not on suspicion, not on the advice of counsel — because his yes may already be part of a commit decision sealed elsewhere. Gussie does the same. If either participant cannot promise — the credit would breach a constraint; the disk is full; the branch is on fire — he replies *no*, and a no is the one luxurious word in this protocol: a no-voter may abort at once and go home, for no lawful commit can ever include his refusal.

*Phase two, the pronouncement.* Bingo collects the votes. All yes: he *writes the commit record to his own log — the court of record — forcing it durable*, and only then tells the participants: *commit*. Any no, or any silence past his patience: he writes abort and says so. The participants, on hearing the decision, make it so — Tuppy turns the staged debit permanent and releases his locks; Gussie likewise — and each sends a small acknowledgment so Bingo may eventually retire the paperwork. Count the postage, sir, in the happy case: prepares out, votes back, decisions out — three messages per participant of consequence, plus the acknowledgments — and, more dearly, *two forced log writes on the critical path at minimum*: each participant's vote, the coordinator's decision. Forced writes are the protocol's heartbeat; every one of them is a place where a crash is survivable, and every economy on them is a place where it is not.

Two economies, sir, that are lawful, and worth naming because every production ledger runs them — they descend from Gray's world and they survive because they respect the vow. Presumed abort: the coordinator, we said, may abort freely before logging a decision — so let the absence of a record mean abort, by standing convention. The coordinator then need not force an abort record at all, nor remember aborted transactions, nor answer a recovering participant's query about one with anything but the presumption; forced writes are saved on every failure path, and the awkward recovery question — I find no record; what happened? — acquires a standing answer. Its mirror, presumed commit, exists and is subtler, relocating a forced write rather than removing it; the desk sets the bookkeeping as a drill. And the read-only participant: a party whose portion of the transaction wrote nothing has nothing to make permanent and nothing to undo; it may answer the prepare with a third word — read-only — release its locks at once, and leave the church before the pronouncement, since either outcome is the same to it. In estates where most transactions read widely and write narrowly, that one word is worth more latency than any protocol cleverness of its decade.

Hear the happy path once as a transcript, sir, thirty seconds, so the ceremony has a sound as well as a diagram. Bingo, to Tuppy: prepare, transaction seventeen — the number a name, not a count. Bingo, to Gussie: prepare, seventeen. Tuppy, to his disk: staged debit, vote yes, seventeen — force. The disk, in its own good time: done. Tuppy, to Bingo: yes, seventeen. Gussie likewise, a beat later. Bingo, to his own disk: commit, seventeen — force. Done. Bingo, to both: commit, seventeen. Tuppy: debit permanent, locks released, acknowledged. Gussie: credit permanent, locks released, acknowledged. Bingo: file closed. Twelve utterances, two forced writes on the critical path, one marriage. Now cut that transcript at any line you please and ask what the widow — the party that survives the cut, holding only its own log — may lawfully do; that question, line by line, is the recovery matrix, and the desk drills it exactly so.

Now the part Gray insisted upon and folklore forgets: the ceremony is defined as much by its **recovery rules** as by its happy path — what each party does on waking from a crash, and what each does about silence. Walk the matrix with me. *A participant crashes before voting:* on waking he finds staged work and no vote in his log; he aborts his portion freely — he never promised — and when the coordinator's prepare arrives again, or its patience expires, the transaction resolves abort. No harm. *A participant crashes after voting yes:* on waking he finds the vote in his log — he wakes prepared — and now he is in the trap — he must ask the coordinator how the story ended, and until an answer comes he holds his locks and waits; this is the state the whole evening orbits. *The coordinator crashes before logging a decision:* on waking he finds prepares sent and no decision; he is entitled to decide abort — nothing has been pronounced — and he says so to all. *The coordinator crashes after logging commit:* on waking, the log speaks; he re-announces commit to every participant, repeating until each acknowledges, which is why the letters carry transaction identifiers and why every participant must treat a repeated decision as the idempotent old news it is — the Post's twins, you recall, are delivered at no extra charge. Silence, throughout, is handled by patience and repetition: the coordinator re-sends prepares to the silent, re-sends decisions to the unacknowledged; a yes-bound participant re-asks the coordinator, and may also — a decent refinement — ask his *fellow participants*, since any of them who heard the pronouncement can repeat it, and any fellow who never voted can testify that no commit is possible. That last courtesy, called cooperative termination, shortens many a vigil, sir — but mark its limit precisely, for the proof will lean on it: if every reachable fellow *also voted yes and also heard nothing*, the company of them stands at the altar together, jointly bound, jointly ignorant, and jointly stuck.

Two engineering footnotes before the theorem, both of them scars from real ledgers. First: the coordinator's log is a *court of record*, and it must be administered as one — the decision is the forced write itself, not the announcement; a coordinator that tells participants commit before its own log is durable has created a world where its crash *un-decides* a pronounced marriage, and the aunt's credit evaporates on recovery. The pronouncement is the ink, sir, not the breath. Second: the in-doubt window is not an abstraction — a participant in doubt holds **locks**, and last week taught you what held locks do to every other transaction that wants those rows: they queue behind it. A fainted officiant does not merely strand one couple; he stalls the whole parish's business calendar, which is why the length of the in-doubt window — in the happy engineering case milliseconds, in the crash case however long recovery takes — is the number a practitioner watches above all others in this protocol.

And where do the patience thresholds come from? Nowhere principled, sir — episode one settled that; they are chosen, and the two dials pull opposite ways. The coordinator's patience with voters is cheap generosity: expiring it early merely aborts a transaction nobody had yet promised, and a retry is the Post's ordinary weather. The participant's patience with a silent coordinator, after a yes, buys nothing at any price — that is the theorem ahead — so the practical dial there is not a timeout at all but an escalation: page the operators, surface the in-doubt transaction on the console, and let the recovery drill — not the protocol — decide whether to raise the coordinator from the dead or, in the last resort, adjudicate by hand with the auditors watching. Every mature transaction manager ships that console, sir; its existence is the theorem's signature in the product.

## Part Three — The Blocking Theorem, by Doppelgänger

Now the theorem, sir, and the doppelgänger takes the stage for the performance the season has been saving for him. The claim, stated with care: *in any atomic commitment protocol in which participants surrender their veto — and with asynchronous messages, some vote-surrendering step is unavoidable for progress — there is a reachable situation in which a correct, live participant cannot decide anything and must wait, indefinitely, on the recovery of a crashed party.* Blocking is not a defect of Gray's ceremony to be engineered away by a cleverer ceremony. It is a property of the specification meeting the world.

The proof is an indistinguishability argument, and by now, sir, you could conduct it yourself — it is episode three's instrument, tuned one string tighter. Put Tuppy on the stand. He has voted yes; the vow is in his log; and now: silence. Bingo says nothing. Gussie says nothing. The Post shrugs — episode one's charter never promised delivery times. I shall now construct two complete worlds, both entirely consistent with everything in Tuppy's experience.

*World one — the marriage sealed.* Three parties move in this world, sir, and you have met all three: Bingo the coordinator; Gussie the other participant; and Tuppy, who is on the stand and does not move at all. Now one step to a beat. Gussie received his prepare. Gussie forced his staged credit and his vote to his own disk, and answered yes. Both vows — Tuppy's and Gussie's — reached Bingo. Bingo, holding unanimity, forced *commit* to the court of record. Bingo posted the pronouncement, one letter to each participant. Then Bingo's machine died. Tuppy's letter is still crawling through a congested exchange. Gussie's letter, however, arrived: Gussie made the credit permanent and released his locks. And then Gussie's machine died too. In world one, a commit *exists*. It is inscribed in a durable log; a participant has acted on it; the aunt's balance, on Gussie's disk, includes the ten pounds. If Tuppy in this world grows tired of waiting and aborts his portion — restores the debit, releases his locks, tells Bertie the transfer failed — the estate has committed on one side and aborted on the other: money created from nothing, agreement's clause dead, the one outcome the entire specification exists to forbid. And note, sir, that the prosecution required no exotic machinery: one crashed coordinator, one crashed participant, one slow letter — every ingredient certified ordinary by episode one's documentary evidence.

*World two — nothing decided.* The same three parties, in the same order of business. Gussie's prepare drowned in the Post; Gussie never voted at all. Bingo waited on the missing vote, and his patience ran out. Bingo was *about* to force abort — and his machine died with the log unwritten. No decision exists anywhere in the universe. In world two, if Tuppy clings to his locks awaiting a commit that will never come, he waits for a ghost — and the honest resolution, once Bingo recovers and consults his silent log, will be abort.

Now the doppelgänger's line, delivered as always without raising his voice: *Tuppy's evidence in the two worlds is identical.* The same vote in his log, the same staged debit, the same silence on every channel. Whatever rule Tuppy follows — and a protocol is nothing but a rule — it reads only his evidence, and must therefore prescribe the *same act* in both worlds. Let the rule say abort: world one convicts it of splitting the marriage. Let the rule say commit: world two convicts it of manufacturing one — committing a transfer whose other half may lawfully vanish, against a possible no. Let the rule say *wait*: correct in both worlds — and that is precisely the theorem. Waiting is the only act that is safe in every world consistent with Tuppy's evidence, and so waiting is what every correct protocol must prescribe. The couple stands at the altar because standing is the *only correct behaviour*, sir. The paralysis is the proof of their good character.

Sharpen the trap once more, because two natural escapes suggest themselves and both are mirages. First: let Tuppy ask Gussie. By all means — cooperative termination is decent practice, and in many failures it ends the vigil. But the proof has anticipated it: in world one Gussie committed and died; in world two Gussie never voted and died; a dead fellow testifies to nothing, and a reachable fellow who is himself yes-and-ignorant merely joins the vigil. The refinement shortens limbo when luck is moderate; it removes nothing when luck is poor, and theorems are priced at poor luck. Second: let the coordinator distribute word of the impending decision to the participants before finalizing it. Sir has just reinvented the third phase, and the museum in the next part is its mausoleum: under partitions the extra round does not close the gap — it relocates it, at permanent cost to every happy transaction.

For the desk's benefit, the theorem's exact perimeter, because precision here is what separates it from folklore. Blocking bites when every party that might hold the decision is unreachable — in our staging, coordinator and Gussie both dark; that conjunction is a total failure of the decision-holders, and the companion results draw the line sharply: grant a synchronous network and forbid partitions, and non-blocking protocols exist — the museum's exhibit builds one; allow total failures or partitions, and every protocol has executions that block. The practitioners' summary is blunter: two-phase commit blocks rarely and briefly when engineered well, always possibly, and the distance between those adverbs is the whole craft of Part Two's recovery matrix.

Attend to what the theorem does and does not say, because the boundary is where engineering lives. It does not say the wait is forever in practice — Bingo recovers, reads the record, repeats the pronouncement, and life resumes; the window is real but usually brief. It does not say every failure blocks — a coordinator crash *before* any commit is sealed resolves cleanly to abort; a participant crash strands nobody but itself if the coordinator lives. What it says is that there exists a conjunction — yes votes surrendered, decision-holders dark, evidence symmetric — in which no rule can do better than wait, and therefore the *worst case* of any veto-honouring protocol includes limbo. And attend, please, to the sabotage this proof convicts in advance, because it ships in real middleware more often than sir would care to hear: the participant configured to "time out and roll back" after voting yes — heuristic abort, the vendors call it, with an adjective doing heroic work. The doppelgänger has just shown you exactly which world that participant gambles against, and the desk's sabotage problem this week has you write out the split-brain ledger it produces, entry by entry, until the auditors arrive.

The operational record here is not hypothetical, sir. Every transaction-manager vendor's documentation carries the chapter — usually titled something anodyne, resolving in-doubt transactions — instructing administrators in committing or rolling back a stranded branch by hand; the chapter exists because limbo happens in the field, wherever the ceremony spans real networks. And the phrase heuristic outcome appears in the XA standard itself, complete with error codes for the aftermath: heuristically committed, heuristically rolled back, and — the standard's own name for the split ledger — heuristically mixed. When a protocol's official vocabulary includes a word for we guessed, sir, the theorem has been acknowledged in the small print.

## Part Four — The Museum of Three Phases

The first generation's response to the theorem was, naturally, to try to beat it, and the handsomest attempt deserves its glass case: **three-phase commit**, Dale Skeen, nineteen eighty-one. The insight is genuinely elegant, sir, and worth thirty seconds of admiration before the placard. Blocking, Skeen observed, comes from a fatal adjacency: in two-phase commit there is a state — voted yes, awaiting news — from which both commit and abort are still reachable outcomes, and a participant stranded there can rule out neither. Very well: *forbid the adjacency*. Insert a buffer state between vowed and married. The coordinator, on collecting unanimous yes, does not pronounce; it first disseminates a **pre-commit** — "a pronouncement is coming" — collects acknowledgments, and only then commits. The states now march in single file: uncertain, prepared-to-commit, committed. A participant stranded in uncertainty knows *nobody* can yet have committed — abort is safe. A participant stranded in pre-commit knows *nobody* can still be uncertain — and here the protocol's actual cleverness lives, in the termination rule under a new officiant. The survivors elect a replacement — episode four's machinery, note well, already smuggled in at the door — and the replacement canvasses the reachable. Anyone committed: commit all. Anyone still uncertain: abort all — safe, because the buffer state guarantees no absent party can have leapt ahead to commit. All reachable parties in pre-commit and none committed: the replacement completes the pronouncement. Each rule is sound, sir — against crashes. Under its stated assumptions the protocol is genuinely non-blocking, and the stated assumptions are the placard: *a synchronous network — bounded delays, honoured — and no partitions.*

Sir has been attending this course for seven weeks and can write the obituary unassisted. Bounded delays, honoured, are episode one's fiction and episode four's expensive rental; a partition is not an exotic event but a Tuesday; and under a partition the buffer state's logic inverts into a weapon. Watch the inversion turn, sir, with the full company on stage, because this placard is the museum's whole lesson and it is earned move by move. Bingo officiates for four participants — Tuppy, Gussie, Barmy, and Oofy — and all four have voted yes, so Bingo begins disseminating the pre-commit. The letters reach Tuppy and Gussie. Before the other two arrive, Bingo's machine dies — and in the same unlucky hour the Post partitions the estate: Tuppy and Gussie on one shore, each holding a pre-commit; Barmy and Oofy on the other, each still uncertain — vowed, and not a word since. Each shore now does exactly what the termination rule instructs, and I mean *exactly*. Barmy and Oofy elect a replacement officiant and canvass their shore: every reachable party uncertain, none committed — abort all, says the rule, and lawfully, for its own logic held that no absent party can have leapt ahead to commit. Across the water, Tuppy and Gussie do likewise: every reachable party in pre-commit, none committed — complete the pronouncement, says the rule, equally lawfully. Then the cable is mended, the shores compare registers, and the estate holds both verdicts at once — married on one shore, annulled on the other — which is the precise catastrophe the extra phase was hired to prevent. And mark exactly where the logic broke, because it was not in either shore's arithmetic. Every clause of the termination rule argued its safety against *crashes*: an absent party who is dead has done nothing and is doing nothing, so a canvass of the living settles the estate. A partitioned party is not dead. He is alive, holding his pre-commit, and running the same termination rule with his own replacement officiant on his own shore. The rule quantified over *all participants*; the canvass could only ever consult *all reachable*; and the substitution — harmless against the dead, fatal against the merely distant — is the gap through which the entire non-blocking warranty fell. Add the bill even in fair weather — an entire additional round of letters and forced writes on every single transaction, paid always, to defend against a crash that comes rarely — and the verdict of history is unanimous: nobody runs it. The scholarly line did continue, sir — quorum-based and enhanced three-phase variants repair the split verdict by requiring majorities before any shore may move, at which point one has reinvented, feature by feature, a parliament with extra steps; the literature's eventual settlement was to keep the parliament and drop the steps, which is precisely where the next part begins. Its placard, fairly written, reads: *a correct solution to a problem the real network declines to pose, at a price the happy path declines to pay.* Its true legacy is diagnostic, sir: it taught the field that the enemy was never the number of phases — it was the assumption of a world in which silence can be told from death. That lesson has a familiar shape: what three-phase commit wanted, without knowing the words, was episode five's failure detector — and the honest version of that bargain, agreement machinery that survives asynchrony by majority rather than by clockwork, already sat in episode four's parliament, waiting for the industry to notice.

## Part Five — Buying Better Physics: Consensus Groups, Spanner, and the Seven-Millisecond Apology

Which brings us to the modern settlement, and the first of the three verbs: *relocate*. The blocking theorem's pressure point was always a *singular* mortal holding a *singular* log — the officiant and his register. Episode five taught the season's standing remedy for singular mortals: make the throne an office, the log a parliament's decree. Run the coordinator as a replicated state machine — its decision is not a forced write on one disk but a committed entry in a consensus group; the officiant who faints is replaced by episode four's election, and the new officiant reads the *replicated* register and completes the ceremony. Run each participant's vote the same way, and no single machine's death strands anyone. Be precise about what this has and has not settled, sir; I shall be honest about the remainder. The theorem stands: consensus itself can stall while a partition denies majorities, and during that stall the in-doubt locks stay held; limbo is *shortened from "until a particular machine is repaired" to "until a majority can be assembled" — shortened, not abolished*. In exchange, every phase now costs a consensus round rather than a disk write. That is the trade the industry made, and it is the correct trade.

Be concrete about the relocation for one paragraph, since everything after tonight assumes it. Each parliament has a leader — episode five's throne — and the transactional roles are played by leaders on behalf of their groups: the coordinator is the leader of one designated group, each participant the leader of its shard's group. A participant's yes is no longer a private forced write but a decree — the staged work and the vote committed through its group's log, surviving the leader's death because any successor inherits the log and the promise with it. The coordinator's decision likewise: pronounced the instant the commit entry is chosen by its parliament's majority, whereupon any successor coordinator, elected after any faint, reads the decree and repeats it to whoever still waits. Every mortal in Part Two's ceremony has been replaced by an office, sir; the faint that stranded the couple now merely triggers a by-election, and the vigil lasts one election timeout rather than one hardware repair. The purists' edition of this move exists in the literature under the plainest imaginable title — consensus on transaction commit — written jointly by Gray and Lamport themselves: the votes become Paxos instances outright, the two-phase scaffolding dissolves into one parliament per participant, and the coordinator dwindles to a convenience. The industry mostly builds the pragmatic edition; but it is a comfort, sir, that when the founder of the ledger and the founder of the parliament finally collaborated, they proved the marriage lawful. The arrangement's most famous storefront is the paper the course now opens with some ceremony.

**Spanner**, Corbett and colleagues, Google, twenty twelve — the course's single most consequential industrial text. The architecture, in one breath, is everything this season has built, assembled: data partitioned into shards; each shard a Paxos group — episode four's parliament, thousands of instances strong; transactions within a shard decided by its parliament; transactions *across* shards — and here is last week's unanswered question, answered — by two-phase commit in which coordinator and participants are all parliaments, the fragile roles relocated wholesale onto replicated stone. Isolation by two-phase locking atop snapshot machinery of last week's kind. So far, sir, a superbly engineered assembly of known parts. What made the paper famous is none of it. What made the paper famous is a confession about *time* — and the audacity of answering a logical problem with a purchase order.

Recall what episode two left standing, armed ever since: physical clocks return, and expensive. Here is why they must. Google wanted **external consistency** — the paper's term, and you, sir, after last week, may use its government name: *strict serializability*. If a transaction commits, and afterwards — genuinely afterwards, in the world's own order, telephone calls included — a second transaction begins, the second must see the first; the star exhibit's telephone must never find a gap. Within one parliament the log's order supplies this for free. Across parliaments, on opposite sides of an ocean, whose order? Episode two's verdict was that "afterwards" across machines is precisely what a distributed system does not have: wall clocks order nothing at fine grain. The classical escape — capture causality with vector clocks — dies at planetary scale and, worse, cannot see the telephone: happens-before tracks messages, and the star exhibit's whole point was that reality contains channels the system never carries. To respect *every* side channel, you must respect *real time itself*. Google's response was not to weaken the promise. It was to change what a clock *is*.

**TrueTime**. In every datacentre, GPS receivers and atomic clocks — actual caesium-grade hardware, purchased with actual money; and an API of magnificent honesty. Ask the time and TrueTime does not return a number, sir — a number would be episode two's lie in a nicer suit. It returns an **interval**: earliest and latest — "the true instant lies between these, and I stake the datacentre on it." The interval's width is the confessed uncertainty: clock drift since last synchronization, network delays in the synchronization itself, all of it measured, bounded, and *admitted* — single-digit milliseconds in the paper's era, a few milliseconds in a well-run estate. The engineering beneath the confession bears one sentence: time masters in every datacentre — some slaved to the satellites, some to the caesium — cross-examined against one another so that a liar among them is outvoted; and between synchronizations each machine's daemon lets its interval *widen* at an assumed worst-case drift rate, snapping it narrow again at the next audit. The sawtooth of humility, sir: certainty leaking between confessions, restored by the network's absolution. Every machine's interval brackets the same true now. And upon that one honest confession, the commit-wait rule builds external consistency with arithmetic a child could check — which is precisely what we shall now do, aloud, numbers small, as the set piece of the evening.

A transaction is ready to commit. The coordinating parliament asks TrueTime for the hour and receives: earliest, ten o'clock and three milliseconds; latest, ten o'clock and ten milliseconds — a confessed uncertainty of seven. The rule has two clauses. *Clause one:* the transaction's timestamp is the *latest* edge — ten-and-ten. Not the middle, not the earliest: the pessimistic edge, the one instant no clock in the estate can yet have passed for certain... and clause two is why. *Clause two — commit-wait:* having chosen the stamp, the parliament *waits*. It holds the pronouncement until TrueTime's *earliest* edge climbs past ten-and-ten — until even the most cautious reading of the true now says the stamp is in the past everywhere. How long is the vigil? From now-earliest at ten-oh-three to past ten-and-ten: seven milliseconds, sir — roughly the confessed width; in general, about one uncertainty's worth of silence. Then, and only then, the commit is announced and the locks released.

Now watch the arithmetic pay, because this is the moment worth dwelling on, and I intend to dwell properly. Let a second transaction begin after the first has committed — genuinely after: Bertie sees the confirmation on his screen in London, telephones the aunt in Sydney, and *she* then issues the read; every side channel reality offers is in play. When Sydney's parliament stamps her transaction by the same rule, it consults its own TrueTime — whose interval, whatever its width, brackets the true now. And here is the whole proof, sir, in two sentences. The first transaction's stamp, ten-and-ten, was *held until it lay in every clock's past* — commit-wait saw to it — so the true instant of its announcement is later than its own stamp. The aunt's transaction begins later still, so when Sydney stamps it at *its* latest edge — an edge that cannot precede the true now — her stamp lands strictly above ten-and-ten. Afterwards in the world implies afterwards in the timestamps: *the wall-clock order and the timestamp order agree*, and a snapshot read at her stamp includes his write. No vector, no gossip, no message from London to Sydney carrying causality — the two parliaments never spoke. They merely both deferred to the same confessed, bounded, dearly kept physics. The one theorem in this course, sir, that is enforced by *waiting seven milliseconds on purpose*: the most expensive apology to physics ever tendered, and the cheapest strict serializability money can buy at that scale. Every transaction pays the little vigil; the estate's engineers are thereby placed on the clock-uncertainty payroll forever — the paper is charmingly frank that keeping the confession *narrow* is now an operations discipline, since every microsecond of sloppiness in the time infrastructure is paid, by every writer, in latency. Episode two's warning about wall clocks, sir: answered at last, and answered in caesium.

And one gift falls out of the arrangement that the paper is rightly proud of, carrying last week's photograph forward. Because every commit bears a truthful timestamp, the state of the entire estate as of half past ten is a well-defined object, assembled without locks and without coordination from every shard's versioned history — a consistent photograph, granted by the clocks rather than choreographed by the machines. A read-only transaction names a timestamp — safely in the past, or the present with a commit-wait-grade guarantee — and reads that snapshot at every parliament it visits, blocking nobody and blocked by nobody; the analysts' enormous queries run against the ledger while the tellers keep writing. The estate's followers serve these reads too: each replica tracks the safe time beneath which its log is known complete, and any read stamped below it needs no leader at all — the throne's postage, saved by honest clocks. The photograph will be industrialized in due course; tonight, note merely that once time itself is trustworthy, consistent reading is something you may simply take, rather than something you must negotiate.

One movement more in this hall, for the estates without a hardware budget: **hybrid logical clocks**, Kulkarni and colleagues, twenty fourteen — the civilian's TrueTime, and CockroachDB's arrangement of record. Weld episode two's two instruments into one stamp: a physical reading, disciplined by ordinary NTP, plus a logical counter that ticks whenever causality demands order the physical part cannot certify. The stamp tracks wall time closely — you may point at a record and say roughly when — while the counter quietly guarantees that message-connected events never stand in the wrong order: Lamport's clock in a wristwatch case. What is *not* obtained, and the honest engineer says so aloud: without a bounded confession there is no commit-wait proof, and the telephone can outrun NTP's error bars — reality's side channels may still glimpse a gap that the stamps deny. The civilian arrangements paper over the residue with retries and uncertainty windows of their own, and pay for it in tail latency rather than in caesium. The stamp's anatomy, for the desk: the physical part is the maximum of the local reading and the largest physical part yet witnessed in any message, so it never runs backward merely because one node's watch does; the logical counter ticks when events arrive faster than the physical part advances, and resets the moment honest wall time overtakes the conversation — and it stays *bounded*, the paper proves, by a constant tied to the clock error and the traffic, which is the starred problem at the desk this week. The moral is the season's oldest, sir: you may buy the bound, or you may live carefully without it, but you may not have it for free.

## Part Six — Percolator: The Coordinator Dissolved

The second verb was **shrink**, and its most instructive specimen is **Percolator**, Google again, twenty ten — a system built to solve a homely problem, incremental indexing of the web, atop Bigtable, a store that offered single-row transactions and nothing more. No transaction manager, no officiant anywhere in the building — and the designers' response was a move of real cunning: if no machine may be the coordinator, let the *data* be the coordinator. Dissolve the ceremony into the rows themselves, and let the court of record be a column.

The mechanics, narrated as ledger-keeping, which is what they are. Percolator gives every row, alongside its data, two small bureaucratic columns: a lock column and a write column. A transaction stages its writes at a snapshot timestamp in last week's multiversion manner, and when the time comes to commit, it performs two-phase commit *in the columns*. Phase one, and the cast before anything moves, because the rows are not equals. Of all the rows the transaction has written, one is anointed the **primary**; the rest are ordinary. The primary's lock cell *is* the transaction's court of record, the single place where its fate will be written. Every ordinary row's lock is merely a signpost bearing the primary's address. Now the move itself: for every written row, primary and ordinary alike, plant a lock — a single-row atomic act, Bigtable's one native sacrament. All locks planted — every vow collected. Phase two: at a fresh timestamp, the transaction executes upon the primary a single-row atomic exchange — erase the primary's lock, inscribe the commit record — and that one cell-write *is the pronouncement*: before it, nothing is committed; after it, everything is, and the remaining rows' inscriptions are mere paperwork, completed lazily, whenever convenient. The officiant has become an entry in the parish register that writes itself, sir — the register is its own priest.

Two pieces of supporting furniture, briefly, because the desk will box them. Timestamps come from a single oracle — a service dispensing strictly increasing stamps, in batches, to whoever asks; at this scale a mundane, replicated, prosaically reliable component, and a standing reminder that not every global agreement needs a parliament: agreement on the next number is cheap when one office holds the till. And reads follow last week's snapshot doctrine with one Percolator wrinkle: a transaction reading a row at its snapshot stamp first checks the lock column, and a lock from an earlier stamp means someone's ceremony is mid-air exactly where the photograph would be taken — so the reader waits briefly or, past patience, invokes the cleanup rite itself, rather than ever reading around a pronouncement in progress.

Now the part that earns this hall its place in tonight's episode: recovery, and the fate of the theorem. A Percolator client is a mortal process; it may faint at any point in the ceremony, leaving locks strewn like dropped vows. Another transaction arrives, finds a stale lock in its path — and here is the difference from Part Three: *the evidence is not private*. The lock names the primary; the primary's cell holds the truth, readable by anyone; and single-row atomicity makes consultation and cleanup safe. Finder reads primary: commit record present? The marriage was pronounced — the finder helpfully completes the dead transaction's paperwork, rolling the write forward, and proceeds. Primary still locked? No pronouncement ever happened — the finder erases the primary lock (atomically, so no late-waking client can pronounce afterward: the erasure *is* a binding abort inscribed in the same court), rolls the debris back, and proceeds. The in-doubt window has not vanished, sir — the theorem is not insulted; there is still an instant of fate held in a single cell — but the *waiting* has been transformed into *work that any passerby may finish*. Nobody stands at the altar: whoever next enters the church completes or annuls the ceremony from the public record and gets on with their own errand. Limbo shrunk from a vigil to a chore. The price, paid in the currency this course has taught you to check first: every conflict is discovered by *encounter* rather than by announcement; cleanup rides on the backs of innocent bystanders' latency; and the whole edifice leans on the store's single-row atomicity the way Part Two leaned on forced writes — the sacrament relocated, never removed.

The system's homely purpose deserves the last word in this hall, because it explains the temperament. Percolator was built to keep a web index fresh — batch work becoming incremental work — where one transaction's latency mattered far less than the fleet's throughput, and where a stray hour-old lock, cleaned up by a passerby, costs nobody a customer. The design bets everything on those proportions, sir: coordination dissolved into cheap cells, conflict discovered on encounter, urgency nowhere. Transplant it unmodified into a checkout page and the bet inverts. Transplant its *pattern* — the public court of record, the anointed primary, the passerby's right of completion — and it improves nearly any ledger it touches. Patterns travel, sir; temperaments do not.

## Part Seven — Calvin: Abolishing the Conversation

The third verb is *renegotiate*, and it has two very different practitioners. The first renegotiates the *machinery*: **Calvin**, Thomson and Abadi, twenty twelve, a design whose opening move is to ask why transactions quarrel at all. They quarrel — deadlock, block, abort, retry — because each node discovers a transaction's demands *during* execution, in whatever order the Post and the scheduler conspire to produce. Calvin's answer: remove the discovery. *Agree on inputs, not outcomes.* All transactions, before touching any datum, are submitted to a global sequencing layer — episode five's replicated log, in its starring industrial cameo three episodes early — which batches them and stamps a single, total, replicated order. Then every replica executes the same transactions in the same order under a deterministic scheduler: same inputs, same order, same code, therefore — the determinism diet of episode five, now the entire cuisine — same state everywhere, no coordination during execution at all. Two-phase commit evaporates, sir, not defeated but *mooted*: there is nothing to vote on. A distributed transaction in Calvin is not a negotiation among parties who might disagree; it is a scheduled performance of a score already published. The blocking theorem is not overturned — it is *starved*: no vetoes are surrendered mid-flight because there are no mid-flight decisions left.

The execution layer earns a sentence more, because deterministic hides the craft. Each replica's scheduler grants locks strictly in the published order — transaction number forty acquires before forty-one wherever the two touch, whatever threads the operating system happens to favour — so the interleaving is not merely repeatable but identical across replicas by construction; and deadlock dies of the same medicine, since a fixed acquisition order admits no cycles. Replication comes free and exact: ship the input log, not the effects — episode five's determinism diet, served as the entire cuisine. And the sequencing layer itself is sharded and batched — parliaments agreeing on batch boundaries rather than on each transaction singly — which is how a design in which every transaction passes through one log avoids making that log the estate's narrowest door.

The price is stated at the door, and the honesty is why this hall is in the tour. Determinism demands the read and write sets be knowable *before* sequencing — the score must list the instruments. A transaction that must read the database to learn what it will touch fits awkwardly: Calvin's remedy is a reconnaissance pass — run it once without commitment to discover its footprint, then submit the now-declared transaction to the sequencer, with a re-check in case the world moved meanwhile. And the *interactive transaction is surrendered entirely at the door*: the pattern beloved of every application programmer — begin; read; think in application code; write; think again; commit — cannot be scheduled in advance, because the *thinking* is not in the score. Calvin serves estates whose transactions arrive as complete stored procedures, and serves them magnificently: no distributed deadlock, no in-doubt windows, replication for free out of the sequencer. Estates that cannot pre-declare their business must shop in the other halls. It is the cleanest deal in the building, sir, and the narrowest. One aside for the finale to collect: *agree on inputs, then compute deterministically everywhere* is also, to the letter, how ten thousand accelerators will share one optimizer in the finale — Calvin is a database wearing the synchronous training loop's constitution, and neither field, so far as I can determine, has yet sent the other a thank-you note.

## Part Eight — Sagas: Honest Abandonment

The second renegotiator renegotiates the *promise itself*, and its papers are older than most of tonight: **sagas**, Garcia-Molina and Salem, nineteen eighty-seven, written for long-lived transactions that would hold locks for hours, and rediscovered wholesale by the microservices era, where every business process crosses a dozen services and no two-phase ceremony is on offer because no service will surrender its veto to another's coordinator. The saga's bargain, stated as its authors stated it, without cosmetics: break the long transaction into a chain of steps, each a *local* transaction, atomic in its own house — and for each step, write, in advance, a **compensating transaction** — the authors' own term, and the trade's ever since, shortened in conversation to a compensation: a second local transaction that undoes the step's business effect. Book the flight; the compensation cancels it. Charge the card; the compensation refunds it. Run the chain forward; on failure at any step, run the completed steps' compensations in reverse. Atomicity is thus re-created at the business level — eventually, all of it or none of it, in effect — but *isolation is abandoned*, and abandoned *honestly*: between a step and any compensation that may later erase it, the intermediate state is visible to the world. There is no photograph, no snapshot, no lock spanning the chain. The aunt may glimpse a booking that will never finally exist.

Stage the hazard, sir, because it is the two doctors' shabbier cousin and it walks through production systems daily. Bertie's travel saga books the flight — step one, committed, visible — and proceeds toward the hotel. The hotel refuses; the saga turns to compensate. But in the interval, another process *read the booked flight and acted*: awarded Bertie his loyalty points; emailed the aunt the itinerary; sold him, on the strength of his confirmed travel, cancellation insurance. The compensation cancels the flight — it cannot cancel the *reads*. The points ledger, the aunt's expectations, and the insurance book now rest on business that never happened: a dirty read of half-done business, made durable by third parties. A compensating transaction, note well, is not an undo, sir — the log is not rewound; a *new* transaction is appended that reverses the entry, and anything that observed the interim stands observed. Whence the discipline that separates adult saga engineering from abdication with paperwork: compensations designed *with* their steps, not discovered after the outage; interim states marked as provisional where outsiders might read them — a booking in state pending-saga is a fence, and cheap; steps that cannot be compensated — the email sent, the parcel shipped, the cash dispensed — deliberately ordered *last*, as pivots beyond which the saga only rolls forward; and the anomaly budget written down, in prose, where the product manager signs: *these* interim states may be seen, by *these* observers, and *this* is the cost when they are. When sir hears a team say "we use sagas" and cannot produce that page, the term of art is not architecture, sir; it is optimism with a vocabulary.

The original paper is more careful than most of its descendants, and two of its distinctions deserve rescue from the slide decks. Backward versus forward recovery: compensating backward is one lawful response to a failed step; the other is to press forward — retry the failed step until it succeeds, or substitute an equivalent, another hotel in the same city — and a well-built saga declares, per step, which direction it takes, because some business processes must not run backward once begun. And the pivot, formalized: order the chain so that everything before the pivot is compensable, everything after it is retriable to success, and the pivot itself is the single point of no return — the shape under which a saga has exactly one moment of commitment, a discipline the desk's design problem enforces. In Bertie's itinerary the pivot is the card charge: refundable bookings before it; the non-refundable ticketing and the aunt's email strictly after; so the saga can always retreat before the charge and always advance beyond it. To which the modern trade adds one operational schism worth naming: orchestration — a single conductor process driving the chain, the coordinator reborn at the business layer, with the same funeral to plan — versus choreography, each service reacting to the previous one's events, which distributes the conductor and thereby distributes the confusion. Either can be built well, sir; only one of them can be *read*, and outages are read at three in the morning.

And the triage that closes the halls, one breath per verb. The cheapest distributed transaction is the one your schema made unnecessary — co-locate what commits together; where things live is next week's entire business, in episode nine. Failing that: if your transactions can be declared, schedule them — Calvin's deal. If your invariants span shards and your purse is deep, buy the physics — Spanner's deal. If your steps are loosely coupled business anyway, compensate honestly — the saga's deal, page and all. And if you must hold the classical ceremony, hold it on parliaments, keep the windows short, and plan the officiant's funeral before the wedding. That sentence goes on the whiteboard, sir; let us proceed there now.

## Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, four items.

Item one. Two-phase commit blocks — by theorem, not by bug. Every participant that votes yes surrenders its veto and may be stranded, locks held, by a dark coordinator; the doppelgänger's two worlds are unanswerable. Plan the coordinator's funeral before the wedding: replicated coordinator state, cooperative termination, alarms on in-doubt age — and treat any middleware advertising heuristic abort-on-timeout after a yes vote as what it is, a split-ledger generator with a reassuring name. The in-doubt console is not clutter, sir; it is the theorem's embassy in your product — staff it.

Item two. Strict serializability across shards may be *purchased* — bounded clock uncertainty, confessed as an interval, paid for at commit with one uncertainty's worth of waiting; *scheduled* — determinism, inputs agreed before execution, the interactive transaction surrendered; or *renounced* — sagas with compensations. Renunciation is a legitimate engineering position exactly when the apology plan is in writing: every step's compensation designed with the step, pivots ordered last, interim visibility budgeted and signed.

Item three. The recovery matrix is the protocol. A commit ceremony is defined by what each party does on waking and on silence — the happy path is the trivial part. Ask of any transactional system: where is the court of record, who may read it, and who may *complete* a stranger's ceremony? Percolator's answer — the record public in the data, any passerby may finish — is the pattern to demand wherever a mortal client can faint mid-pronouncement.

Item four. Keep the two clauses in separate pockets: atomicity is whether the work becomes permanent; isolation is who may peek meanwhile. Sagas abandon isolation, not step-atomicity; snapshot stores relax isolation while committing atomically; and the phrase "distributed transactions" in a brochure tells you, by itself, which clause is meant — nothing. Ask for the anomaly, not the adjective, as last week taught; ask for the funeral plan, as this week does.

The reading. The Spanner paper — Corbett and colleagues, twenty twelve — the one industrial paper this season could least do without: read the TrueTime section twice, once for the API's honesty and once for commit-wait's arithmetic, and notice on every page the season's earlier episodes looking back at you: Paxos groups, leases, snapshots, locks. Garcia-Molina and Salem, nineteen eighty-seven, on sagas — short, clear, and decades ahead of the architecture that would need it; read the compensation discussion with your own outage postmortems open beside it. And on the shelf: Gray's nineteen seventy-eight notes, for the two-phase ceremony and the in-doubt state in Gray's own plain hand; Skeen's three-phase paper, as the museum label; the Percolator paper for the dissolved coordinator; Calvin for the determinist bargain; and the hybrid logical clocks paper for time on a civilian budget; and, for the connoisseur of foundations, Gray and Lamport's joint paper on consensus on transaction commit, in which the two traditions of this whole course — the ledger and the parliament — are formally wed by their respective founders, and the ceremony, fittingly, blocks nowhere a majority stands.

The desk awaits with chapter eight: atomic commitment specified formally beside consensus with the veto clause doing the separating; the two-phase protocol boxed with its full recovery matrix, wake-state by wake-state; the blocking theorem proved in the doppelgänger's two worlds, with the cooperative-termination refinement and its exact limit; commit-wait's external-consistency argument structured into a lemma you can carry; Percolator's column-ledger protocol boxed, primary anointment and passerby cleanup included; problems across the halls — recovery-matrix drills, who does what on waking; the sabotage, its bug proven fatal as ever: a participant fleet configured to abort on timeout after voting yes, its split outcome exhibited entry by entry; the travel saga's compensations designed and then convicted under interleaving; and, starred, the hybrid-logical-clock bound proof.
