The Parliament of Machines

An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Two: Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events

News from the Patent Office

Good day, sir.

In nineteen hundred and five, in the Swiss city of Bern, a young examiner at the federal patent office — a technical expert, third class, whose applications for university posts had not prospered — published a paper on the electrodynamics of moving bodies. Among its consequences was one that unsettled physics and, in due course, everybody else: simultaneity is not a fact about the world. Whether two distant events happen "at the same moment" is not something the universe has an opinion about; it depends on the observer. Two flashes of lightning that strike "simultaneously" for a passenger on the platform strike one-after-the-other for a passenger on the train, and — this is the part that takes a moment — neither of them is wrong. There is no master clock in the sky to appeal to. There is no universal now. The speed of light is finite, influence takes time to travel, and "at the same time, far apart" turns out to be a phrase from before we knew better.

Seventy-three years later, in nineteen seventy-eight, Leslie Lamport delivered precisely the same news to computing, in a paper published in the Communications of the ACM under the title "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System" — a paper that has since become one of the most cited our field possesses, and whose opening move is the one the patent clerk made: stop assuming a universal timeline, and see what honest structure remains. Lamport has been entirely candid about the lineage. The idea, he has written, came directly from special relativity: a message in a distributed system plays the role of a light signal, the fastest thing by which one place can influence another — and once you take that seriously, the question "which of these two events on two different machines happened first" dissolves in your hands exactly the way simultaneity dissolved in Bern.

Tonight, sir, we pay the third debt from last week's ledger — there is no shared clock — and we pay it in full, which is to say: we do not merely observe that the machines' watches disagree. That would be a complaint about engineering. The finding is worse and far more interesting: even with perfect watches, the question those watches were meant to answer does not, across machines, entirely exist.

The itinerary. First, the honest engineering complaint anyway — physical clocks and their sins, because you operate fleets of them and the sins are expensive and documented. Second, the dismantling: what "which happened first" can and cannot mean when the only carrier of influence is the Post. Third, the rebuilding: happens-before, the modest and unbreakable order that survives, with concurrency as its honest residue. Fourth and fifth, two beautiful instruments — the Lamport clock and the vector clock — that let machines carry causality around in their luggage. Sixth, a brief glimpse of a better-mannered Post. And seventh, the set piece: a small miracle of Chandy and Lamport's devising, in which we take a coherent photograph of a system that refuses, even for an instant, to hold still — and in doing so resolve the very paradox the episode opens. There is no now. There will, nonetheless, be a picture.

Part One — The Sins of Physical Clocks

Let us begin where the working engineer begins: with the clocks themselves, and the instinct that surely this is all just a synchronization problem — tighten the discipline, buy better hardware, and the timeline is restored. The instinct is wrong twice over, and I want to price the first wrongness properly before we reach the second, deeper one.

Inside nearly every machine you operate sits a sliver of quartz, vibrating. Its frequency is the machine's heartbeat, and it is not quite the frequency stamped on the tin. A perfectly ordinary crystal drifts by some tens of parts per million — take twenty parts per million as a round figure. It sounds like nothing. Do the arithmetic aloud: a day is eighty-six thousand four hundred seconds; twenty parts in a million of that is a little over a second and a half — call it a second and three quarters — per day. Left to itself, an ordinary server clock wanders by the better part of a minute a month. And the drift is not even constant: it breathes with temperature, which is to say with load, which is to say your clocks run measurably differently during the incident than they did before it — a detail with a certain poetic cruelty.

So we discipline them. The instrument is the network time protocol — NTP — a hierarchy of authority descending from atomic clocks and satellite receivers down through strata of servers to the machine under your desk. Its method is elegant and its central assumption is worth saying aloud, because it is a fallacy from last week wearing a lab coat: a client measures the round trip to a time server, assumes the outbound and return journeys took equal time, and splits the difference to estimate the offset. Symmetric paths. On real networks — congested one way, quiet the other, routed differently in each direction — the assumption fails silently, and the failure becomes a bias in your clock that no amount of polling repairs, because every poll makes the same assumption. Under good conditions, NTP holds machines within a millisecond or a few of each other on a local network, and within some tens of milliseconds across the wide area. The residue it never removes has a name worth carrying away, sir, because the literature will assume you have it: clock skew. Drift is what one clock does on its own; skew is what two clocks do to each other — the standing disagreement between any two watches in your fleet, and the quantity every cross-machine timestamp comparison silently rests upon. Under bad conditions — an overloaded server, a misconfigured upstream, an asymmetric route — it quietly holds them to nothing at all, while continuing to report success.

And there are the discrete indignities. When a clock is found to be wrong, the daemon must choose between slewing — running the clock slightly fast or slow until it converges, so that time never jumps — and stepping — yanking it to the correct value in one motion, so that time visibly lurches, and occasionally, horribly, moves backwards. Every piece of software that ever computed a duration by subtracting two wall-clock readings has an opinion about backwards time, and the opinion is usually an exception at three in the morning. Virtual machines add their own flourish: a paused guest wakes with its clock frozen in the past, then experiences the correction as a seizure.

Then there is the leap second — my favourite of the sins, because it is inflicted by international agreement. The Earth's rotation, being a large wet rock, does not keep atomic time; every so often the custodians of coordinated universal time — UTC — decree an extra second, inserted at midnight, so that the atomic clocks and the planet stay reconciled. The clocks of the world are thus asked to experience a minute containing sixty-one seconds, a concept for which a remarkable amount of software was not prepared. On the thirtieth of June, two thousand twelve, the scheduled leap second tripped a defect in the Linux kernel's timing machinery, and machines across the internet — perfectly healthy machines — sat down at midnight and spun. Prominent websites stumbled; most memorably, as the trade press documented at the time, an airline's check-in system went down with the leap second, and queues formed at counters on the far side of the world because the planet's rotation had been adjusted by one second. I invite you to savour the causal chain. The remedy that has since spread through the industry is one Google published openly: the leap smear — rather than inflict the extra second at a stroke, dilute it homeopathically across the whole day, running every clock imperceptibly slow so that no clock is ever surprised. Note what the remedy is, sir: it is choosing to make every clock slightly and deliberately wrong, in a coordinated fashion, so that none of them is ever suddenly wrong. Civilized mendacity. I confess a genuine admiration.

One footnote to the smear, offered as a specimen of how repairs breed fine print: smearing is a local decision. A fleet that smears and a fleet that does not — or two fleets smearing on different schedules — disagree with each other, by design, for the duration of the smear, and any cross-fleet timestamp comparison during that window inherits the disagreement. The cure for the leap second is a small, scheduled, planetary clock skew. It is the right trade; it is also this episode's thesis in miniature: among machines, even the corrections to time are relative.

One more sin, and this one you can prosecute in your own codebase this week, because it is less about the clocks than about which clock one consults. Every machine actually offers two. There is the time-of-day clock — the one NTP disciplines, the one that names the date, the one that can be slewed, stepped, smeared, and marched backwards by decree. And there is the monotonic clock — a humble stopwatch, counting since boot, promising nothing about what o'clock it is and everything about never running backwards. The rule of hygiene falls straight out: durations, timeouts, and intervals are the monotonic clock's business; the time-of-day clock is for naming moments to humans, and for nothing else. Measure an elapsed interval by subtracting two wall-clock readings and you have written a subtraction that is occasionally negative — and "occasionally" will choose its moment. I would wager, sir, that a respectable fraction of the industry's mystery timeouts trace to a duration computed across a clock step. And note the small, telling asymmetry in the bargain: the monotonic clock is trustworthy precisely because it has renounced the ambition of knowing what time it is. Keep the shape of that trade in view; the season is full of it.

Now collect the moral, in two layers. The engineering layer: wall clocks across machines agree to within milliseconds on a good day, and to within nothing in particular on a bad one — while a message between two machines in the same building takes a fraction of a millisecond. The skew between the clocks is larger than the events they would need to order. Sorting cross-machine events by wall-clock timestamp is therefore not a slightly noisy measurement; at fine grain it is noise wearing the costume of measurement. You have seen the costume, sir: every engineer who has collated logs from several machines by timestamp has, at some point, watched a reply apparently precede its request — an effect solemnly logged before its cause. The merged log was not revealing a miracle. It was committing perjury with digits.

And the deeper layer, which is tonight's real subject: suppose the engineering were solved. Suppose, by some extravagance, every clock in the fleet agreed to the nanosecond. You could then place every event on one timeline — and the timeline would still not answer the question you actually care about, which is never really "which happened first" but "could this have influenced that." Two events a nanosecond apart on opposite sides of a datacentre are ordered by your perfect clocks, and the order is causally meaningless: no message — no influence of any kind — could have crossed between them in that interval. The perfect timeline orders things whose order cannot matter, and it is silent about the only relation with consequences. Physics offered the patent clerk the same bargain, and he declined it. So shall we. To the dismantling, then.

Part Two — Dismantling the Question

Here is the question we are taking apart: event a happened on Bingo; event b happened on Gussie; which happened first?

The question presumes a master timeline — a universal ledger in which every event in the fleet has its row, and the rows have an order, and the order is a fact. Physics gave up this picture in nineteen oh five, and the reason translates into our vocabulary with almost embarrassing directness. In relativity, the fastest carrier of influence is light; events too far apart, too close in time, for light to travel from one to the other are causally sealed off from each other — and for exactly such pairs, observers in different states of motion genuinely disagree about which came first, with no fact of the matter to arbitrate. Order is real only where influence is possible. Where influence is impossible, order is a bookkeeping convention.

Before translating the physics into our vocabulary wholesale, let me stage the disagreement with materials from your own trade. Two services each keep a log. In Bingo's log, his cache refresh — at his own three minutes past eleven — precedes Gussie's write, which Bingo's monitoring scraped a moment later. In the collation run by the analytics pipeline, which trusts Gussie's timestamps — skewed those few milliseconds the other way — the write precedes the refresh. Two collations, two histories, both internally coherent, produced from the same events by nothing more sinister than a choice of whose clock to believe. Which is correct? If no message passed between the two events, the question has exactly the standing of the train passenger's quarrel with the platform: none. The logs are not lying, and they are not both right — the category of "the true order" simply does not extend to that pair of rows. The sooner a postmortem admits this, the sooner it stops reconstructing fictions with a straight face.

Translate. In a distributed system the fastest — indeed the only — carrier of influence between machines is the message. Not the wall clock: the clock carries no influence; it merely decorates events with numbers whose cross-machine meaning we have just priced. If no message, and no chain of messages, travels from the neighbourhood of event a to the neighbourhood of event b, then a cannot have influenced b — not "did not," sir: could not have — and any answer to "which came first" is convention, not physics. Different observers of the system — which is to say, different collations of its logs — may legitimately disagree, and there is nothing to appeal to. Lamport's move, the entire move, was to stop mourning the master timeline and ask instead: what order does the system actually possess? What survives?

The correspondence with the physics deserves one explicit paragraph, because Lamport drew it himself and it repays being held consciously rather than absorbed as mood. The light signal becomes the message: the fastest carrier of influence — in our world, the only one. The light cone, the region of space-time an event can reach, becomes the event's causal future: everything reachable from it by roads of letters. Spacelike separation — too far apart, too close in time, for light to connect — becomes concurrency: merely elsewhere. The observer, with his private simultaneity, becomes a log collation, with its private ordering of other machines' events. And the one thing all observers agree upon in relativity — the order of events connected by a signal — becomes the one thing all collations agree upon here: the order of events connected by messages. The analogy is not decoration, sir; it is load-bearing enough that a physicist and a distributed-systems theorist can, with a small dictionary, read one another's impossibility results. Ours is merely the cheaper laboratory.

Two things survive, and they are bedrock — the only bedrock there is.

First: within a single process, order is real. Bingo is one machine executing one sequence of steps; his own events are ordered as honestly as anything in this world. His diary is trustworthy about his own day.

Second: a message is received after it is sent. The letter cannot arrive before it was posted. Whatever the Post does — delay, reorder against other letters, lose the thing entirely — it does not deliver into the past.

That is the complete inventory. Everything else — every other ordering claim across machines — must be built from these two planks, or it is decoration. It seems a poverty. It is in fact a foundation, and the rest of tonight is the architecture.

Part Three — Before, After, and Elsewhere

We now define the order that the two planks support. Lamport called it happens-before, and I shall define it aloud in its three clauses, precisely, because it is the load-bearing definition of the season and possibly of the field.

Clause one: if a and b are events of the same process, and a comes earlier in that process's own sequence, then a happens before b.

Clause two: if a is the sending of a message and b is the receiving of that same message, then a happens before b.

Clause three: the relation chains. If a happens before b, and b happens before c, then a happens before c.

And that is all. Happens-before is the smallest relation containing those three clauses — nothing is before anything else unless these rules force it. Let me make it concrete with the company. Bingo performs some local step — call it his morning entry. He then writes a letter to Tuppy; Tuppy receives it, makes an entry of his own, and writes onward to Gussie; Gussie receives that. Now trace: Bingo's morning entry happens before his send, by clause one. His send happens before Tuppy's receipt, by clause two. Tuppy's receipt before Tuppy's entry, clause one; before Tuppy's send, clause one; before Gussie's receipt, clause two. And clause three chains the whole procession: Bingo's morning entry happens before Gussie's receipt, through two letters and three desks. Influence had a road. That is what the relation records: the roads.

Now the crucial vocabulary for everything the relation does not say. Suppose that while all this correspondence was in flight, Gussie — before his letter arrived — paused to make himself a cup of tea. Where does Gussie's tea stand with respect to Bingo's morning entry? Run the clauses: same process? No. Send and receive of one message? No. Any chain? None — no sequence of letters connects the entry to the tea in either direction. So the tea does not happen before the entry, and the entry does not happen before the tea. The two events are — and here is the season's phrase for it, to be used exactly and often — concurrent: not before, not after, merely elsewhere.

Attend to what concurrency means and does not mean, because the word is a standing trap. It does not mean "at the same time" — we have abolished "the same time"; that is the whole point. It does not mean the events were close together, or far apart, or anything about wall clocks at all. It means precisely this: no road of influence connects them, in either direction. They are causally sealed off — each outside the other's reach. And note the epistemic flavour, which will pay dividends all season: happens-before is potential causality, generously drawn. If a happens before b, then influence was possible — the road existed — whether or not anything of consequence travelled it; the relation can overstate influence, never understate it. But its negation is ironclad. If two events are concurrent, then no protocol, no cleverness, no forensic reconstruction can make one the cause of the other. When we say "concurrent," we are not confessing ignorance about the true order, sir. We are reporting that there is no true order to be ignorant of.

Drill it once more, because fluency here is fluency everywhere later. Bingo's send and Gussie's tea: concurrent, as established. Bingo's morning entry and Tuppy's entry: ordered — the road runs through the first letter. Tuppy's send to Gussie and Gussie's tea: run the clauses — no shared process, no connecting letter in either direction — concurrent, despite the fact that one of them would, on any wall clock you care to consult, have "happened first." The genealogical image serves well and we shall keep it: ordered events are ancestor and descendant; concurrent events are cousins — contemporaries of a sort, sharing ancestors perhaps, but neither descended from the other. The season will ask "ancestor or cousin?" a hundred times before it ends, and the answer will move money.

One more observation and we can start building instruments. Happens-before is a partial order. Some pairs are ordered; concurrent pairs simply are not; and the shape of the whole is not a timeline but a lattice of roads — a genealogy, if you like, where some events are ancestors of others and some are merely distant cousins. Engineers meet this partiality and itch to totalize it — to force every pair into an order, because timelines are comfortable and databases have sort keys. There are exactly two honest ways to do that, and the season will price both: manufacture an order arbitrarily but consistently, which costs you meaning — the next two parts build the machinery and post the warning signs; or rent an order from a distinguished authority through whom all events pass — which costs you a throne, and thrones must be defended, and that is episode four. What you may not do is read a total order off the wall clocks and call it causality. That is the perjury of part one, now with a theorem behind the indictment.

Part Four — The Lamport Clock

Having demolished the wall clock's claim to order events, Lamport's nineteen seventy-eight paper does something with the confidence of a conjuror: it builds a clock anyway — a logical clock, made of nothing but a counter, that captures exactly as much of "before" as actually exists, and not one drop more.

The mechanism first, because it is three rules and they fit in a waistcoat pocket. Each process keeps a single counter, starting at nought.

Rule one: every time a process performs an event — any event: a local step, a send, a receipt — it first ticks its counter up by one, and that new value is the event's timestamp.

Rule two: every letter carries, alongside its business, the sender's counter value at the moment of sending.

Rule three: upon receiving a letter, before ticking for the receipt itself, the recipient first raises his counter to the larger of his own value and the value carried by the letter. Then he ticks, and that is the receipt's timestamp.

Let us run it with the company, small numbers, entirely aloud. Bingo begins his day: morning entry — Bingo's counter reads one. He writes to Tuppy; the send is an event — counter two — and the letter carries the number two. Tuppy, meanwhile, has been idle; his counter reads nought. The letter arrives. Rule three: Tuppy raises his counter to the larger of nought and two — two — and then ticks for the receipt: Tuppy's counter reads three, and three is the receipt's timestamp. Tuppy makes an entry of his own: four. Writes onward to Gussie: five, and the letter carries five. Gussie — who has spent this entire time on his tea, one event, counter one — receives it: the larger of one and five is five, tick, six. Observe what rule three accomplished: the receipt's timestamp was forced above the send's, no matter how far behind the recipient's own counter had lagged. The letter did not merely carry news; it carried time, and dragged the recipient's clock forward to meet it.

Now the theorem — Lamport calls it the clock condition, and it is the modest, exact promise this instrument makes: if a happens before b, then a's timestamp is strictly less than b's. The order the system genuinely possesses is faithfully reflected in the numbers.

And the proof, which I shall now give you entire, because it is two sentences and I want you to notice it is two sentences. If a happens before b, then by the definition — the three clauses of part three — there is a chain of steps from a to b, each step being either two consecutive events on one process, or a send and its matching receipt. Along every step of that chain the timestamp strictly increases — same-process steps increase it because rule one ticks before every event, and message steps increase it because rule three lifts the recipient above the carried value before ticking — and a finite chain of strict increases ends strictly higher than it began. That is the whole proof, sir. Causality is a road; the counter climbs at every paving stone; therefore the end of any road stands higher than its start. When I promised you last week that this field's arguments fit the ear, this is the sort of thing I meant — though I concede I am also lulling you, since next week's proof is rather more than two sentences.

Now the trap — and I give it a full paragraph because it catches working engineers by the dozen, and because it is the precise point where this beautiful instrument, misread, becomes the wall-clock perjury all over again. The clock condition is a one-way street. If a happens before b, the timestamps are ordered. The converse — if the timestamps are ordered, then a happens before b — is false, and not subtly false. Recall the run we just performed: Gussie's tea bore timestamp one; Bingo's send bore timestamp two. One is less than two. Did the tea happen before the send? Run the clauses: no shared process, no letter, no chain — the tea and the send are concurrent, merely elsewhere, causally sealed. The numbers put them in an order; the world contains no such order. A Lamport timestamp comparison tells you either "b definitely did not happen before a" — that much the condition guarantees, in contrapositive — or it tells you nothing at all. An engineer who sorts events by Lamport time and reads the result as a causal history has manufactured exactly the fiction we spent part two demolishing, with better tooling. I have watched it done in postmortem documents, sir, by people who cite the paper.

So what is the manufactured order for? This is where Lamport's paper turns from diagnosis to architecture, and where it quietly plants a seed we shall harvest in three weeks. Take the Lamport timestamps and break the remaining ties — concurrent events with equal counters — by any fixed convention: alphabetical order of the gentlemen's names will do. The result is a total order on all events in the system: arbitrary where the world was silent, but consistent — consistent with every road that does exist, and, crucially, computable by everyone from local information, so every process that sees the same events arranges them identically. An order you manufactured, not one you discovered; the warning label stays glued on. But now the architecture: Lamport's paper puts the manufactured order straight to work on mutual exclusion — granting a shared resource to one claimant at a time, with no coordinator to do the granting. The scheme, in one breath: every request for the resource is broadcast to the whole company, stamped, and queued; every gentleman sorts his copy of the queue by the manufactured order; and a claimant helps himself precisely when his own request stands at the head of his own copy and he has heard something later-stamped from everyone else — the hearing-from-everyone being how he knows no earlier-stamped request can still be loitering in the Post. No lock server, no chairman: the order itself is the arbiter, and because every process computes the same total order from purely local information, all of them agree who stands at the head without ever discussing it. Feel the shape of that trick, sir — agreement achieved not by negotiation but by determinism applied to a shared order — because the paper then, almost as an aside, sketches its generalization: if several replicas of a deterministic machine apply the same commands in the same total order, they remain perfect copies of one another, forever. One paragraph, sir. The replicated state machine, tossed off in passing in nineteen seventy-eight, in the middle of a paper about clocks — a throwaway remark the industry would spend the next thirty years industrializing. Episode five collects that inheritance in full; when we get there, remember where you first heard it. And one landing on your own estate before we take up the richer instrument, because this course's standing promise is that theory arrives somewhere you already operate. You have been running Lamport clocks for years under assumed names. Every term in Raft, every ballot in Paxos, every epoch, generation, and fencing token you will meet from episode four onward is a Lamport clock in livery: a counter that only rises, carried on messages, merged by taking the maximum, whose entire purpose is to let a recipient say "this instruction is stale — I have seen a higher number" without consulting any wall clock whatsoever. When ZooKeeper stamps its transactions, when a freshly elected leader raises the epoch and the deposed leader's letters begin to bounce, that is rule three drawing a salary. The paper is dated nineteen seventy-eight. The payroll is current.

For tonight, the modest summary: the Lamport clock gives you a cheap, consistent, manufactured total order, plus a one-way guarantee about the real partial one. What it cannot do is answer questions about the real one — it cannot look at two timestamps and tell you "ordered" from "merely elsewhere." For that, one counter is simply not enough information. The repair is delightfully literal: carry more counters.

Part Five — The Vector Clock

Diagnose the Lamport clock's limitation precisely, because the repair falls straight out of the diagnosis. A single counter compresses an event's entire causal ancestry — everything it has heard of, through every chain of letters — into one number. Compression loses information. Two very different pasts can compress to comparable numbers, and then the numbers imply an order the pasts do not have. If we want the timestamps to characterize causality — to answer "before, after, or elsewhere" from the stamps alone — the stamp must summarize the past without losing it. The minimal honest summary turns out to be: one counter per colleague.

This is the vector clock, and its history has a pleasing symmetry: it was introduced independently, in the same year, nineteen eighty-eight, by Colin Fidge in Australia and Friedemann Mattern in Germany — the field discovering the same instrument twice, on opposite sides of the planet, presumably concurrently. Each process now keeps not one counter but a full roster — an entry for every member of the company — and I shall speak these rosters in the season's fixed template. At any moment, Bingo's clock reads: Bingo three, Tuppy one, Gussie nought — meaning Bingo has performed three events of his own, has heard, directly or through intermediaries, of Tuppy's first event, and knows nothing of Gussie's doings at all. An event's vector timestamp is a précis of its entire ancestry: for each colleague, how much of that colleague's diary this event could possibly have been influenced by.

The rules, mirroring the Lamport clock's, spoken plainly. Rule one: on any event, a process ticks its own entry by one. Rule two: every letter carries the sender's entire roster. Rule three: on receipt, the recipient first raises each entry of his roster to the larger of his value and the letter's value — entry by entry, the merge — and then ticks his own entry for the receipt.

Run it aloud, same story as before — and let me set the company out before anybody moves. Bingo will make a diary entry and write to Tuppy. Tuppy will receive that letter, make a diary entry of his own, and write onward to Gussie. Gussie, who has spent the whole afternoon on his tea, will receive that. Three rosters, three entries apiece, and I shall always speak them in the same order: Bingo, then Tuppy, then Gussie.

Bingo makes his morning entry, ticking his own entry in the roster — Bingo's clock reads: Bingo one, Tuppy nought, Gussie nought. He writes to Tuppy. The send is an event too, so he ticks again: Bingo two, Tuppy nought, Gussie nought — and the letter carries that whole roster.

The letter reaches Tuppy, whose roster until this moment read three noughts. He merges, entry by entry: the larger of his nought and the letter's two in Bingo's place, noughts elsewhere — Bingo two, Tuppy nought, Gussie nought. Then he ticks his own entry for the receipt: Tuppy's clock reads: Bingo two, Tuppy one, Gussie nought.

Tuppy now makes a diary entry of his own — another event, another tick of his own entry: Bingo two, Tuppy two, Gussie nought. He writes onward to Gussie — a third event, a third tick: Bingo two, Tuppy three, Gussie nought, and that roster travels with the letter.

And Gussie, whose whole afternoon amounted to the one event, the tea: his roster reads Bingo nought, Tuppy nought, Gussie one. The letter arrives. He merges, entry by entry — Bingo two, Tuppy three, Gussie one — and then ticks his own entry for the receipt: Gussie's clock reads: Bingo two, Tuppy three, Gussie two. Read that final roster as the précis it is: this receipt sits downstream of two of Bingo's events and three of Tuppy's, and of one prior event of Gussie's own. The ancestry, itemized.

Now the comparison rule, and then the theorem that makes this the episode's second instrument rather than a mere elaboration. Say one roster is below another when every entry is less than or equal, and at least one entry is strictly less. So Bingo two, Tuppy nought, Gussie nought sits below Bingo two, Tuppy three, Gussie two. But compare Gussie's tea — Gussie one, and noughts — against Bingo's send — Bingo two, and noughts: the tea is ahead on Gussie's entry, the send is ahead on Bingo's. Neither below the other. Incomparable.

The theorem — and it is theorem two of this chapter at the desk, stated with the respect an if-and-only-if deserves: a happens before b precisely when a's vector sits below b's. Both directions, this time. Ordered vectors mean ordered events; incomparable vectors mean concurrent events. The stamps no longer merely respect causality — they encode it, completely. Two events, two rosters, and you can pronounce "before," "after," or "merely elsewhere" with certainty, no further evidence required.

The proof strategy, aloud, both directions — the full argument is on paper tonight. The forward direction is the Lamport argument run entrywise: along any road from a to b, no entry ever decreases — merges only raise, ticks only raise — and the traveller's own ticks ensure at least one entry strictly grows; roads lift rosters. The converse is where the extra information earns its keep, and the argument has a lovely epistemic shape. Suppose a's roster sits below b's, and say a is one of Bingo's events — his fifth, so a's roster reads five in Bingo's entry, since Bingo counts his own diary exactly. Then b's roster reads at least five in Bingo's entry. But here is the crux: how does anyone besides Bingo come to hold a number that large in Bingo's entry? Only by merge — and merges happen only at receipts. A number in Bingo's entry is testimony, and testimony travels only by post. Trace the testimony backwards, letter by letter: each merge that raised the entry points to a sender who held the number earlier, and the trail terminates only at the source — at Bingo himself, at or after his fifth event. The trail is a road from a to b. Hence a happens before b. In one sentence: you cannot know of my fifth act unless a chain of letters connects my fifth act to you — and the vector is precisely a ledger of what you know. Concurrency, in this light, acquires its final and best characterization: two events are concurrent exactly when each is ignorant of some part of the other's past. Mutual, certified, provable ignorance. That is what "merely elsewhere" cashes out to.

Before the invoice, let me show the instrument doing the one job that will matter most in the weeks ahead: detecting concurrency in anger. Cast your mind back to last week's third performance — Bertie's two delivery notes, "leave with the porter" and then "ring the top bell," racing through the Post, arriving at Gussie in the wrong order, and Gussie ending the day quietly disagreeing with his colleagues. Replay it with rosters. Bertie's first instruction enters at Bingo, who applies it and forwards it stamped — say — Bingo four, Tuppy two, Gussie one. The correction enters at Bingo a moment later and leaves stamped Bingo five, Tuppy two, Gussie one. Compare the stamps: four, two, one sits below five, two, one. Ordered — the correction demonstrably saw the original; it is a true successor, and every replica, in whatever order the Post delivers the pair, can apply the elder first or discard it outright: the stamp, not the arrival time, carries the truth, and Gussie's little tragedy from last week becomes impossible. But now run the darker variant, and mind where each note lands, because the landing is the whole difference. Bertie, impatient with Bingo's silence, does not send both notes to the same gentleman. The porter note goes to Bingo. The bell note goes directly to Gussie. Bingo receives the porter note and stamps it, in due course, Bingo four, Tuppy two, Gussie one. Gussie, meanwhile, has heard little from Bingo lately, so his own roster is well behind on Bingo's entry; he receives the bell note and stamps it Bingo two, Tuppy two, Gussie three. Now compare the two stamps, entry by entry. The porter note stands ahead on Bingo's entry — four against two. The bell note stands ahead on Gussie's — three against one. Neither sits below the other. Incomparable. Concurrent. And now the system knows something last week's system could not even express: these two instructions are not an update and its correction — they are rivals, written in mutual ignorance, and no protocol has any business silently pretending that one superseded the other. What one ought to do with certified rivals — keep both and ask Bertie; merge them under some lawful rule — is a genuine question with a genuine menu, and it belongs to episodes six and seven; the shopping cart obliged to hold two kettles at once is exactly this moment at industrial scale. Tonight's point is smaller and sharper: the vector clock converts "the replicas mysteriously disagree" into "the replicas hold two provably concurrent writes." From a haunting into a diagnosis. Diagnoses have treatments, sir. Hauntings merely have postmortems.

The costs, stated honestly, because this instrument is priced by the roster. Every letter now carries an entry for every member of the company — the freight grows with the fleet, and a fleet of thousands makes the stamps heavier than many payloads. Worse, the roster presumes you know who the company is: membership — who is in the vector — is itself a distributed agreement problem, which should by now give you a familiar prickle at the back of the neck; we shall meet it properly when we meet agreement properly. Engineering has answers — prune entries, gossip compactly, and a refinement called the dotted version vector, which I name tonight and deliberately leave wrapped, because it belongs to episode six, where a shopping cart will need to hold two kettles at once and ask which one Bertie truly meant. You might hope, of course, to compress the roster — surely some cleverness shrinks a company of a thousand down to a stamp of civilized size while still answering "ancestor or cousin" exactly. The hope is natural, and it is vain: a starred problem at tonight's desk walks the ambitious reader to the theorem that the vector's size is not an implementation's clumsiness but the problem's true dimension — characterizing causality in a company genuinely requires one coordinate per member. What the industry actually does, therefore, is not compress the truth but ration it: track fewer participants — versions per key rather than per node; prune the retired; or retreat to the one-number clock and accept its one-way honesty. Every deployed scheme you will meet is one of those three retreats, priced differently.

For tonight, the honest summary of the two instruments side by side: the Lamport clock is one number — cheap, total after tiebreaking, manufactured, one-way. The vector clock is a roster — costly, partial, truthful, two-way. One gives you an order to act on; the other gives you the facts of the matter. Mature systems, we shall find, tend to carry both and confuse neither.

Part Six — A Glimpse of Disciplined Delivery

Before the set piece, a short corridor, glimpsed and deliberately not entered.

The Post, you recall, reorders. And now that we own the vocabulary, we can say precisely which reorderings are rude: the Post may show you an event before showing you that event's causes. Tuppy asks a question of the room; Gussie's answer, racing through a luckier route, reaches Bingo before the question does — and Bingo beholds a reply to nothing, an effect arriving in advance of its cause. Nothing in last week's charter forbids it. But notice: with vector clocks, Bingo can detect the rudeness. The answer's roster confesses its ancestry — it sits downstream of a Tuppy event Bingo has not yet seen — and so Bingo can simply set the letter aside, unopened, until its prerequisites arrive. Hold the mail until its past has been delivered. Do this systematically and you have built causal delivery: a Post that never shows anyone an effect before its cause — still free to shuffle concurrent letters, because merely-elsewhere events have no order to violate, but forbidden to invert the roads.

That one idea — buffer until the ancestry is present — is a genuine consistency contract, purchasable at known cost, and it sits at a very particular altitude: stronger than the Post's anarchy, far cheaper than a total order, and exactly aligned with what the roads actually require. But contracts belong in a catalogue, alongside their prices and their fine print, and the catalogue is episode seven — where causal consistency takes its place in a hierarchy, and where its practical children, the session guarantees, turn out to be things your systems already half-promise under folk names like read-your-own-writes. Tonight I merely wanted you to see the corridor exists, and to notice that it was the vector clock that unlocked it. Noted in the ledger; onward. The photograph awaits.

Part Seven — The Photograph

Now the set piece, and let me build the problem before I produce the miracle, because the problem is the episode's own paradox wearing a business suit.

You operate a system — say, for tonight, a small bank. You would like to know its global state: every account balance, everywhere, at once. The reasons are not exotic; they are Tuesday. A backup — a checkpoint from which the world could be restored. An audit — does the money add up. A detector — is the system deadlocked, is a computation finished, has an invariant quietly broken. Every one of these questions has the same grammar: what is the state of the whole system, now? And we have spent this entire hour executing "now." There is no universal instant to photograph. Machines cannot even agree what time it is, and if they could, the agreement would be causally meaningless. The question seems not merely hard but ill-posed — and yet the backup must be taken. Industry, as usual, cannot wait for the philosophy to resolve.

Let me first show you what goes wrong when one photographs naively, with the company. Bingo keeps Bertie's current account: sixty pounds. Tuppy keeps his savings: forty. One hundred pounds in the world — that is the invariant an audit should confirm. Now Bertie, ever active, moves ten pounds from current to savings: Bingo debits ten — his ledger reads fifty — and posts the ten pounds to Tuppy in a letter. While that letter is in the Post, the auditor telephones. He calls Bingo first: fifty. He calls Tuppy a moment later — the letter has still not arrived: forty. Total: ninety pounds. Ten pounds have vanished from the audit. Or run the interleaving the other way: the auditor calls Tuppy just after the letter arrives — fifty — but had called Bingo just before the debit — sixty. Total: one hundred and ten. The audit has minted ten pounds. Notice the precise shape of the second failure, because it is the shape to remember: the photograph included a letter's arrival — Tuppy credited — without including its departure — Bingo not yet debited. An effect without its cause. The audit assembled a picture of the world containing a consequence whose origin it denies, and such a picture corresponds to no state the system was ever in, could ever have been in. Money conjured by bad photography.

So let us say what a good photograph would even mean, since "the state at one instant" is off the table. Picture the space-time diagram of the whole bank — every gentleman's timeline, every letter an arrow between them — and picture drawing a single frontier across it: a cut, separating each timeline into a past and a future. The photograph records each process as of its own frontier-crossing, and each channel's in-flight letters. Call the cut consistent if it commits no effect-without-cause: no letter received in the past of the frontier was sent in the future of it. Arrows may cross the frontier forwards — a letter sent in the past, still undelivered: that is simply money in flight, and an honest photograph records it as such — but no arrow crosses backwards. Here, then, is the redefinition that rescues the ill-posed question, and it is pure Lamport in spirit: we abandon "the state at an instant," which does not exist, and pursue "the state along a consistent cut," which does. The frontier need not be straight; it need not correspond to any single wall-clock moment; different processes may be photographed at wildly different local times. It need only respect the roads.

Very well — but how? The gentlemen cannot see the diagram; they live inside it. Each knows only his own ledger and his own mail. And they cannot stop: a bank that pauses the world to take its picture has solved the problem by not having a system. The photograph must be taken live, from within, by the participants, while the letters keep flying.

Enter, in nineteen eighty-five, in the ACM's Transactions on Computer Systems, K. Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport — the same Lamport, seven years on, now finishing the thought — with an algorithm of such economy that once you have seen it work you will suspect it of hiding something. It hides nothing. And before we open it, let me give the thing its proper name, since "the photograph" is my image and not the field's: what we are about to take is a distributed snapshot, and the procedure for taking it is known everywhere as the Chandy–Lamport algorithm. Snapshot is the word to carry into company and to search the literature by; the photograph is merely how I shall keep it in your ear. The entire mechanism is one special letter, called the marker, and two rules. One honest disclosure first, per the standing terms of this course: the algorithm requires a better Post than our default — for this chapter, channels must be reliable and first-in-first-out: letters on a given route arrive, and arrive in the order posted. A genuine strengthening of the model, purchased and declared — last week's triage, practised — and you will see in a moment the exact instant where the first-in-first-out clause earns its entire keep.

The rules, as correspondence. Any gentleman may initiate the audit. Rule one — the recording rule: the initiator records his own ledger, at once, and immediately — before conducting one further scrap of business — posts a marker on every outgoing channel. Rule two — the marker rule: any gentleman receiving his first marker does the same at once: records his ledger, notes the channel that marker arrived on as having empty in-flight state, and posts markers on all his outgoing channels. And for every other incoming channel, he opens a docket: every ordinary letter arriving on that channel after his own recording, but before that channel's marker, goes into the docket — and when the marker arrives on that channel, the docket closes, and its contents are that channel's recorded state. Two kinds of thing go into a snapshot, sir, and no third: the process state, which each gentleman copies out of his own ledger, and the channel state — the letters in flight along a given route — which is precisely what a closed docket holds. The marker, you see, is a sweep — a line drawn down each channel, saying: everything ahead of me on this route belongs to the past of the photograph; everything behind me, to its future.

Now the performance, with Bertie's ten pounds in flight, so we may watch the money get counted exactly once. The state of play: Bingo has debited — his ledger reads fifty — and the ten-pound letter is in the Post toward Tuppy, whose ledger reads forty. At this delicate moment, Tuppy takes the chair and initiates the audit. Tuppy records his ledger: forty. Tuppy writes to Bingo, immediately: marker. And Tuppy opens a docket on his incoming channel from Bingo. Two letters are now converging on the participants: the ten pounds, ambling toward Tuppy; the marker, hurrying toward Bingo. The ten pounds arrives at Tuppy first. Tuppy has already recorded, so the tenner does not touch his recorded forty — that figure is fixed, whatever else befalls him tonight. He credits his live ledger, which accordingly reads fifty. And the marker for that channel has not yet arrived, so into the docket the tenner goes as well. Ten pounds, noted as in flight.

Then Tuppy's marker reaches Bingo, and it is the first marker Bingo has seen. He does the three things rule two requires, in order. He records his ledger — fifty, the balance the debit left him. He notes the channel from Tuppy as empty. And he posts a marker back to Tuppy. That marker travels the same route as the tenner did — and here, sir, is first-in-first-out collecting its salary: posted after the tenner, it cannot overtake it; the sweep arrives behind the money, never ahead of it. It reaches Tuppy; Tuppy's docket for that channel closes, containing exactly one item. The audit assembles: Tuppy recorded, forty. Bingo recorded, fifty. In flight from Bingo to Tuppy, per the docket: ten. One hundred pounds. Conserved to the penny — while the money was moving, while both ledgers were changing, without one instant of pause, without one glance at a wall clock.

And with the full company? The two-gentleman audit shows the mechanism; the general protocol is the same sentence pronounced louder. Markers go out on every outgoing channel — had Gussie been holding Bertie's deposit box as well, Tuppy's initiation would have posted him a marker too; Gussie, on his first marker, records his own ledger, returns markers to both colleagues, and opens dockets on each remaining incoming channel, closing each as that channel's own marker arrives. Every channel is swept exactly once; every gentleman records exactly once; the audit is complete when every docket in the company has closed. The bill for the elegance is easy to read off the mechanism: one marker per channel, so a fully connected company of n gentlemen pays on the order of n squared markers per snapshot — trifling for three, worth budgeting for three thousand, and one reason the industrial costume this algorithm eventually wears sends its markers down a pipeline rather than around a clique. The photograph scales exactly as well as the postal map allows.

Why does it work — not for this run, but always? The strategy, aloud; the full proof is theorem four of tonight's chapter, and it is a proper one. Call an event pre-snapshot if it occurs at a process before that process records, post-snapshot otherwise; the photograph's claim is that it captures precisely the frontier between the two. The single fact doing all the labour is this: a letter posted post-snapshot can never be received pre-snapshot. Why? Its sender recorded before posting it — and by rule one, recording is followed immediately by markers, so the sender's marker entered that channel ahead of the letter; first-in-first-out keeps it ahead; therefore the marker reaches the recipient first, and the marker's arrival — by rule two — forces the recipient to record on the spot, if he somehow had not already. By the time the tardy letter arrives, its recipient is post-snapshot. No backwards arrow can cross the frontier: the cut is consistent — no effect without its cause, no minted money, ever. And the dockets are exactly right by the same sweep: a docket collects what arrives after the recipient recorded but ahead of the channel's marker — which is precisely the mail posted pre-snapshot and received post-snapshot: the letters genuinely in flight across the frontier. Sent-and-not-received, itemized per channel. The books must balance, because the frontier leaks nothing in either direction.

Two practical notes before the philosophy, so the instrument leaves here usable. First: what is a photograph for? The classic answer — the one Chandy and Lamport built the paper around — is the detection of stable properties: conditions which, once true, stay true. A deadlock. A terminated computation. A permanently broken invariant. Here the plurality of presents turns from liability into licence. The photograph, we shall prove, depicts a state the system could genuinely have occupied, situated between the audit's beginning and its end. So if a stable property holds in the photograph, it holds in truth — it held in a reachable state, and stability carries it forward into the real present. And if it fails in the photograph, then it had not yet occurred when the audit began. The picture may be of a moment that never was; yet for every question whose answer cannot un-happen, it is a truthful witness. For unstable properties — "was the queue ever exactly seven deep" — it is, and honesty requires the label, no witness at all. Second: the mechanics of collection. Recording is entirely local — each gentleman ends the protocol holding his own ledger copy and his dockets — so the fragments must still be gathered, by ordinary post, to whoever wants the album; and the gathering may be as leisurely as you please, because recorded fragments do not age. Nor need there be one photographer: several gentlemen may initiate audits independently — markers carry an audit's identifier, dockets are kept per audit, and the runs interleave without quarrel. And throughout all of it the letters keep flying; at no point did anyone ask the bank to close. The pause we could not afford turns out to be a pause nobody needed.

And now the coda, which is the deepest thing tonight and the episode's designated moment of wonder, so allow me a held breath. Ask the awkward question: the state the audit assembled — Tuppy forty, Bingo fifty, ten in the Post — was the bank ever actually in that state? Scan the true history: no. At no actual moment did that exact configuration obtain; the pieces were recorded at different times, mid-flow. The photograph depicts an instant that never happened. And here is the extraordinary part, proved on paper tonight by an argument you already half-own: it is nonetheless a legitimate instant. Take the true history and notice that concurrent events — merely-elsewhere events — can be swapped in the telling without changing anything anyone experienced; no observer inside the system can distinguish the retellings, for the retellings differ only where no road runs. Theorem four shows the recorded state is what you reach by one such innocent reshuffling: herd every pre-snapshot event before every post-snapshot one — the consistency of the cut is exactly what makes the herding possible — and the photograph sits precisely at the boundary, a state the system could have passed through, from which the true future does follow. In a world with a master clock, we would call such a photograph a falsehood. But there is no master clock — that is the whole hour — and so the honest accounting is this: the system's history was never one sequence of instants; it was a lattice of roads, and every consistent cut through that lattice is as much a "now" as any other. The algorithm did not fake a present, sir. It selected one — legitimately, from the plurality of presents the mathematics actually offers — and no one inside the system could ever testify against it. There is no the now. There are nows, plural; and Chandy and Lamport taught the machines to develop a photograph of one of their own choosing, mid-flight, with a single roll of markers. I have admired a great many algorithms in my service, and I permit myself, once per episode, to say so: this one is a small piece of philosophy that compiles.

One receipt to file before we close, sir, so the ledger stays honest: this photograph — this exact algorithm — is not a museum piece. It is running tonight, under an assumed name and industrial tailoring, inside stream-processing machinery you personally operate; its markers travel your pipelines at this hour. I shall say no more, because the unmasking is scheduled — episode ten, where it will be performed with full ceremony — and a debt entered now makes the reveal sweeter. Into the book it goes.

Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, then — the standing two or three minutes of what tonight obliges a designer to carry into a review. Four items.

Item one. Never order cross-node events by wall clock. A timestamp comparison across machines is an opinion wearing digits, and at message granularity it is not even a well-informed opinion. If a design or a runbook sorts, joins, or adjudicates by cross-machine timestamps — last-write-wins by clock, log-merge forensics, expiry decided by somebody else's watch — demand the uncertainty bound in writing. If no bound can be produced, what you are holding is folklore with a schema. Later in the season you will see what it costs when a company actually goes and buys the bound; the price will clarify why nobody gets it for free.

Item two. Two clocks, two jobs, never crossed. Durations, timeouts, and intervals belong to the monotonic clock; naming moments for humans belongs to the time-of-day clock. Any duration computed by subtracting wall-clock readings is a latent incident with a date of its own choosing.

Item three. If concurrent writes can happen, choose your regime on purpose: carry causality explicitly — vector clocks, version vectors — so that rivalry is detectable and adjudicable; or rent a total order from a leader so that rivalry is impossible by construction. Either is defensible. Drifting between the two, which is the industry's default posture, is precisely where the lost updates live.

Item four. A snapshot is not a pause. Any design that requires stopping the world to obtain a consistent view is paying for a theorem nobody proved; markers deliver consistency in motion. Corollary for the estate you already operate: interrogate your checkpoint and backup tooling about what its "consistent" actually means — a consistent cut, or a superstition with a cron schedule.

The reading, assigned in the standing manner. First, tonight's text itself: Lamport, "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System," Communications of the ACM, nineteen seventy-eight. It is short, it is readable, and it is one of the most cited documents our field has produced; when the field established a prize for its most influential work, in the year two thousand, this paper was the inaugural selection — the honour has since taken Dijkstra's name. Read it whole; and when you reach the small paragraph about state machines, pause and smile, because you will be holding an acorn, and we have a standing appointment with the oak. Second: Chandy and Lamport, "Distributed Snapshots: Determining Global States of Distributed Systems," ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, nineteen eighty-five — brief, complete, and quietly perfect; as you read, mark the exact sentence where first-in-first-out is spent, and notice that it is spent nowhere else. And the standing companion as ever: Kleppmann, chapter eight — his pages on unreliable clocks pair with tonight's first part like consommé with dry sherry.

The desk now awaits with chapter two. Happens-before made formal; theorem one, the clock condition, with the converse's failure exhibited as a trace you can point to; theorem two, the vector characterization, both directions proved in full; theorem four, the photograph's correctness — including the shuffling argument that makes a present-that-never-was legitimate, which you will half-build yourself, since one of the problems has you prove the very swapping lemma the theorem leans on. Protocol boxes for both clocks and for the marker algorithm, checked against the papers. And the problems in three tiers, including the season's second sabotage: the thrifty auditor — a snapshot variant whose small economy with markers mints money on demand. Per the standing rules, I certify the bug fatal; the minting execution is written out in the solutions, ten counterfeit pounds and all. One starred door for the ambitious: a proof that the roster cannot be compressed — that characterizing causality among a company of a given size truly requires one entry per member, no cleverness permitted.

And the ledger — where tonight, sir, we hold a small ceremony: the first debt of the season moves from the left column to the right. "No shared clock," issued in episode one, is paid — and note the currency it was paid in: not a synchronization protocol, which would have been paying a falsehood with a gadget, but a replacement theory of order, under which the clock's absence stopped being a defect and became a fact with structure. Four fresh entries are filed tonight, each due later in the season: physical clocks will return, armed and expensive, a company purchasing better physics and tendering the apology in hardware; the photograph will return in industrial costume; a total order may be rented from a leader — the throne, and the defence of the throne; and causal delivery, glimpsed tonight through a doorway, will become a purchasable contract, with a price tag attached.

Next week, then: the summit of the impossible. April, nineteen eighty-five, the Journal of the ACM. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson publish a proof scarcely longer than a dinner menu, and a decade of protocol tinkering simply ends — fault-tolerant consensus in the asynchronous world is shown to be not difficult, not expensive, but unachievable, full stop. The adversary we have been calling the Post receives its formal commission as the scheduler; hesitation is revealed as a weapon of war; and the proof is conducted properly — all twelve to fifteen minutes of it, as ruled and budgeted — after which we tour the loopholes, every one of them, through which the entire practising industry makes its escape. Bring the doppelgänger, sir. It has the leading role.

Until then, sir.
