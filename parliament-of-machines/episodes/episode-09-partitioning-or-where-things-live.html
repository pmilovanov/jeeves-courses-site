The Parliament of Machines

An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Nine: Partitioning, or Where Things Live

The Resignation Letter

Good day, sir.

Permit me to describe the day a cache fleet dies of arithmetic. The estate runs ten cache servers, and every key in the business is assigned by the oldest rule in the book: hash the key, divide by the number of servers, take the remainder, and the remainder names the machine. Key to server, in one line of code; it has worked, wordlessly, for two years. Traffic grows, as traffic does; the operators, prudent souls, purchase an eleventh server and add it to the fleet at four in the afternoon, ahead of the evening peak, congratulating themselves on their foresight.

Consider, sir, what the arithmetic does at the moment the divisor changes from ten to eleven. The remainder of nearly every hash changes with it — not some remainders, not the unlucky tenth, but roughly ten in every eleven of them. A key that lived on the third machine now belongs, by law, to the seventh; the seventh's tenants are scattered to the first and the ninth; the entire address book of the estate is reshuffled in a single stroke. And a cache whose address book has been reshuffled is a cache in name only: the evening's first request for nearly every key goes to a machine that has never heard of it, misses, and falls through to the database beneath — which was sized, of course, on the assumption that the cache would absorb nine parts in ten of the load. The fleet did not fail, sir. Every machine is healthy. The arithmetic failed: at four in the afternoon the operators, meaning only to add capacity, sent the database the entire internet at once. Hash modulo N is a resignation letter one writes to oneself in advance, postdated to the day the fleet changes size — and every estate that has used it has eventually had the letter delivered.

The morning after such an evening has a fixed liturgy, sir; I have read the postmortems and they might share an author. The graphs show the cache hit rate falling off a cliff at four, the database latency rising like a startled pheasant at one minute past, the queues backing into the application tier by ten past, and the on-call engineer — who will spend the night rediscovering everything this episode contains — restarting healthy machines one by one, because restarting healthy machines is what one does at midnight when arithmetic is the culprit and arithmetic does not appear in the process list. The fix deployed at dawn is always the same fix: never again shall the address of a key depend on a number that changes.

The repair had in fact been published in advance, in nineteen ninety-seven, by David Karger and colleagues at MIT, in a paper written for a problem that barely existed yet — cooperating web caches — under a name that gives away nothing: consistent hashing. The paper made two promises that sounded, at the time, almost too modest to matter: that each server would hold about its fair share of the keys, and that when a server joined or left, the number of keys that moved would be about one over n — one eleventh, for our fleet of eleven — rather than ten elevenths. Add a server, move almost nothing. The authors and their students founded a company on the idea, called Akamai, which quietly became a load-bearing wall of the web; and the technique escaped the cache trade entirely, into storage, into databases, into episode six's Dynamo school, until today there is scarcely a large system in which the clock face we shall draw tonight is not spinning somewhere in the basement. Tonight, sir, is about where things live — the question last week left standing, when an entire evening of transactions assumed the shards were simply there. Placement is a decision; somebody, or something, must make it; and the quality of that decision governs every fan-out, every rebalance, and every celebrity-induced outage in the estate. I will also confess the evening's temperament in advance: after two weeks of impossibility theorems and fainting officiants, tonight is nearly all good news and elementary arithmetic — the one branch of this course where the mathematics is coin-flipping, the proofs fit in the ear, and I flag the territory, gladly, as friendly. Enjoy it, sir; episode eleven is about failure. The itinerary: why shard at all; the two great placement schemes and their characteristic pathologies; the clock face built aloud, with the movement argument done entire in the ear — the set piece; the elegant sibling that needs no ring; the academic apotheosis and the road the industry actually took; the two churches of the shard map; rebalancing without storms; routing; and the hot-key survival kit. The whiteboard at the close is, for once, mostly economics.

Part One — Why Shard At All

Begin with the question a disciplined engineer asks before any ring is drawn: must we? The alternative to partitioning is the bigger machine, and the bigger machine is a genuinely excellent answer for longer than fashion admits — memory and cores have grown faster than most businesses, a single well-provisioned server dispatches astonishing volumes, and every theorem of this course is trivially satisfied by an estate of one. The ceiling is real, however, and it is triple. There is the ceiling of physics: past some size, the machine you want is not sold, and the machine below it is priced as jewellery — cost per unit of capacity bends viciously upward at the top of the catalogue, which is why the estates that define this field were built by companies whose economics demanded warehouses of commodity boxes rather than one cathedral mainframe; the theorems of this course are, among other things, the price of that procurement decision. There is the ceiling of blast radius: one machine is one failure domain, and episode one's documentary evidence applies to it in full — the mainframe's uptime is magnificent until the day of the firmware update, the flooded machine room, the fat-fingered migration, whereupon the business discovers that availability was never about the mean but about the worst Tuesday; the bigger the single basket, the more instructive the day it drops. And there is the ceiling of geography: a single machine is somewhere, and the speed of light bills every client who is elsewhere — episode two's constant, unrepealed, unappealable, and utterly indifferent to the size of the machine. When the business outgrows any of the three, the data must be cut into partitions — shards, tablets, ranges, virtual nodes; the trade has a dozen words and one idea — each shard small enough to live on a machine, move between machines, and fail without taking the estate with it.

And once the cut exists, notice how much of the estate's grammar reorganizes around it, because this is why the decision is architectural rather than clerical. The shard becomes the unit of everything: the unit of placement — the map speaks in shards, not keys; the unit of movement — rebalancing ships shards whole, never grains; the unit of recovery — a dead machine's obligations are enumerated as the shards it held, each re-homed independently, which is precisely what makes the fleet's recovery parallel where the single great machine's was serial; the unit of throttling, of quota, of billing, of blame. Choose the shards too large and every one of those operations becomes a lumbering indivisible; too small and the map itself becomes the estate's biggest table. The trade's instinct — shards of modest, movable size, thousands of them per fleet, many per machine — is not fashion, sir: it is the observation that every operational verb in the paragraph above conjugates more gracefully in the plural.

And here a marriage must be formalized, because the course has been living with the couple unwed for three weeks. Partitioning and replication are different axes of the same placement decision. Replication — episode six's business — makes copies of data for durability and availability: the same shard, several machines. Partitioning cuts the data into different pieces for capacity: different shards, different machines. Every serious estate does both at once: the unit of placement is the shard, each shard is replicated as a group — very commonly, after episodes four and five, as a parliament with its own little log — and the estate is a matrix: shards down one side, replicas across the top. Picture it with the company, since the picture recurs from here to the finale: shard seven's parliament is Bingo, Tuppy, and Gussie, with Bingo on the throne; shard eight's is Tuppy, Gussie, and Barmy, with Gussie presiding; each machine hosts replicas of many shards, leads some, follows others, and the placement scheme's real output is this whole seating chart — including the quiet constraint that no shard's three members share a rack, a switch, or a storm, because replicas that share a failure domain are episode one's lesson learned a second time, at full price. The walk-clockwise rule of tonight's ring, in industrial dress, therefore reads: the next distinct machine in a distinct failure domain — placement answering to physics as well as arithmetic. Keep the axes distinct in the design review, sir, because they answer different questions — replication answers what survives, partitioning answers what fits and where — and because the pathologies of tonight are all partitioning's own: no amount of replication rescues a scheme that put all the popular keys in one place. Last week names tonight's business precisely: every protocol of episode eight assumed somebody had already decided that Bertie's account lives at Tuppy's branch — and observe how much hung on the unexamined decision: co-locate the two accounts and the transfer is one shard's local transaction, no officiant, no vows, no theorem; separate them and the entire fainting-clergy apparatus deploys. The cheapest distributed transaction, last week's triage said, is the one your schema made unnecessary — and the schema's power to make it unnecessary is exactly tonight's subject. Tonight, somebody decides.

One more framing before the schemes, and it is the whiteboard's economic heart, stated in advance so the whole hour can hang evidence on it. A partitioning scheme is the choice of which fan-out you pay. Cut the data one way and reads scatter across every shard while writes stay local; cut it another way and writes fan out while reads stay put; rebalance eagerly and the movement itself becomes load. The evening ahead will produce the same trilemma in four costumes — hash against range, local against global indexes, eager against lazy balancing, pull against push routing — and in every costume the resolution is identical: there is no scheme in which reads, writes, and rebalances are all free; there is only the scheme whose expensive operation is the one your business performs least. Hold that sentence, sir; every design in the next hour is an answer to it, and the whiteboard at the close will hand it back to you as item one.

Part Two — Hash Versus Range

Two great schemes divide the world, and each is best introduced by the pathology of the other.

Partition by range. Order the keys — alphabetically, numerically, by time — and cut the ordered line into contiguous stretches: this shard holds everything from Ashby to Fothergill, the next from Fothergill to Marsham. The virtue is locality: keys that are neighbours in the order are neighbours in the estate, so a scan — every account opened in March; every surname in one county — touches one shard or a few adjacent ones, not the whole fleet. Range is the scheme of ledgers, time series, and anything a human asks for in stretches — and the image to keep, sir, is the encyclopaedia: volume one, A to Bertram; volume two, Bertram to Chatsworth; the spines labelled with their boundaries, so that finding an entry means reading the spines, and reading a run of neighbours means pulling one volume, perhaps two. Notice one under-praised virtue while the volumes are before us: the boundaries need not be uniform, and must not be — the letters are not equally populous, so the volumes are cut where the content dictates, thick stretches of alphabet in thin volumes and thin stretches in thick ones; range partitioning inherits exactly that freedom, splitting where the data is dense, and the boundaries are therefore state — somebody keeps the list of spines, a fact that will matter in Part Five. Its characteristic pathology follows directly from its virtue; call it the monotonic-key dogpile. Let the key be a timestamp, or an ever-increasing order number — the parcels ledger keyed by dispatch time is the canonical patient — and consider where every new write lands: at the end of the order, in the newest range, on one shard — always. The historical ranges sit in dignified retirement, visited by the occasional auditor; the newest range absorbs the entire living write load of the business, and does so on its busiest day of the year, because monotonic keys and seasonal peaks arrive together — the Christmas post, all of it, addressed by construction to one door. The estate may be a hundred machines wide, and the write load lands on one of them, today; when that range fills and splits, the load moves wholesale to the new last shard, tomorrow. A hundred machines, one at work, ninety-nine spectators — the fleet as an audience watching the newest shard burn. Every time-series estate learns this within a week of launch, sir, and the remedies — salting the key with a prefix, splitting the tail eagerly, writing to the day's shard round-robin — are all confessions that the pure order was unaffordable.

Partition by hash. Feed the key through a good hash and place by the hash value: the key's identity is scrambled into uniform anonymity, and neighbouring keys fly to opposite ends of the estate. The virtue is uniformity: a decent hash spreads any workload's keys evenly, monotonic or not; the dogpile dissolves, because consecutive order numbers land on unrelated machines. The price is exactly the virtue inverted: locality is destroyed. The scan that touched one shard under range now touches every shard in the fleet — scatter the query, gather the results, pay the fan-out on every read that asks for a stretch. Hash is the scheme of point lookups — carts, sessions, profiles, caches; anything fetched by exact name — and the Dynamo school of episode six is hash-partitioned to a member, which is no accident: a cart is the point lookup made flesh. The catechism per table is mercifully short, sir: does the business ever ask for this data in stretches? Ledgers, timelines, audit trails, anything with a report — range, and manage the tail. Fetched only by exact name — carts, sessions, tokens? Hash, and never think about it again. Both, honestly both? The compound-key treaty below, or two tables and a pipeline, and no shame in either. And hash has its own celebrity problem, which no uniformity can cure — the hot key, in the trade's flatter term, and the celebrity in this house's, since the row behaves like one and will be handled like one: the hash spreads keys, not popularity. One key, requested a million times an hour — the celebrity row, the viral item, the national dashboard — hashes to one place, whatever the scheme; uniform placement of skewed love is still skewed load. The celebrity is Part Seven's business, and the course will keep the appointment.

Three footnotes of craft before the summary, each a scar. First, on the hash itself: the office asks uniformity and stability, not secrecy — a fast non-cryptographic hash is the standard tenant, and the one property that is genuinely non-negotiable is that the function never change: a fleet that upgrades its hash library and discovers the new version disagrees with the old about where everything lives has re-sent itself the resignation letter, through the post of a dependency file. Pin the placement hash, sir, forever; it is the one function in the estate with tenure.

Second, the standard armistice between the two schemes: the compound key — and since most industrial schemas are precisely this treaty, signed per table, permit me to walk it rather than wave at it. The key is built in two halves, and the two halves are treated differently; that asymmetry is the entire treaty. The outer half goes through the hash, and the hash alone chooses the shard — the trade names this half the partition key. The inner half is never hashed at all: the rows sharing a partition key, gathered on their one shard, are kept sorted by the inner half — the trade names it the clustering key, or sort key, and sir will meet both halves under those names in every store that ships the treaty. Now stage it in Bertie's shop, where each order's key is the customer first and the order's time second. One write, walked: an order of Bertie's is hashed on the customer half only — so every order of Bertie's, whatever its hour, lands on the same shard — and within that shard it is filed by its time half, after Bertie's earlier orders: one encyclopaedia volume per customer, kept in time order. Collect the consequences one at a time, because each is a fan-out chosen on purpose. The point lookup — this customer, this order — hashes the partition key and walks to one shard. The per-customer scan — everything Bertie ordered in March — hashes the same partition key to the same one shard, and finds the rows already adjacent in time order: range partitioning's virtue, enjoyed locally. The monotonic dogpile dissolves, because time is no longer the half that places: a thousand customers hash to a thousand different arcs, and the newest writes of the whole estate arrive scattered across the fleet instead of queueing at one door. And what survives of hash's price is the estate-wide scan alone — every customer's March together — which still pays scatter-gather, because March is strewn wherever the customers hashed. Hash across the estate, range within the shard: that is the treaty entire.

Third, on skew generally: uniformity claims are claims about keys in expectation, and businesses are not expectations — one tenant is a hundred times the size of the median, one county holds half the population. The schemes bound what arithmetic can bound; the residue is Part Seven's celebrity, and pretending otherwise on the whiteboard merely schedules the discovery for production.

The engineering summary, then: range buys scans and pays with dogpiles; hash buys uniformity and pays with scatter-gather; the compound key is the standing treaty; the mature stores ship all of it and make sir choose per table — and the choice, note, is precisely Part One's sentence about fan-outs, in its first concrete costume. Tonight's remaining machinery — rings, directories, rebalancers — operates beneath either scheme: given the cut, the question becomes which machine holds which piece, and how that answer survives the fleet changing size. Which returns us, sir, to the resignation letter, and to the repair.

Part Three — The Clock Face

Now the set piece. The company takes the stage, and I shall build consistent hashing in the ear, slowly, because the construction is small enough to hold whole and the argument at the end is the thing worth marvelling at.

The construction. Draw a circle — a clock face, though we shall mark it not with hours but with every possible hash value, zero at the top, wrapping around at the maximum back to zero: the hash space bent into a ring. Now place the servers on the ring: hash each server's name — Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie — and set each machine at the point its hash names. Bingo lands, let us say, at one o'clock; Tuppy at five; Gussie at nine — scattered by the hash, artlessly, wherever the arithmetic drops them. The placement rule is one sentence: hash the key onto the same ring, then walk clockwise; the first server you meet is home. A key hashing to three o'clock walks clockwise and meets Tuppy at five: Tuppy's tenant. A key at seven walks to Gussie at nine. A key at eleven walks past the top of the dial, wraps, and meets Bingo at one. Each server, you see, owns the arc that ends at its position — Tuppy owns everything from Bingo's one o'clock around to his own five — and the ring is fully spoken for: every key has exactly one home, found with no table, no negotiation, no message; any party who knows the server list can compute every placement alone.

Walk three tenants to their lodgings, so the rule is in the hands and not merely the ear. The umbrella ledger hashes — the key's name hashes, mind, nothing about its size or worth — to half past two: clockwise, first doorstep is Tuppy's at five; Tuppy's tenant. The aunt's flower-committee notice hashes to twenty past nine, just beyond Gussie: clockwise past ten, past eleven, wrap the top, and Bingo at one o'clock takes her in. Bertie's cart hashes to four: Tuppy again — the hash neither knows nor cares that Tuppy already has the ledger; uniformity is a statistical promise, not a courtesy call. Three keys, three walks, no conversation with anybody: that is the whole runtime of the scheme, and it is the same walk whether the estate holds three keys or three billion.

Now the wonder, sir. Barmy joins the fleet. Hash his name: he lands at three o'clock, in the middle of Tuppy's arc. What changes? Precisely this: the keys between Bingo at one and Barmy at three — keys that formerly walked past three o'clock to reach Tuppy — now meet Barmy first. They, and only they, change address. The keys from three to five stay with Tuppy; everything in Gussie's arc, everything in Bingo's, every other tenancy on the dial is untouched, unaware. Compare, if you will, the modulo estate, where the divisor ticking from three to four reshuffled three keys in four. Here the disturbance is one arc, and the arithmetic of the expectation is short enough to do entire, in the ear, as promised. The servers were thrown onto the ring by a hash — random positions, in effect. Take the dial as it stood before Barmy arrived: n servers, and therefore n arcs, dividing the circle among them. By symmetry — no position on the dial is special — each arc's expected share is one over n of the circle. Now let Barmy arrive. He lands inside exactly one arc, and he bisects it. A key moves only if it lies in the portion of that one arc he has taken; every key on every other arc stays exactly where it was. And the portion he has taken is simply Barmy's own tenancy — the share of one server among the n plus one now sharing the dial. One over n plus one, then, is the expected fraction of the estate that changes address. Add a server, move one over n. Remove a server — Gussie leaves for the storm — and his tenants walk clockwise to his successor, and only his tenants move: one over n again. That is the entire theorem, sir, and you have just heard the proof's whole spine: symmetry places the arcs fairly in expectation, and the walk-clockwise rule confines every membership change to a single neighbourhood.

Stage the departure too, because operationally it is the more frequent ceremony and the more instructive one. The dial as it now stands, before anybody moves: Bingo at one, Barmy at three, Tuppy at five, Gussie at nine. Gussie's machine is retired at nine o'clock — planned, this time, a decommissioning rather than a death. His arc ran from Tuppy's five o'clock around to nine; every tenant in it walks clockwise past the vacated position and arrives at the next doorstep — Bingo's, at one, once the dial wraps. Bingo inherits, in one motion, everything Gussie held; Tuppy and Barmy are not consulted and not disturbed; and the data movement is a single stream, Gussie to Bingo, throttled at leisure since the retirement was planned. Now let the same departure be unplanned — the storm, not the decommissioning — and the ring's other marriage pays off: Gussie's arc was never held by Gussie alone but by the walk's next two distinct machines as replicas, so his death moves no data at all in the first instant; the successors already hold copies, the ring simply stops routing to a dead position, and the replacement copies are rebuilt in the background, throttled, at the unhurried pace Part Six will insist upon. Placement schemes are judged at four in the afternoon, sir, but they are designed for the storm at nine. The desk does it properly — expectations, variance, the lot — and flags the chapter, rightly, as friendly territory: the mathematics is elementary probability throughout, honest coin-flipping, no impossibility anywhere in sight. And mark what else the construction quietly banked, because it is easy to hear the movement bound and miss the second gift: the placement is computable by anyone, from the roster alone. No lookup service was consulted, no message sent; a client holding the server list — a few dozen names — reconstructs the entire address book of a billion keys in its own arithmetic. The map, in this church, weighs nothing. Hold that lightness in mind for Part Five, where its price is disclosed.

The variance, and the repair of the repair. I said expected share, and an honest narrator leans on the adjective. Throw three darts at a circle and the arcs are rarely fair: someone draws a sliver, someone a third of the dial — with few servers, the luck of the hash is the whole story, and the unluckiest machine in a modest fleet may hold several times its fair share. The desk states the caveat in its formal dress — the fair share holds in expectation, while the guarantee that holds with high probability carries a logarithmic factor of slack, and the difference between those two phrases is precisely the machine that pages you — but the engineering translation is short: one dart per machine is a lottery, and lotteries have winners. Worse, when a server leaves, its entire arc lands on one successor — the neighbour inherits the whole estate of the departed, a lump of exactly the kind a fleet under stress cannot digest. The standing repair is virtual nodes — v-nodes, when the trade is in a hurry: place each physical machine on the ring not once but many times — Bingo under a hundred virtual nodes, a hundred aliases for the one gentleman, Bingo-one through Bingo-one-hundred, each hashed to its own position. Each machine now owns a hundred slivers scattered around the whole dial rather than one contiguous arc. The law of large numbers does the rest, sir: a hundred lucky and unlucky slivers average out, and every machine's total tenancy concentrates tightly around the fair share — the desk computes how tightly, and the answer improves as one over the square root of the virtual-node count. The lump dissolves too: when Gussie departs, his hundred slivers fall to a hundred different clockwise successors — the inheritance is spread across the entire surviving fleet, each survivor picking up a crumb. And heterogeneity comes free, which is why every industrial ring ships this way: the machine with twice the disk simply takes twice the virtual nodes, and owns, in expectation, exactly twice the estate. Episode six's Dynamo ran precisely this arrangement, and you may now hear its preference lists with new ears: the home trio for a key is the first three distinct machines met on the clockwise walk — the ring placing, the walk replicating; both axes of Part One's marriage, on one dial. The virtual nodes are not free, in the interest of full disclosure: a hundred of them per machine means the roster's ring representation is a hundred times longer, the successor bookkeeping a hundred times busier, and a departing machine's tenancy — while beautifully spread — now arrives as a hundred small transfers to coordinate rather than one; the virtual-node count is a dial, and the trade turns it to the low hundreds and stops. And one refinement of the modern era deserves its sentence: the pure ring caps nobody — a machine simply receives what its arcs receive — and a twenty-seventeen refinement from Google's theorists, consistent hashing with bounded loads, adds the missing clause: cap every server at a chosen factor above the mean, and let a key whose first-met server is full continue its clockwise walk to the next; the paper proves the balance holds and the movement bound survives. The desk's reading carries the citation; the load balancers of the trade adopted it within the year, which for a theory paper is approximately overnight.

The elegant sibling. Before leaving the construction, one alternative deserves its paragraph, because it achieves the same two promises with no ring at all and a proof one can do while pouring tea: rendezvous hashing, published by Thaler and Ravishankar in the mid-nineties, a whisker before the ring, and long overshadowed by it. The rule: to place a key, hash the pair — key with each server's name — obtaining one score per server, and the key lives with the highest bidder — highest random weight, in the literature's plainer name for the same rule, the weight being that score. That is the whole algorithm, sir. Balance is immediate: for each key, every server is equally likely to bid highest — a fair lottery per key, fairer than the ring's arcs, with no virtual nodes needed. And the movement bound falls out of a two-line observation: when Barmy joins the auction, a key moves only if Barmy's bid beats all the incumbents' — which, all bids being exchangeable, happens with probability exactly one over n plus one; and when a server departs, only the keys it was winning move, each to its runner-up bidder. One over n, both directions, exactly — not in expectation over arc sizes but per key, by symmetry of the lottery itself. The price is the bidding: placing a key consults every server's score — n hash evaluations against the ring's single hash and a lookup — which is why the ring won the very largest fleets and rendezvous won the small and medium ones: the load balancer choosing among a dozen backends, the cache client choosing among twenty peers, places where n is small, the arithmetic is nothing, and the exactness and simplicity are worth everything. Heterogeneity, incidentally, is one weighted formula away — scale each server's score by its capacity, with a small logarithmic correction the desk records to keep the lottery fair — so the auction serves mixed fleets as gracefully as the ring's aliases do. Set the two constructions side by side once, since the interview question is inevitable: the ring pays memory and setup — aliases, sorted positions, a binary search per lookup — and answers each placement in one hash; the auction pays arithmetic per placement — a hash per server — and holds no state at all beyond the roster; the ring's balance needs its virtual nodes, the auction's is exact from birth; both move one over n on membership change; and both are, at the sizes each is deployed, effectively instantaneous, so the choice is temperament and fleet size, not performance theatre. Two constructions, one moral, and the moral is the marvel stated plainly: the resignation letter was never about hashing — it was about coupling every key's address to a number that changes. Decouple the addresses from the fleet size, by geometry or by auction, and growth becomes a local event. Machines join; almost nothing notices. After eight episodes of theorems about what cannot be done, sir, permit the course one evening of unqualified good news.

Part Four — The Apotheosis and the Road Not Taken

The ring, having conquered the datacentre's basements, also conquered the academy's imagination, and the academic line deserves its respectful paragraph. In two thousand one, Stoica, Morris, Karger — the same Karger — Kaashoek, and Balakrishnan published Chord, which asked: suppose the fleet is not a fleet at all but a rabble — thousands of machines across the open internet, joining and leaving at will, no operator, no roster, nobody who knows the whole ring. Can a key still find its home? Chord's answer: yes, and in logarithmic time. Each node knows its clockwise successor — the minimum for correctness — plus a small ladder of fingers, held in what the paper calls the finger table: the node halfway around the ring from it, the node a quarter around, an eighth, and so on down. A lookup hops by the largest finger that does not overshoot, halving the remaining distance each time, and reaches any home in about the logarithm of the fleet size — for a million nodes, twenty hops — each node maintaining only that slender ladder while no one, anywhere, holds the map. It is a genuinely beautiful result, sir; and the protocol's deeper cleverness is in how the ladder survives the rabble: nodes join and vanish continuously, so each node runs a standing stabilization conversation — verifying its successor, repairing its fingers, handing off keys — and the paper proves the ring heals faster than plausible churn tears it, with correctness resting on the successor pointers alone while the fingers are mere acceleration. It launched a thousand papers; its fingerprints are on every distributed hash table the peer-to-peer era produced, and through them on file-sharing networks that carried, at their peak, a respectable fraction of the internet's entire traffic — theory with unusually wide distribution, sir, even if the distribution was not always of theory.

And now the honest observation, sir, delivered with the respect it is owed: inside the datacentre, the industry mostly took the other road. Not because Chord is wrong — because Chord answers a question the datacentre does not ask. Weigh the premises one by one, sir, because the exercise is the useful part — it is how one audits any imported design. Chord assumes membership is unknowable: the datacentre's membership is a file. Chord assumes no node may be special: the datacentre appoints special nodes for breakfast. Chord assumes hops are cheap relative to the alternative of maintaining global state: in the datacentre the global state is megabytes and a hop is a microsecond spent twenty times over. Every premise inverted, the conclusion inverts with them. The datacentre has an operator; its fleet is a roster, not a rabble; a thousand machines is a list that fits in memory with room left for the poetry of the configuration files. Where the whole membership can be known, a lookup need not hop twenty times across a ring — it can consult the list once, locally, in no hops at all. And there is a deeper reason, which episode five equipped sir to name: someone must be accountable for the map. When a shard is misplaced, when two nodes both claim an arc, when a rebalance goes wrong at three in the morning, the operator's first question is who holds the truth about where things live — and a protocol whose truth is smeared across every node's private fingers, converging eventually, answers that question poorly at exactly the wrong moment. The peer-to-peer world accepted that price for a reason — it had no operator and wanted none; openness, resistance to any central hand, was the entire point, and twenty hops across strangers' machines was cheap rent for it. The datacentre's premises are the inverse on every axis: machines that trust one another, latencies measured in microseconds where the open internet's are milliseconds with mood swings, an operator with a pager, an old institutional habit of courts of record — and, after episode five, a consensus kernel sitting right there, already elected, already durable, with room on its docket for one more small item. The two roads compress to a sentence apiece, sir: Chord distributes the map because it must; the datacentre centralizes the map because it may. There is also a plain arithmetic point that closes the case: the map is small. The data may be petabytes, but the map — shard boundaries to machine names — is a list of a few hundred thousand entries at the very worst, megabytes, a rounding error; the one component in the estate that never needed to be distributed for capacity. Distribute the data, sir; keep the map close — a slogan worth engraving, since half the over-engineering this course has witnessed consists of distributing things whose entire contents fit in a pamphlet. The map moved in with the parliament, and the next part tours the household.

Part Five — Two Churches: The Directory and the Gossiped Ring

Where does the shard map live? The industry split into two churches on the question, and both congregations are thriving, which tells you the schism is about temperament as much as truth.

The directory church. Its cathedral is Bigtable — Google, two thousand six — and its doctrine: placement is explicit state, held in a directory, administered by a master, guarded by the consensus kernel. Bigtable cuts tables into contiguous ranges called tablets — range partitioning, for the scan-loving workloads it serves — and the map from ranges to servers is itself data, stored in a special metadata table, which is itself split into tablets, whose own addresses are held in a single root tablet, whose address is held in the little consensus service — Chubby, episode five's lock service in its most famous cameo. A client seeking a row walks the hierarchy downward: kernel to root, root to metadata, metadata to tablet server — three lookups, all ferociously cacheable, so the steady state is one direct hop with the hierarchy consulted only on a miss. Stage it once with the company, since the walk is the church's whole liturgy. The cast first, before anybody is asked anything. There is the kernel — a handful of parliament machines, episode five's Chubby. There is Bingo, who holds the root tablet. There is Tuppy, who holds the metadata tablet covering the middle of the alphabet. And there is Gussie, who holds the data tablet with the row on it. Bertie's application wants that row, and knows none of this. Now the walk, one question at a time. It asks the kernel: where is the root? The kernel answers: Bingo. It asks Bingo: who covers the middle of the alphabet? Bingo's root tablet answers: Tuppy. It asks Tuppy: who is this row's landlord? Tuppy's metadata answers: Gussie. And it asks Gussie, who serves the row. Three questions on the cold path, each answer cached with an expiry, and thereafter Bertie's application walks straight to Gussie until told otherwise. And the tenancy itself is fenced with the full episode-five ritual, because two landlords for one tablet is this church's doppelgänger nightmare: a tablet server holds its tenancy under a lease in the kernel; lose the lease — a pause, a partition, the classic false death — and the master may reassign the tablet, with the kernel's lease machinery guaranteeing the old landlord's authority expires before the new one's begins. The map is not merely stored under the parliament, sir; its every transfer of tenancy is adjudicated there. And the map's changes travel by subscription: interested parties — the master, the smart clients, the proxies — register watches with the kernel, episode five's little notification service in its intended employment, so that a tenancy change is pushed to whoever cached the old address rather than discovered by them at their leisure. The steady state is thus doubly quiet: reads go straight to landlords, and the hierarchy speaks only when something moves. Attend to what the arrangement gets you, sir. Placement is now policy, not arithmetic: the master may move a tablet because a disk is filling, split a range because it runs hot, park a tenant's shards away from a noisy neighbour — decisions no hash can express, taken by a component whose job is to have opinions. The tablet count per server is a number someone chose; the map is a court of record someone can read; and when the three-in-the-morning question arrives — who holds the truth? — the answer is a service with a name, a log, and a page in the runbook. The price is the church's standing anxiety: the directory is machinery on the critical path of every cold lookup, the master is a role that must itself be elected and fenced — episode five's whole curriculum — and the hierarchy is a thing that can be misconfigured at scale. The church pays gladly, and its answer to the anxiety is the caching, the parliament beneath, and the operational fact that the machinery has now run the largest estates on earth for two decades.

The gossiped-ring church. Its cathedral is Cassandra, heir to Dynamo's furniture: the clock face itself is the map — placement is the arithmetic — and what little membership state exists spreads by episode six's gossip, every node learning the token positions of every other, no master anywhere, no directory to fail, any node able to answer any client and forward to the right tenant because every node can do the placement sum itself. The church has kept up with Part Three's craft, note: the early cathedrals assigned each machine one token and suffered the arc-lottery accordingly; the modern service runs virtual nodes as standard issue, each machine holding a bouquet of tokens, with the balance and inheritance benefits the desk computes. The virtues are Dynamo's virtues, inherited intact: no single component whose death stops placement; symmetry that makes the fleet easy to reason about machine by machine; an estate that keeps accepting writes through weather that would have the directory church paging its bishops. The anxieties are the mirror image, sir, and by now you can recite them with me: placement-by-arithmetic cannot express policy — the ring puts the arc where the hash says, not where the operator wishes; hot arcs are repaired by moving tokens, which is gossip-coordinated surgery; and the truth about membership is eventually consistent, which is a phrase whose costume episode seven taught you to check — during the disagreement window two nodes can both believe they own an arc, writes accepted under both beliefs, and the nets beneath — read repair, anti-entropy, hinted handoff, the whole episode-six janitorial staff — must reconcile the tenancies afterward. The church's honest answer is that it planned for this: the same machinery that reconciles replica disagreement reconciles placement disagreement, one broom for both messes — an economy of mechanism the directory church cannot claim, at the price episode six itemized. Two churches, one schism: explicit map with an accountable owner, versus arithmetic map with no owner to lose. The course, pressed for its own confession, observes only this: the industry's newest cathedrals — the Spanners, the sharded parliament estates of episode eight — have overwhelmingly built directory-style, on the grounds that the consensus kernel was already paid for, and that at three in the morning, accountability compounds better than symmetry. But the ring church's insight was real and remains banked: wherever the fleet is caches or balancers — where a stale map costs a miss rather than a lost write — the arithmetic map is lighter, and the miss is cheap. Match the church to the price of being briefly wrong, sir; that sentence retires more architecture debates than any benchmark. And carry the review questions that fall out of it, three of them, applicable to any storefront: where is the map, and who may write it? — a service with a log, or every node's gossip state; what happens when the map is wrong? — a redirect with a correction, a miss, or a lost write, in descending order of civilization; and who notices first, the system or the customer? — the directory church can alarm on its own court of record; the arithmetic church discovers disagreements when the nets reconcile them. No church answers all three perfectly; a candidate storefront that cannot answer them at all is not a church, sir, merely a hope with a logo. And in fairness to the smallest estates, there is a third pew, unfashionable and frequently correct: the map as a configuration file — twelve shards, twelve addresses, checked into version control, changed by pull request. It is a directory whose parliament is the code review; it scales to precisely the point where placement changes outpace the patience of humans; and until that point it possesses the finest observability property in this entire episode, which is that one can read it. The course mentions it, sir, because architecture reviews routinely propose a gossip mesh for what is, on inspection, nine machines and a spreadsheet.

Part Six — Rebalancing, the Operational Crucible

The map, however held, must sometimes change — a shard outgrows its machine, a machine joins, a hot range needs splitting — and rebalancing is where partitioning schemes go to be judged, because moving data is itself load, served by the same disks and cables as the customers. The craft has three commandments, each written in an outage somewhere in the trade's collective memory.

First: move on purpose, not by arithmetic. The resignation letter was the extreme case — a scheme in which growth implies mass movement — but milder versions lurk everywhere: the fixed rule that splits any range at a size threshold, the eager balancer that chases perfect uniformity. The mature posture treats every movement as a decision with a budget: so many megabytes per second of rebalance traffic, throttled beneath customer traffic, preemptible the moment latency climbs. And do the drain arithmetic aloud in the design review, sir, because nobody else will: a machine holding a few terabytes, drained at a throttle polite enough not to disturb the customers, takes hours — which means the fleet's true capacity question is never "can we add a machine" but "how many machines can we be mid-draining at once while surviving the next failure," and the answer to that is the real, honest headroom of the estate. Shard size falls out of the same sum: shards of tens of gigabytes move in minutes and spread a dead machine's inheritance across the fleet; shards of terabytes move in shifts and land like pianos. A rebalance, sir, is roadworks: necessary, scheduled, signposted, budgeted in advance, and never conducted across every lane at rush hour.

Second: fear the storm. Here is a hazard the season will formalize in due course, foreshadowed now because rebalancing is its favourite trigger, and I shall stage it with the company so the shape lodges. The fleet is at four in the afternoon again — the hour has form. The cast, announced before anything moves: Gussie, Tuppy, and Barmy, three machines of the fleet, each carrying its own load of shards; and one party who is not a machine at all — the balancer, a dutiful process that watches health checks and moves shards off any machine it judges unwell. Now, one move at a time. Gussie's machine slows: a failing disk retries its reads, not dead, merely laboured — the doppelgänger's oldest trick, crashed-versus-slow, in overalls. The balancer sees Gussie miss three health checks in a row, declares him unhealthy, and begins conscientiously moving his forty shards out to the survivors. That is the first move. Now the second. The moving is copying, and copying is disk and network. Tuppy is one of the survivors; he receives eleven of Gussie's shards while still serving his own customers; and Tuppy slows. Tuppy misses three health checks. The balancer, dutiful, declares Tuppy unhealthy in his turn and begins moving Tuppy's shards. And now the third. Barmy is receiving shards from Gussie and shards from Tuppy at once — the double burden — and Barmy follows the pair of them down. Within the half hour the fleet's bandwidth is consumed entirely by furniture removals, every van unloading onto a floor that is being carried out the back, customer traffic starving in the corridors — a rebalancing storm, the estate consumed not by load but by its response to load. And mark the signature, sir: remove the original cause — replace Gussie's disk — and the storm continues; it is self-sustaining now, each move causing the slowness causing the next move. The medicine became the disease and then became the epidemic. The remedies are dampers on the feedback loop: hysteresis, so a node must be unhealthy for minutes, not moments, before its tenancy moves — the balancer, of all components, must not believe the doppelgänger's first performance; rate limits that cap total concurrent movement whatever the apparent need, so the fleet can never spend more than a budgeted fraction of itself on removals; a global view before local action — a balancer that can see many nodes degrading at once should conclude "storm" and stand down, where one that sees only its patient concludes "move faster"; and a panic brake — the operators' ability to freeze all rebalancing with one switch, which every mature system ships and every postmortem in this genre wishes had been pulled earlier. The full theory of such self-sustaining overloads — metastability, the outage that persists after its cause is gone — is episode eleven's centrepiece; tonight, note only that the balancer is a control loop, and control loops without damping oscillate.

Third: split before you must. The monotonic dogpile taught that the newest range burns; the general lesson is that splitting a shard under load is surgery on a struggling patient — the split itself costs reads, writes, and a map update, all charged to the busiest machine in the fleet, at the hour it can least afford them. The craft pre-splits: a table expected to be large is born as many shards, boundaries guessed from the key distribution and corrected later at leisure; a range observed to be warming is divided while still comfortable, the boundary chosen where the access histogram actually peaks rather than at the arithmetic midpoint — split at the celebrity's doorstep, not halfway down the street. The balance of evidence — how warm, how fast, which boundary — is the directory church's home fixture, since a master with a map and a load report can decide, where a ring must wait for its arithmetic to be overruled by hand.

And one estate-wide fan-out question belongs in this part, because rebalancing exposes it: secondary indexes. The data is partitioned by primary key; the business insists on querying by something else — colour, county, price. Two designs, and the fan-out lands on opposite sides; take Bertie's shop, partitioned by item number, and the customer who insists on browsing by colour. The local index: each shard indexes its own tenants — Bingo's shard knows which of Bingo's items are red, Tuppy's knows Tuppy's — so a write updates data and index together, on one machine, in one local transaction, cheaply; and the browse for red umbrellas must ask every shard, since red items live everywhere the hash scattered them: scatter-gather on every colour query, the fleet interrogated to answer one shopper. The global index: the index is itself a partitioned table, partitioned by colour — red's entries on one shard, blue's on another — so the browse goes to one place and reads the whole answer, cheaply; and every write fans out: the new red umbrella updates the item's data shard and the red index shard, two machines, coupled either distributed-transactionally — last week's entire episode, invoiced per write — or asynchronously, in which case the index lags the truth and the shopper occasionally clicks a red umbrella that has just sold out, which is episode seven's staleness wearing shop clothes. There is no third design, sir; there is only the choice of victim. Local indexes tax reads; global indexes tax writes — Part One's sentence again, wearing an index's clothes — and the mature stores again ship both and make sir declare, per index, which of the two taxes the business can afford.

Part Seven — Routing, and the Survival Kit for Celebrity

Two practical matters close the tour, and the second is the one every architect meets with dread.

Routing. The map exists; who consults it? Three arrangements, in ascending order of client sophistication, and the tell for each is who gets paged when it misbehaves. Hold the three by where the map lives: in the fleet itself, in a tier standing before the fleet, or in the client's own pocket.

The first arrangement is the dumb client, and the map stays inside the fleet. Bertie's application knows nothing of placement; it sends its request to any node at all — or to a load balancer that chooses one for it. Say the request lands on Tuppy. Tuppy consults the map, or does the ring arithmetic, and finds the key is Gussie's tenant; he forwards the request to Gussie, receives Gussie's answer, and relays it back to Bertie's application — which never learns that Gussie exists. The price is one extra hop on every request; the return is zero client knowledge, which means the placement scheme can be upgraded without a single application team being consulted. Cassandra's coordinator-any-node is the type specimen, and when the arrangement misbehaves, the database operators are paged, which is where the expertise lives.

The second arrangement is the proxy tier: the map moves into a routing layer between clients and fleet. Clients stay dumb; the proxies stay current; the operators get one throat to throttle and one place to observe every query in the estate. The price is another tier to run, scale, and page — plus a hop nothing removes.

The third arrangement is the smart client: the map moves into the client itself — fetched at startup, refreshed on change, often by subscription to the directory so updates are pushed rather than polled — and the request goes straight to the tenant. Zero extra hops, maximum efficiency, the choice of every latency-obsessed estate; and a new failure mode of the first importance, distributed now into every application binary in the company: the client's map can be stale. The mature protocols therefore version the map — an epoch, a generation number — stamped on every request, so a tenant receiving a request for an arc it no longer owns answers not with silence or, catastrophically, with obsolete service, but with a correction: wrong address, here is the new epoch, ask again. Sir will recognize the shape from episode five: the routing epoch is the fencing token's civilian cousin, and the desk's sabotage this week — clients that cache the ring and skip the version check — ends, as sabotages here traditionally end, with a write applied at an address that had already been condemned, and a customer's update lost in the demolition. The general law is worth one clean sentence, because it recurs in every system with a map: a stale map is harmless only if the estate refuses to honour it — the staleness must be caught at the server, by epoch check, because the client, by definition of staleness, does not know to catch it. Any design in which an old map is silently honoured has decided that placement is advisory; and placement, tonight has argued at length, is law.

The celebrity. The hot key, to give the thing its trade name once more before we live with her for a page. Now the row every scheme dreads, because it defeats the premise beneath all of tonight's mathematics: that load, like keys, is divisible. One key — the viral post, the flash-sale item, the national election result — draws a fleet's worth of traffic to a single address, and no placement scheme can divide what is, by construction, indivisible: the ring puts the key on one arc, the directory in one range, and that machine becomes the estate's brightest ember. Stage it once, briefly, for the record: the aunt's notice — some triumph of the parish flower committee, photographed becomingly — is taken up by the national press at teatime, and by six o'clock her single row is receiving more traffic than the rest of the estate combined. The hash placed her fairly; fairness was never the issue; the issue is that the world's affection is not uniformly distributed and never will be. The survival kit, sir, in escalating order of surrender. Cache it: celebrity is read-heavy in the overwhelming case, and a small cache in front — even a few seconds of staleness — absorbs orders of magnitude; the aunt's admirers do not need the flower count fresher than the kettle boils. But caching under celebrity has its own famous failure, the stampede: at seven o'clock the cached copy's lease expires, and ten thousand concurrent admirers miss together and descend on Gussie's shard as one — the thundering herd, an outage manufactured entirely by an expiry timestamp. The discipline is standard and cheap: let one request per key refresh while the rest wait a beat or are served the slightly stale copy — request coalescing, stale-while-revalidate; the trade has several names for the one idea, which is that a cache miss should be an event, not a demographic. The full pricing of stampedes belongs to episode eleven, which is where the herd is properly anatomized. Split the reads: replicate the celebrity's shard wider than the standing factor and spread reads across the copies — replication, for once, rescuing partitioning, since reads divide even when keys do not; the follower reads carry episode six's staleness fine print, which for a flower count is no fine print at all, and the widening can be automatic — a shard whose read rate crosses a threshold sprouts extra followers for the afternoon and sheds them at dusk, fame handled as weather. Salt the writes: when the celebrity is write-hot — the live counter, the auction of the century — split the key itself: a hundred sub-counters under a hundred salted names, each salted name hashing to its own shard, every write choosing a salt at random. Do the arithmetic aloud: a hundred thousand increments a second against one row is one shard's funeral; against a hundred salted rows it is a thousand a second apiece, which is a shrug — and the read now sums a hundred rows, which is a scatter-gather paid only by readers of that one key. Sir will recognize the construction: it is episode seven's grow-only counter wearing a wig — dividing the indivisible by making the merge lawful, commutativity forgiving what placement cannot fix. And, at the limit, special-case it: the greatest estates simply detect their celebrities and hand them to bespoke machinery — a dedicated fleet for the day's viral keys, a broadcast tier that pushes the election result outward rather than letting the world pull it — because past some fame, generality is the wrong tool and the honest architecture says so aloud; the detection itself is a small standing census of the hottest keys, cheap to run and worth its weight in postmortems unwritten. What does not work is denial, sir: the load report always finds the celebrity eventually; the only question is whether the architect finds it first — and the estates that answer "we have no hot keys" divide, on inspection, into those with the census and those with the surprise.

Closing — The Whiteboard and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, four items, economics throughout.

Item one. Partitioning is the choice of which fan-out you pay — reads (hash, local indexes, scatter-gather), writes (range under monotony, global indexes), or rebalances (modulo N, eager balancers). No scheme escapes all three; match the expensive one to the operation your business performs least, and write the choice down where the next architect can find it — the choice was made against a workload, and workloads change; an undocumented fan-out decision is a trap set for one's successor.

Item two. Hash modulo N is a resignation letter, postdated to the day the fleet changes size. Decouple addresses from fleet size — the ring with virtual nodes, or the rendezvous auction — and membership change becomes a local event: one over n moves, the rest of the estate unaware. That decoupling is the single cheapest piece of foresight in tonight's territory.

Item two and a half, for the schema page specifically: co-location is transaction avoidance — keys that commit together should live together, the compound key is the standing treaty between hash and range, and the placement hash has tenure. Item three. Every shard map needs an owner, and every rebalance needs a budget. Directory or ring, someone answers "where does this key live, and who says so" at three in the morning — prefer the answer with a name and a log. Movement is roadworks: throttled, scheduled, preemptible, with hysteresis on the triggers and a panic brake the operators have rehearsed. The balancer is a control loop; undamped control loops are episode eleven's opening exhibit.

Item four. The map is state; treat it with the respect episode five taught. Version every routing table, stamp every request, and let wrong-address answers carry the correction — the routing epoch is the fencing token's cousin, and a client that skips the version check is this week's sabotage. And for the celebrity: keep the census of hot keys running, cache with stampede discipline, widen the replicas, salt the writes, and past some fame, special-case with dignity — the load report will find the celebrity regardless; arrange to have found it first.

The reading. Karger and colleagues, "Consistent Hashing and Random Trees," nineteen ninety-seven — short, elementary in the honourable sense, and the rare theory paper whose corporate consequences you have used this very day; read the balance and movement claims against the desk's proofs. The Bigtable paper, two thousand six — read tonight for its placement machinery: the tablet, the metadata hierarchy, the master's duties; its data model you knew a decade ago, but the directory church's liturgy is all in section five. And on the shelf: Thaler and Ravishankar on rendezvous hashing, for the auction and its two-line bounds; the Chord paper, for the fingers and the logarithm and the road not taken; the bounded-loads refinement of the ring, whose full citation the desk carries, for the modern cap on the unlucky server; the Chubby paper, reread from episode five, now that you have seen what the little kernel actually holds; and the Dynamo paper once more, whose ring you may now reread with tonight's arithmetic in hand.

The desk awaits with chapter nine, flagged as friendly territory, and the flag is honest: the balance and movement expectations of the ring proved in full with elementary probability; the virtual-node variance computed, with the one-over-root-k improvement made precise; rendezvous hashing's per-key bounds proved in their two lines; problems — load variance drills, a split policy for monotonic keys designed and defended, the routing-epoch protocol specified; the sabotage, proved fatal with a written counterexample as ever: smart clients that cache the ring without version checks, run to the lost write, demolition and all; and, starred, Chord's logarithmic routing bound, for the ambitious.

The ledger, sir, and briefly. Last week left one question standing, and tonight answered it: where things live is a decision, taken by arithmetic on a dial or by a directory under a parliament, with the trade-offs priced and the churches named. Two questions travel the other way, out of tonight and into the weeks ahead. First: everything tonight partitioned data at rest — but the same question stalks computation: where does the work live, who moves it to the data, and what happens when the photograph of episode two must be taken across a partitioned, flowing estate — next week, where the log finally takes its starring role. And second: stampedes, storms, and the other self-sustaining weather glimpsed tonight in the balancer's spiral and the cache's herd, which wait for the evening when failure itself becomes the engineering discipline. More of this season's questions are now answered than open, sir, which is a novelty.

Next week: the log and the dataflow. A humble file, append-only, becomes the estate's spinal cord; batch processing is rediscovered as the art of moving computation to data — tonight's new question in its turn, partitioning the work instead of the bytes; a photograph is taken of a river; exactly-once is prosecuted for fraud and settles out of court under a more honest name; and the season's oldest instrument — the Chandy–Lamport snapshot, on the shelf since episode two, teased for eight consecutive weeks — is picked up, dusted, and found running, under an assumed name, inside every stream processor on earth. Two questions this course has carried for weeks are settled in one evening, sir, the season's oldest among them. Bring tonight's shards; next week we set them flowing.

Until then, sir.

— End of Episode Nine.
