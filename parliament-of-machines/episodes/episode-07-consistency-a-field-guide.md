# The Parliament of Machines

*An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Seven: Consistency: A Field Guide*

## One Word, Four Professions

Good day, sir.

Consider the word *consistent*, and permit me to demonstrate that you have been employing four different technical terms under one spelling for the whole of your career. To the theorist of episode three, consistency was the C in CAP — and it meant, we now know precisely, linearizability: the replicated service behaving as one up-to-date copy. To the database professional, consistency is the C in ACID — where it means something else entirely: that a transaction carries the database from one state satisfying the application's invariants to another; a statement about *your* rules, not the system's timing, and famously the one letter of that acronym that names an obligation of the *application* rather than the engine. To the replication engineer of last week, consistency is the degree to which the copies agree — a spectrum with "eventual" at one end, which is a third meaning. And to the working programmer, "the data looks consistent" means, bluntly, that nothing on the screen embarrasses anyone this afternoon. Four professions, four meanings, one syllable — the most overloaded word in computing, and the source of more cross-department misunderstanding than any other single term I could name. Contracts have been signed across that ambiguity, sir. Postmortems have been written across it.

I have watched the four professions collide in a single meeting. The architect says the new store is consistent, meaning the replicas converge; the database administrator hears the ACID letter and assumes the invariants are enforced; the product manager hears the programmer's sense and assumes the screens will agree; and the theorist in the corner hears the CAP letter and quietly notes that the system cannot possibly mean *that*, because it stayed available through last month's partition. Four nods, one word, zero shared propositions — and the meeting proceeds to commit engineering resources on the strength of the agreement. The bill arrives months later, as a support ticket, a compliance question, or a headline.

Tonight the word is pinned to the bench and dissected, and the field guide is drawn — every promise a replicated system can make about what its clients observe, arranged in a hierarchy, priced, and given its government name. And the evening has a second patron alongside the theorists, because this territory possesses something rare in our field: a consumer-protection bureau. Since two thousand thirteen, Kyle Kingsbury — a man with a laptop, a test harness named Jepsen after a popular song of the period, and a methodology of magnificent bloody-mindedness — has been taking the databases of the market at their brochures' word, subjecting them to partitions and process crashes of episode one's documentary kind, checking the surviving histories against the formal definitions we shall build tonight, and *publishing the autopsies*. Vendors who claimed linearizability were shown histories their systems produced that no linearizable system could produce. Marketing pages were quietly rewritten across an industry.

The method deserves a paragraph of respect, because it is tonight's doctrine in overalls. The harness raises the database under test as a small estate of nodes; a crowd of client processes performs randomly generated operations — reads, writes, increments, compare-and-swaps — while the harness, playing episode one's documentary villains, cuts the network into halves, pauses processes mid-stride, and skews clocks; every invocation and every response is logged with its time; and afterwards a checker searches the transcript for a lawful explanation under the model the vendor advertised. No source code is consulted. No benefit of the doubt is extended. A document store beloved of startups was shown to lose acknowledged writes under partition; a coordination service was caught serving reads it had promised were fresh; more than one system advertising the summit was found, under the checker's lamp, to be dwelling several landings below it. The reports are courteous, reproducible, and devastating. It is the most wholesome sustained act of consumer protection our field has seen, it made the formal definitions commercially load-bearing, and it means tonight's vocabulary is not seminar furniture: it is the language in which the market's claims are now audited, and in which several debts of this course fall due within the hour.

A word, too, on the evening's position in the season, because the constitution marks tonight as the hinge. Six episodes built machinery: clocks and their replacement theory of order, impossibility, parliaments, consensus in captivity, the replication schools. Five episodes hence, the machinery industrializes: transactions, partitions, logs, the engineering of failure, and the capstone. Tonight is the customs house between the two halves — the hour in which every guarantee the first half built is weighed, named, and given its price tag, so that the second half may *shop* rather than build. If the season has a single hour to keep on the shelf and replay before an interview, sir, it is this one.

The itinerary: the client's-eye doctrine; the summit, linearizability, defined with teeth at last; the descent through the hierarchy, meeting several old acquaintances under their government names; the star exhibit, which is your rite of passage; the other axis — transactions, isolation, and two careful doctors; the wedding of the kettles under a convergence theorem; the deepest sentence the field has produced this century; and, at the close, the half-term audit of the season's ledger, briefly ruled.

## Part One — The Client's-Eye Doctrine

One doctrine governs everything tonight, and adopting it is half the education: **a consistency model is a contract about observable histories, and it says nothing about implementation.** Nothing about quorums, logs, leaders, vectors — nothing about how. Only about *what clients can be shown*.

The vocabulary, installed with care because every definition tonight compiles down to it. Clients interact with the system through **operations** — a write, a read, an increment, a transaction. Each operation has two visible instants: its **invocation**, when the client posts the request, and its **response**, when the answer arrives. Between them the operation is *in flight*, and its duration is real — the whole discipline lives in taking that interval seriously. A **history**, then, is the complete transcript of some execution as seen from outside: every invocation and every response, from all clients, with their real-time order — and note what a history deliberately forgets: everything inside the machine room. Two systems with utterly different machinery that can produce exactly the same set of histories are, by the doctrine, *the same system* as far as any contract is concerned. This is why the vocabulary transfers wholesale from shared-memory multiprocessors — where it was born — to planet-scale storage: the contract language never mentions the substrate.

Let me read you thirty seconds of history, so the notation has a sound. Bingo invokes a write, setting the meeting hour to seven — pause — Tuppy invokes a read — pause — Bingo's write responds, confirmed — pause — Tuppy's read responds, with the *old* hour. Notice what the transcript records and what it declines to record. It records that Tuppy's read and Bingo's write overlapped — Tuppy's invocation fell between Bingo's invocation and Bingo's response — so the two operations were genuinely concurrent, and no contract tonight may be scandalized by either answer Tuppy received. It does not record which replica served Tuppy, what the quorums were, or whether the machinery was a chain, a parliament, or a boy on a bicycle. The overlap is the doctrine's whole mercy: an operation in flight is an operation whose place in history is *not yet fixed*, and every model tonight is a rule about how much of that freedom the system may keep.

And a word on why the intervals are taken so seriously, sir, because it is the discipline's signature. An operation is not an instant; it is a request posted and an answer awaited, and between the two the client knows nothing — the write may have landed, may be landing, may be lost. A transcript may even end with operations still in flight: invocations that never received a response before the observation window closed. An honest checker must then consider both completions — perhaps the operation took effect, perhaps it never did — and the Jepsen harness spends a great deal of its cleverness exactly here, on the pending operations, because a crashed client's unacknowledged write is precisely the case that separates honest stores from lucky ones.

And what is a contract, in this language? A consistency model is simply a *set of permitted histories*. The system promises: every transcript I will ever produce lies in this set. Stronger models are smaller sets — fewer histories permitted, more comfort for the client, more coordination for the implementor; weaker models are larger sets — more histories permitted, cheaper machinery, stranger afternoons. Testing, in the Kingsbury manner, is precisely membership-checking: collect an actual transcript, ask whether it lies in the promised set, and publish the answer.

Picture the guide, sir, as a nest of fenced paddocks on one field — the field being every transcript that could conceivably be written down. The outermost fence, eventual consistency, excludes almost nothing; each stronger model draws a smaller paddock strictly inside; and the summit's paddock is the smallest in which anything useful still lives. Two consequences fall out of the picture before a single definition is stated. Strength comparisons become containment checks — one model is stronger than another precisely when its paddock lies inside the other's — which is why the atlas at the end of this hour can be drawn as a lattice at all. And a system may lawfully be *better* than its contract: a quiet, healthy cluster produces summit-grade transcripts all week under a floodplain contract. The contract is not a description of behaviour but a floor beneath it, and the audit question is never what the system did on a calm Tuesday, but what the contract permits it to do on the worst night of the year.

One more piece of furniture, the one that makes definitions tractable: a **sequential specification** — what the object would do on one machine, one operation at a time: a register returns the latest value written; a set contains what was added and not removed. Every model tonight is a rule about how a messy concurrent history may be *explained* by some well-behaved sequential story. The models differ only in what "explained" is allowed to forget.

One admission, freely made, before the climb: checking membership is not cheap. Given a transcript and the summit's definition, deciding whether a lawful explanation exists is, in the general case, computationally hard — the checker may face an exponential search among the orderings of overlapping operations, and this is a theorem about the problem, not a complaint about anyone's code. The practical bureaus manage with cleverness: transcripts kept short, objects kept simple, searches ruthlessly pruned. I mention it, sir, because it flavours the whole enterprise — the definitions are crisp, but auditing against them is genuine work, which is partly why an industry needed a Kingsbury before it acquired the habit.

## Part Two — The Summit: Linearizability, With Teeth

Now the summit, and with it the retirement of three debts at a stroke — the C of CAP left as a promissory note in episode three; the phrase *behaves as a single machine* banked unexamined in episode five; and the fine print on lease-served reads, deferred the same evening. All three were always the same debt, sir, and here is the payment.

The definition, from Maurice Herlihy and Jeannette Wing, nineteen ninety, in the ACM's Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems — the paper is "Linearizability: A Correctness Condition for Concurrent Objects," and the condition reads: a history is **linearizable** if one can assign to each operation a single instant — call it the *linearization point* — lying strictly within that operation's invocation-to-response interval, such that executing all operations instantaneously at their assigned instants, in that order, satisfies the sequential specification. Hear it in the plain register: every operation *appears* to take effect atomically, at some moment while it was genuinely in flight, and the resulting one-at-a-time story must be a lawful story for the object on a single machine. Two clauses carry all the weight. The story must be *sequentially lawful* — reads return the latest write, sets contain what the story says. And the instants must respect *real time*: if operation one's response precedes operation two's invocation — if they did not overlap — then operation one's instant precedes operation two's. Non-overlapping operations may never be reordered in the explanation. Overlapping operations — genuinely concurrent ones — may be ordered either way; that freedom is the definition's entire mercy, and it is exactly the freedom episode two taught us the world actually offers.

The founders' own first exhibit was a queue, and it earns its keep here. Bingo enqueues an apple; overlapping him, Tuppy enqueues a pear; then, after both responses, Gussie dequeues — and receives the pear. Lawful? The two enqueues overlapped, so the explanation may order them either way: choose the pear first, then the apple; Gussie's dequeue then lawfully yields the pear, and the history linearizes. But let Gussie dequeue *twice* and receive the pear both times, and no ordering of the enqueues can save it — no sequential queue surrenders the same fruit twice; the fault lies against the sequential specification, not against time. Two ways to die, sir, and the definition polices both: the story must be lawful *as a story*, and the instants must be lawful *as times*. Every checking drill at the desk is a hunt for a witness to one or a proof that both hunts fail.

Test the instrument against last week's crime scene, because this is what teeth means. The aunt read the new balance; the uncle, invoked *after* her response, read the old. Is there an assignment of instants? The write overlaps both reads, so it may be placed variously — but the aunt's read and the uncle's read do not overlap: hers must precede his. For her to read new, her instant follows the write's; for him to read old, his precedes the write's — which puts his instant before hers. Contradiction; no assignment exists; the history is not linearizable — and notice, sir, that we did not mention quorums once. The verdict came entirely from the transcript. That is the client's-eye doctrine paying rent, and it is literally how the Jepsen harness convicts a database: no access to the machinery, only the transcript and a search for a lawful assignment of instants.

You have, without ceremony, already met linearization points in the machinery of previous weeks — worth pointing at, because the instant is only mystical until it is a line of code. In last week's chain, a write's point is its application at the tail — the single place where the write becomes simultaneously durable and visible. In episode five's replicated state machine, every command's point is its application to the state machine after commitment — which is exactly why serving reads through the log preserves the summit, and why serving them from a follower's lagging copy forfeits it. In the ABD register, a write's point falls within the lodging of its stamp at a majority — and a read's, delightfully, may be dragged forward by the write-back it performs on the writer's behalf. The definition, note well, demands none of this specificity — it asks only that the instants *exist* — but diagnosis is faster when one knows where implementations customarily keep them.

Now the three payments, formally. The C in CAP: Gilbert and Lynch's proof, you may now verify, constructed exactly a history with no lawful assignment — a completed write on one side of the partition, a later non-overlapping read of the old value on the other. *Behaves as a single machine*: that phrase is this definition — the single machine is the sequential story, and "behaves as" is the existence of the assignment; episode five's replicated state machine earns the phrase precisely when reads are served in a way that admits the instants, which they are when served through the log, and which brings us to — the lease fine print. A leader serving reads locally under a majority-granted lease: linearizable, *exactly insofar as* the lease's drift assumption holds, because the lease is what guarantees no successor's write can have a linearization instant during the old leader's tenancy; let the drift bound fail — one paused leader, one fast clock — and the transcript can contain a stale read invoked after a new write's response: the assignment dies, and Jepsen-grade audits have caught precisely this in the wild. The fine print, sir, in one sentence: *a lease converts a timing assumption into a consistency guarantee, and inherits the assumption's failure modes.*

Pause on the first payment a moment longer, because episode three's promissory note deserves a proper discharge. When CAP was stated, its C was a placeholder — "the system behaves correctly," whatever that meant. You now hold the exact sense in which the theorem is true: the C is *this* definition, the assignment of instants respecting real time, and the proof's engine is precisely the star exhibit you will watch shortly, performed by a partition instead of a lagging log — a write completes on one shore; a later, non-overlapping read on the other shore cannot have heard; real time demands the read see the write; the severed cable says it cannot. Weaken the C to the junior model and the theorem *fails to bite* — sequential consistency, causal consistency, the whole coordination-free storey below, all remain purchasable on both sides of a partition. The theorem forbids exactly the summit, exactly during the severance, exactly for the shore that cannot raise a majority — no more. Most invocations of CAP in industry meetings, you will now notice, are invocations of a theorem about linearizability by parties who have not yet decided whether they need linearizability; tonight closes that loophole from both ends.

One clause from the founders' paper deserves its sentence, because it corrects a natural slander: linearizability is not a synchronization scheme, and it forbids no concurrency. The definition never requires an operation to wait upon another — a pending operation can always be completed from any reachable state — and the paper proves this and gives it a name worth carrying away: linearizability is a **nonblocking** property. That is what separates the *contract* from the coarse machinery, the one big lock, the one grand queue, that most cheaply satisfies it. The contract constrains transcripts; how much parallelism survives beneath is engineering's affair, and the lock-free trades have spent three decades demonstrating that the answer is: a very great deal.

One more property, quiet and profound, before the descent — the plan calls it the property's superpower and I concur: **locality**. Herlihy and Wing proved that a system composed of linearizable parts is linearizable as a whole: if every individual object — each key, each queue, each register — separately satisfies the definition, then any history over all of them together does too. No cross-object coordination required, no global anything. It sounds like a bookkeeping lemma; it is in fact the licence for the entire modern storage industry: build each shard linearizable, and the estate of ten thousand shards is linearizable, free. No weaker model in tonight's guide has this property, sir — sequential consistency, notoriously, composes into nonsense — and when we reach episode nine's partitioned world you will see locality doing silent load-bearing work under everything. The proof is structured at the desk; its strategy — each object's linearization instants interleave lawfully because real time is *shared* across objects — is one sentence, and the sentence is the point: real time is the one resource every object already agrees about.

And the negative half of that claim deserves its exhibit, because it is the standard interview trap. Take two queues, and a history in which each queue, examined alone, is perfectly sequentially consistent — yet any single story explaining both at once must thread both clients' program orders through both objects simultaneously, and none can. The desk sets the founders' own two-queue history as a problem; work it once and you will never again believe that per-object guarantees of the junior kind add up to an estate-wide guarantee of any kind. Locality is the summit's private privilege: linearizability composes because real time is global; sequential consistency does not, because private timelines negotiated per object owe each other nothing.

## Part Three — Down the Hierarchy

Descend now, model by model, and at each landing: the promise, the price, and what folk name it has been wearing in your systems.

**Sequential consistency**, the summit's immediate junior — Lamport again, nineteen seventy-nine, in a two-page note about multiprocessors. Keep the sequential story; keep each client's *program order* — every client's own operations appear in the story in the order that client issued them; but surrender *real time across clients*: the story may interleave different clients' operations in any order whatsoever, however the wall clock disagrees. Every client sees a world consistent with his own actions in order, and all clients see the *same* world — but the world may run on a private timeline unmoored from the clock on the wall. The price this drops: the coordination that made once-seen-forever-seen hold across clients — last week's blocking write-back, the round trips. What it keeps: one agreed story, everyone's actions in their own order. Folk sighting: a system whose replicas apply one agreed log but serve reads locally without freshness checks is typically sequentially consistent and not linearizable — the log gives one story and program order; the local reads surrender real time. The star exhibit will make this distinction physical in a moment.

The definition's birthplace explains its shape, sir. Lamport wrote it for multiprocessor hardware — the question was what programmers may assume when several processors share one memory — and modern hardware answers: not even this. Every processor on your desk buffers its writes privately before publishing them, and a core may read its own buffered write before any other core can see it — an optimization that already violates the single-agreed-story clause, which is why the hardware trades speak of relaxed memory models and fence instructions, and why the same vocabulary keeps surfacing at both ends of the scale, from nanoseconds on a chip to continents on a cable. The economics are identical in both trades: publishing every write to everyone before proceeding is the price of the agreed story, and both industries concluded the price was negotiable. And an industrial sighting named properly, since the brochure in this case is honest: ZooKeeper, the coordination service of episode five's acquaintance, serves reads from any replica by default — a single agreed order of writes, program order per session, no freshness clause — and its manual offers a sync operation to purchase the missing real-time guarantee per read. That is sequential consistency sold under an accurate label, with linearizability available as an upgrade at the till — the rare shop where the tiers are printed on the menu.

**Causal consistency**, the landing where an old friend is waiting with its papers. Surrender even the single agreed story: clients may now see *different* interleavings — but every client must see them *respecting happens-before*: if one operation could have influenced another — episode two's roads: same client, or read-what-was-written, chained — then nobody, anywhere, may observe the effect before its cause. Concurrent operations — merely elsewhere — may appear in different orders to different observers, and lawfully so. This, sir, is the corridor glimpsed in episode two's part six, now purchased as a contract: the causal-delivery debt, **paid** — and priced. And the price is exactly the machinery episode two built: carry the causal paperwork — vector clocks, or their engineering rations — and hold mail until its ancestry has arrived. No consensus, no quorum round trips, no waiting on distant continents: causal consistency is the strongest model in this guide that remains available under partition and cheap under geography — a theorem-shaped fact that has made it the darling of a generation of research systems — and its weakness is equally exact: no agreed story means no arbitrating races; two concurrent kettle-writes stay two kettles. Causality orders the roads; it cannot rank the merely-elsewhere.

Two footnotes on this landing, one flattering and one sobering. The flattering: the theorem-shaped fact can be sharpened — there are results to the effect that causal consistency, in its real-time-respecting variant, is essentially the *strongest* contract an always-available, partition-tolerant store can honour. It is not merely a pleasant waypoint but the ceiling of the coordination-free storey, which is why the research shopfronts of the last decade — stores that record each write's dependencies and hold it at a replica until its ancestry has arrived — cluster exactly here. The sobering: the paperwork is not free. Dependencies must be captured, carried, and checked; a write's ancestry fans out alarmingly under heavy read traffic — read fifty items before writing, and your write now depends on fifty histories — and the engineering literature on causal stores is, in large part, a literature on pruning, batching, and bounding that fan-out. Causal is cheap compared to consensus, sir; it is not cheap compared to nothing.

**The session guarantees**, the practitioner's landing — and here four folk names finally meet their government. Terry and colleagues — the Bayou gentlemen, sir, the receipts-keepers of last week — published in nineteen ninety-four a small taxonomy of promises scoped not to the whole world but to one *session*: one client's continuing conversation. **Read your writes**: after my write responds, my own later reads see it. **Monotonic reads**: my own reads never go backwards — once I have seen the new world, *I* am not shown the old, whatever other clients endure. **Monotonic writes**: my writes apply in my order. **Writes follow reads**: what I write is ordered after what I had read when I wrote it. Four modest promises, sir, and note the economy: each binds only one session's view, so each is purchasable with session-local machinery — sticky routing, a session token carrying the last-seen stamp — no global coordination at all. They are what your systems have half-promised for years under names like "sticky reads"; taken all four together and extended across clients, one lands back at causal consistency, which is the tidy way to remember both.

Give each of the four its thirty seconds of theatre, because each was named after a bruise. Read your writes: Bertie posts the notice, refreshes the page, and the notice is gone — he was routed to a replica his own write had not reached; last week's stale-follower farce, now outlawed by name. Monotonic reads: the aunt sees the new price, passes her own device to the uncle, and the price reverts before his eyes — her session hopped replicas backwards; the flicker, outlawed per session. Monotonic writes: Bertie posts a notice and then a correction, and at some replica the correction is applied *before* the notice it corrects has arrived — amendments to documents that do not yet exist; the third guarantee orders one client's writes everywhere they land. Writes follow reads: Bertie reads Tuppy's question and posts the answer, and at a distant replica the answer stands above a question that has not yet appeared — testimony preceding the evidence; the fourth forbids it. And the provenance is worth savouring: Bayou was built for machines that expected to be *disconnected* — the roaming laptops of the early nineties, syncing when docked — which is exactly why its promises are scoped to the session and payable in tokens rather than rounds. The network's absence was the design's premise, not its failure mode.

The tokens deserve one sentence of mechanics, since they recur in every modern shopfront: the session carries a compact receipt — the stamp of its last write, the stamp of the freshest state it has read — and every replica answering the session first checks the receipt: am I at least this new? If not, wait, or redirect. The four guarantees are four disciplines about when the receipt is updated and consulted — which is why they cost a token in a cookie rather than a quorum in a datacentre, and why they are the highest tier purchasable from the machinery for nothing more than honest bookkeeping.

**Eventual consistency**, the floodplain, and the beat this course has owed the estate agents since the founding plan. The promise: if writing ceases, replicas eventually agree. Attend to the grammar, sir. Every other model tonight is a *safety* property — a constraint on every prefix of every history: this you may never show a client. Eventual consistency constrains *nothing at any finite moment* — any read may return anything, any interleaving, any antiquity, and the contract is unbroken so long as convergence remains scheduled for some unspecified future. It is a **liveness property wearing a safety costume**: it promises only that something good happens *eventually*, while dressing in the vocabulary of guarantees — and "eventually," as I observed in another context, is the operative confession; an estate agent's word, "up-and-coming," "full of potential," a promise about a future no one will be present to audit. This is not a dismissal — convergence is a real and valuable property, last week's nets exist to deliver it, and for carts it is the honest choice deliberately made. The objection is solely to the *costume*: "our system is eventually consistent" answers no question a client can ask about any read it will ever perform. The honest follow-up — and the desk drills it — is always: *eventual, plus what?* Plus session guarantees? Plus causality? Plus bounded staleness with a number? The floodplain is habitable, sir; one merely insists on seeing the flood insurance.

And because the floodplain deserves actuaries as well as scorn, note that the trade has learned to measure it. Bailis and colleagues built exactly the instrument the estate agent never volunteers: probabilistic staleness bounds, computed from measured replication delays, answering *how eventual* in the only useful currency — after so many milliseconds, with such-and-such confidence, a read returns the latest write. On a healthy cluster the answer is often startlingly good — a handful of milliseconds to near-certain freshness — which explains the floodplain's popularity better than any brochure: most reads on most days are perfectly fresh, and the contract's weakness is invisible until the day it is not. The audit question, as ever, prices the worst night of the year, not the calm Tuesday.

And a map for the whole territory: Kingsbury maintains, at the Jepsen site, a consistency atlas — the models of tonight and their transactional cousins arranged as a lattice, edges marking strict strength, annotations marking which models survive partitions and which require coordination. I assign it tonight not as reading but as *equipment*: it is the one-page answer to "is this model stronger than that one," it hangs well beside a whiteboard, and the course will treat it henceforth as the standing atlas of this hour's territory.

Before the exhibit, the field protocol for using the hierarchy in anger, since a guide is for carrying. Three questions, in order. First: *who compares notes?* If distinct clients share side channels — telephones, dashboards, a common aunt — real time is observable from outside, and the summit or its strict transactional cousin belongs on the requisition; if sessions are solitary, the session guarantees may be the entire bill. Second: *what must survive partition?* If the estate must keep answering on both sides of a severed cable, episode three has spoken: nothing above the causal landing is available to you, and the choice is among causal, sessions, and the floodplain with insurance. Third: *what arbitrates races?* If concurrent writes must yield one agreed winner — locks, uniqueness, auctions — no landing in this part suffices at any price, because arbitration is agreement, and that requisition goes to the parliament of episodes four and five. Most designs, honestly interrogated, decompose room by room: a linearizable sliver where notes are compared, causal or session middleware for the conversation, floodplain for the analytics. The guide's purpose is not to pick one landing for the estate, sir, but the cheapest landing for each room.

## Part Four — The Star Exhibit

Now the rite of passage. The distinction between the summit and its immediate junior — linearizability and sequential consistency — is the subtlest gap in the guide, the one interviews probe and vendors blur, and the plan's ruling is that you should *hold the separating history in your ear*, whole. Staged slowly, then, with the company.

The cast first, sir, before anybody moves. One register — the shop's advertised umbrella price — replicated at two gentlemen, Bingo and Gussie. Sequential consistency is in force: one agreed log of writes, and reads served locally, from whichever copy is to hand, with no freshness check. Two clients: Bertie, at the counter, who will write; and his aunt, at home, whose read will be served by Gussie. And one number to hold in the ear throughout: the price standing on both copies as the scene opens is **five shillings**.

Now, one move to a beat. Bertie watches the new price go up, and he **writes ten shillings**. The write lands in the agreed log. It applies at Bingo's copy — Bingo now holds ten. And it *responds* to Bertie: done, confirmed, finished. That response is a recorded instant, and the whole exhibit turns on it.

Next beat, and here is the load-bearing act: Bertie **telephones his aunt**. The telephone is outside the system entirely — a channel the contract cannot see. "The price is ten shillings now," he says. The aunt, a diligent woman, rings off.

Next beat: the aunt **reads the price**. Her read is invoked after the telephone call, which began after Bertie's write had responded; the real-time order is total and unambiguous. Her read is served at Gussie — where the log entry, lawfully lagging, has not yet applied; Gussie's copy still stands at the old value. She reads: **five shillings**.

Is the system in breach? Check the summit first. Bertie's write responded before the aunt's read was invoked: non-overlapping; any linearization must order the write's instant first; her read must then see ten. She saw five. **Not linearizable** — the assignment cannot exist. But now check the junior model, and check it honestly. Sequential consistency demands one story, lawful, respecting each client's own order. Take the story: *the aunt's read of five, then Bertie's write of ten.* The aunt's program order: one read — respected. Bertie's: one write — respected. The story is sequentially lawful: her read saw the then-current five; his write then set ten. One story, both program orders intact: **sequentially consistent**. The system is in breach of nothing it did not promise — and yet the family has direct experience of the breach, because the telephone call *happened*: information travelled outside the transcript, and the contract's junior tier is precisely the tier that declines to honour channels it cannot see. That is the whole gap, sir, felt in the hand: **linearizability respects real time — and therefore respects every side channel reality contains; sequential consistency respects only the orders each client can testify to from inside.** If your clients never compare notes — no telephones, no meetings, no shared dashboards — the junior tier is observationally the summit, at a fraction of the price. The moment they compare notes, the gap is a support ticket: "I told her it was updated, and she looked, and it wasn't." Every such ticket in your career, sir, was this exhibit, performed by amateurs.

Now vary the staging twice, because edges are worn smooth by handling. Variation one: disconnect the telephone. Bertie writes ten shillings; the aunt, uninstructed, happens to read five at Gussie a moment later. The transcript is identical — but ask now who can *testify* to a breach. Nobody: the aunt did not know a write had occurred; no single client observed both sides of the reordering; the family's entire experience is explained by the story the junior model supplies. This is the precise sense in which sequential consistency is the summit *for clients who never compare notes* — the gap between the models is exactly as wide as the side channels among your clients, and for a population of strangers it is observationally nil. Variation two: keep the telephone, but let Gussie's replica, on receiving the aunt's read, first confirm against the log that it holds the latest applied entry — a freshness check, one round trip into the machinery — before answering. Her read now returns ten; the assignment exists; the summit is restored — and the invoice is itemized: that round trip, on every read, is what linearizability *costs*, and serving reads without it is what sequential consistency *saves*. The entire hierarchy, sir, is folded into that one design decision, examined under a telephone.

And a third variation, to complete the compass: let the aunt's read *overlap* Bertie's write — invoked before his response arrived. Now even the summit permits her five shillings: the operations were concurrent, her read's instant may lawfully precede the write's, and no telephone can manufacture a breach, because the call itself began before the write was confirmed and so testifies to nothing. Overlap is the definition's mercy, and this is what the mercy looks like across the counter: an answer that would be a scandal a moment later is, a moment earlier, simply the truth of a world still in flight.

Hold it whole once more — write responds; telephone; read returns the past; lawful story exists but only by reordering across clients — and the rite is passed. The desk sets the same history in the formal notation and has you prove both verdicts, then vary the staging until each model's edge is worn smooth in the hand.

## Part Five — The Other Axis: Transactions and Isolation

Everything so far concerned single operations on single objects. But half the industry's consistency vocabulary comes from a different tradition — the database hall, where the unit is the **transaction**: several reads and writes, bracketed, demanding to appear as a unit. And the halls have separate vocabularies that the professions confuse daily, so let us lay the two axes side by side, because the confusion is not pedantic — it has a body count, and tonight it gets two doctors.

The database hall's summit is **serializability**: transactions appear to execute one at a time, in *some* order — any order. Hear what is present and what is absent: present, the one-at-a-time story, now over multi-step transactions; absent, *any clause about real time*. Serializability is the transactional cousin of sequential consistency, not of linearizability — a serializable database may lawfully execute your transaction as though it ran yesterday, provided the story stays lawful; and the industry phrase "serializability is the gold standard" elides exactly the clause the star exhibit just made physical. The confusion is named, sir, and hereby executed: **the two words are not synonyms, and neither implies the other** — one governs the interleaving of transactions, the other the respect of wall-clock order; the desk has you build histories exhibiting each without the other. And the grand unification, paying the terminology forward: demand both — one-at-a-time stories *and* real-time respect — and you have **strict serializability**, the strongest promise in the combined guide: transactions, atomic, in an order consistent with the clock on the wall. Bank that name carefully; next week a company purchases it across continents with atomic clocks, and the invoice makes sense only if the name is exact.

Make the missing clause physical, because it surprises even the seasoned. A read-only transaction served from last night's snapshot is *perfectly serializable* — order it in the story before every transaction that committed today, and the one-at-a-time tale is lawful; the customer staring at yesterday's balance is receiving a legal, gold-standard, serializable view. Strictly serializable it is not — today's deposits responded before the read was invoked, and real time demands they be visible. Vendors have shipped exactly this behaviour under exactly this defence, sir, and the defence is *technically sound*, which is the lesson: the gold standard, as the industry recites it, contains no clock. When a brochure says serializable, the trained ear now asks the auditor's question — serializable *as of when?*

But the hall's daily reality is not the summit; it is the negotiated tier called **snapshot isolation**, and its resident anomaly is tonight's second drama. The arrangement, in one breath: every transaction reads from a consistent *photograph* of the database as of its start — the Chandy–Lamport spirit, industrialized into what the manuals call **multiversion concurrency control**, MVCC on every vendor's feature list — and writes succeed unless two transactions wrote the *same* item concurrently, in which case one aborts: first committer wins. Reads never block, writes conflict only on overlap, performance is lovely, and the promise *sounds* like serializability if recited quickly.

The machinery beneath, in one paragraph, because a later evening will industrialize it: the store keeps every version of every item, each stamped with the logical time of its committing transaction; a transaction beginning at a given instant reads, for each item it touches, the newest version stamped before that instant — a photograph assembled lazily, item by item, from the version shelves; and at commit, the engine asks whether any item in the transaction's *write set* has acquired a newer version since the transaction began — if so, abort and apologize; if not, stamp and shelve. Notice the asymmetry the doctors are about to exploit: the *writes* are checked for collision; the *reads* — the evidence on which the transaction acted — are checked against nothing at all. A transaction may act on evidence already obsolete the moment it acted, and commit unchallenged, provided it defaced no item that another defaced meanwhile.

Now the doctors. A hospital's rule — the application's invariant, the ACID-C sense of consistent: at least one doctor on call at all times. Tonight: two, Tuppy and Gussie, both rostered. Each, at the same weary hour, opens a transaction to book off. Tuppy's transaction reads the roster from its snapshot: *two on call* — safe to leave, one remains; he writes himself off. Gussie's transaction, concurrent, reads *its* snapshot: *two on call* — safe to leave; he writes himself off. Do the write sets collide? They do not, sir — Tuppy wrote Tuppy's row, Gussie wrote Gussie's row: different items, no conflict, first committer wins is never invoked, **both commit**. The roster reads: nobody on call. Each transaction, run alone, was impeccable; each read a genuinely consistent photograph; the invariant died *between* them, in the gap the photographs could not see — each snapshot was blind to the other's in-flight write, and the rule lived across *both* rows while the conflict detector watched each row alone. The anomaly's name is **write skew**, its formal residence is the nineteen ninety-five critique that showed the industry's isolation levels were folk taxonomy — and your bank has met it, sir; so has every system that checked a constraint under snapshot isolation and enforced it nowhere else.

The shape, abstracted for the field guide, so you can find it without the doctors: *read a neighbourhood, decide on the evidence, write a corner the neighbourhood-rule watches but the detector does not.* Two transactions each read an invariant-bearing set of rows, each conclude their own change is safe, and each write a disjoint corner of that set — no write-write conflict, both photographs individually lawful, invariant dead between them. Once the shape is named you will see it everywhere: two registrars each check that a username is unclaimed and each insert their claim as a fresh row; two withdrawals each verify that the *combined* balance of a linked pair of accounts covers the amount and each debit a different account; two officers each confirm the vault will still have a second keyholder tonight and each sign themselves out. Rosters, uniqueness, floors that span accounts, budgets that span line items — any rule that lives across rows is a doctors' rule, and snapshot isolation guards only the rows.

The remedies, priced. First, true serializability — and modern engines offer it by a refinement that carries a name of its own, **serializable snapshot isolation**, which keeps the photographs and the optimism but additionally watches the *pattern* of reads and writes among concurrent transactions — who read what another then wrote — and aborts whenever the pattern could close into the cycle write skew requires. Under such an engine the doctors do not both walk free: one commits; the other is aborted with an apology, retries against the new roster, sees one doctor remaining, and stays. The premium is paid in false alarms — the detector is conservative, and some innocent interleavings are aborted on suspicion — but the invariant survives without a single application change. Second, materialize the conflict — make the contending transactions touch a common row, an on-call token, so the detector can see the fight; a fine trick, provided every future transaction remembers the etiquette. Third, lock the neighbourhood explicitly and surrender the concurrency. What is *not* a remedy is hoping, and the desk's drills include finding the write-skew shape in three innocent-looking schemas.

The axes now stand assembled, and the confusion of professions we opened with resolves into a sentence per profession: the replication hall's models govern single operations against real time; the database hall's levels govern transactions against each other; strict serializability is the corner where both summits meet; and the ACID-C — the application invariant, the doctors' rule — is *your* obligation, which every level below the summit protects only partially, in ways you are now equipped to name.

## Part Six — The Wedding of the Kettles

Now the second half of the hour, as the plan has it, and a change of key: from promises about *ordering* to a discipline that makes ordering *irrelevant*. The kettles are getting married, sir; you are asked to stand.

Recall the estate of the problem. Last week's honest mode kept concurrent rivals as siblings and handed the merge to the application — union, for carts. Episode one, further back, left a glinting observation in a drawer: the replicated *counter*, alone among the exhibits, forgave the Post's reordering entirely, *because addition commutes*. Tonight the drawer opens — the first instalment on debt number four — and the contents assemble into a theory. The question: for which objects can replicas accept updates independently — no coordination, no leader, merely-elsewhere writes welcomed — and still be *guaranteed* to converge to one agreed state? The answer arrived in mature form in twenty eleven, from Marc Shapiro, Nuno Preguica, Carlos Baquero, and Marek Zawirski: **conflict-free replicated data types** — CRDTs, an acronym this course spells out once per episode and thereafter pronounces letter by letter, as ratified — with a theorem attached: design the object so that its states form a **join-semilattice** — a family in which any two states have a **least upper bound**, the smallest state lying above them both, and that combination is called their **join**. The merge an engineer writes is that join, sir, and the name is worth keeping, because it is the join's algebra that does the work: being a least upper bound, a join is automatically commutative, associative, and idempotent — order, grouping, and repetition cannot disturb it. Make every update move a replica *upward* in that family, and exchange states arbitrarily, joining what arrives with what one holds. Then convergence is not hoped but *proven*: any two replicas that have absorbed the same updates — in any order, along any routes, with any duplications — occupy the identical state. The name of the guarantee is **strong eventual consistency**, and mark the upgrade from the floodplain: not "they will eventually reconcile somehow," but "reconciliation is a pure function with algebraic manners; same news, same state, no negotiation, no siblings, no application on call at midnight."

Build the family aloud, smallest to largest, each repairing its predecessor's embarrassment. **The grow-only counter.** One tally per replica — Bingo counts Bingo's increments, Tuppy Tuppy's — and the join, in this family, is the *entrywise maximum*; the value is the sum. Why maximum and not addition, sir? Because the join must be idempotent — the Post duplicates, and joining the same news twice must change nothing; a maximum absorbs repeats, a sum double-counts them. You will recognize the shape instantly: it is the vector clock's merge, repurposed from tracking causality to *being the data*. Embarrassment: it cannot decrement. **The positive-negative counter**: carry two grow-only counters, one for increments, one for decrements; value is the difference; join each half by maximum. Decrement achieved — by the delightfully honest trick of making *removal itself a growth*: the down-tally only ever grows. Embarrassment: nothing yet holds *things*.

**The observed-remove set** — the wedding gown itself. Adds and removes of elements, with adds winning against concurrent removes — the cart's own law. The construction: every add attaches a fresh unique tag to its element — the kettle in Bertie's cart arrives wearing tag one, and every later add draws a fresh tag of its own, tag two, tag three, as the numbering runs on; a *remove* does not subtract the element; it records as removed *precisely the tags it has observed* — removal is testimony about what was seen, nothing more. Membership: an element is present if it wears any tag not yet covered by removal testimony. The join: union the add-tags, union the remove-testimony — both grow-only, both idempotent, the lattice laws hold by construction.

And now watch the law operate — the cast named, sir, before anything moves. Two copies of Bertie's cart: one at the club, one in the study. Each copy holds a single kettle, wearing tag one, and that is the only kettle either copy has ever heard of. And the Post is slow this evening, so neither side will hear of the other's doings until both have acted.

First move. At the club, Bertie removes the kettle. His copy has observed exactly one tag — tag one — so his remove testifies against tag one, and against nothing else, testimony being limited to what was seen.

Second move, concurrent with the first, in the study: a fresh kettle goes into the cart. Being an add, it draws a fresh tag of its own — tag three, the next free tag in the numbering — and no testimony anywhere in the world mentions tag three.

Third move: the two copies exchange states at last, and join them, in whichever order you please. Union the add-tags: tag one and tag three. Union the removal testimony: tag one, and tag one alone. Now read the membership rule off the result. Tag one is covered by testimony — that kettle is gone. Tag three is covered by nothing — *that kettle survives*. The concurrent add wins, not by timestamp, not by luck, but because the remove could not testify against news it had never received — causality, baked into the algebra as jurisprudence: *one may only remove what one has observed.* The two kettles, sir, are wed: not by choosing between them, but under a law by which every replica, told the same history in any order, reaches the same cart.

Run the tallies once with the company, so the counter is a machine and not a metaphor. Three replicas — Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie — each holding a row of three tallies, one per replica, all beginning at zero. Bingo records four increments of his own: his row reads four, zero, zero. Tuppy records three: zero, three, zero. Gussie, offline in the storm, records one: zero, zero, one. Now the gossip. Bingo merges Tuppy's row — entrywise maximum gives four, three, zero; value, seven. The Post, in character, delivers Tuppy's *same* row to Bingo again: maximum of four and four, of three and three — nothing moves; the duplicate is absorbed without a tremor. Gussie rejoins and merges Bingo's row: four, three, one — value, eight; Bingo merges Gussie's in return: four, three, one — eight. Any order, any repeats, any routes: eight. And observe the discipline that made it lawful: each replica increments *only its own* tally. The row is a ledger of testimonies, one column per witness, and the maximum is sound precisely because each witness's own count only ever grows. Let Bingo update Tuppy's column even once, and the algebra collapses on the spot into last week's lost-update crime scene.

One relation attends the wedding under a cloud, and honesty requires the introduction: the last-writer-wins register is, technically, a member of the family. Its state is a value bearing a timestamp; its merge keeps whichever pair carries the larger stamp; that merge is commutative, associative, and idempotent — a lawful join, a certified semilattice, full strong-eventual credentials. And it is precisely the arbitration this course arraigned last week: the law it converges under is *the Post's perjured clock decides*, and the update it discards is discarded forever, silently, on that testimony. The lesson is load-bearing, sir: the theorem guarantees *convergence*, never *justice*. Every object in this family converges; whether it converges on a defensible answer is a property of the law you chose, not of the algebra that enforces it. The observed-remove set is celebrated not because it converges — the perjurer converges too — but because its law encodes causality, and causality is the just arbitration. Choose the object, and you have chosen the verdicts.

The theorem beneath the ceremony, stated as the desk will prove it: a state-based object whose states form a join-semilattice, whose updates are inflations — each moves the state upward — and whose replicas merge received states by the join, achieves strong eventual consistency: delivery of the same update-set, in any order with any repetitions, yields the same state everywhere. The proof's engine is one line of algebra — *the join of a set of states does not depend on the order or multiplicity of joining* — which is exactly commutativity, associativity, and idempotence doing what they are for. And the wonder budget is spent here, as scheduled, on the inversion the whole construction performs: every other model tonight fought the Post's disorder with coordination — holding mail, blocking reads, electing sequencers. The semilattice *surrenders* the battle entirely: it re-designs the *object* until disorder is powerless against it, until merge forgets nothing and therefore order was never the point. Episode one's counter knew this in embryo; the shopping cart practised it commercially; the theorem names the entire species.

Two engineering notes complete the settlement, and both are invoices. First, the matter of what travels. Everything tonight shipped whole *states* — rows of tallies, bags of tags — joined on receipt; that is the **state-based** school, and it asks nothing of the Post beyond eventual gossip; last week's anti-entropy nets suffice unmodified. There is a dual school, the **operation-based**, which ships *operations* instead — "increment by one," "add tag three" — and travels far lighter, but demands far better manners of the Post: operations are not idempotent, so delivery must be exactly-once, and for some objects causally ordered — episode two's machinery, re-hired as a delivery service. The trades' standing compromise has a name of its own, the **delta CRDT**: ship only the recently changed *fragments* of state, and take both advantages at the cost of neither, mostly. Second, the metadata bill, presented without discount: the tags accrue, and so does the removal testimony — those covered tags are episode six's **tombstones**, in algebraic dress — and a set that has lived busily for a year may weigh many times its visible contents. Pruning the archive — discarding testimony that every replica has certifiably absorbed — requires knowing what *every* replica has absorbed, which is a global fact, which requires... coordination. There is the season's irony at its purest, sir: the coordination-free object may be *used* forever without agreement, but *slimmed* only with it. Nothing in this field is free; the semilattice merely lets one choose when to pay, and the choosing is the luxury.

The costs of the law itself, stated with matching honesty: the law must fit the business — add-wins is a choice, and a banking balance is not a semilattice, however devoutly one wishes it; and nothing here provides an agreed *order* — a CRDT cannot run an auction. It is a large, lawful, coordination-free province, sir, with a well-marked border — and the border is patrolled, as ever, by the race test.

## Part Seven — The Horizon: CALM

One movement remains before the audit, and it is the view from altitude. The season keeps meeting the same fork: some tasks yielded to cleverness without coordination — the photograph, the register, the cart — while others — the bit, the lock, the auction — demanded a parliament every time. Is there a *law* sorting the forks? There is a candidate, sir, and it may be the deepest single sentence the field has produced this century. Joseph Hellerstein and Peter Alvaro, developing a decade of work on declarative distributed logic, stated and their school proved the **CALM theorem** — consistency as logical monotonicity: *a distributed program has a coordination-free, eventually consistent implementation if and only if it computes a monotone function of its inputs.* Monotone, in the logician's sense: more input never retracts prior output — conclusions only accumulate. The cart's union: monotone. The grow-only tallies: monotone. The photograph's frontier: monotone in its cuts. But a *negation* — "the auction is closed, no further bids," "the account balance never dipped below zero," "this is the *final* state" — anything that must assert a completed totality over inputs still possibly in flight — is non-monotone, and non-monotonicity is precisely where coordination becomes not an engineering preference but a mathematical obligation: somewhere, something must *seal the set*, and sealing a set across machines is agreement, and agreement is episode three's terrain with all its invoices. The theorem's proof is honestly beyond this course — it lives in declarative-semantics territory the syllabus does not visit — and is stated here with the respect and the pointer the honesty clause requires. Its practical edition fits on the whiteboard and changes design reviews: *find the negations.* Every place a specification says "final," "exactly," "never," "the winner," is a place coordination will be spent; everything phrased as accumulation can, in principle, flow free. Read your own designs for their quantifiers, sir, and the invoices become legible before the architecture is drawn.

Practise the classification once, aloud, on four fragments from the season's own case files. A watchlist assembled by union from many stations: monotone — more reports, more entries, nothing retracted; free to flow. The question *has this parcel's ancestry arrived*: monotone — ancestry only accumulates, and the answer, once yes, stays yes forever; which is exactly why causal consistency needed no parliament. The question *is this replica's copy the latest*: non-monotone at its heart — "latest" is a totality claim over writes still possibly in flight, and there stands the round trip that the star exhibit's second variation priced. And *the winner of the auction is Bingo, at seven shillings*: non-monotone by construction — one further bid retracts the conclusion — and there stands episode three's bit, wearing an auctioneer's coat. Notice, finally, the jurisprudential trick the observed-remove set performed under tonight's canopy: removal *sounds* like negation — and naive removal is — but recording removals as accumulating *testimony against observed tags* re-drafts the negation as a growth, and the theorem's licence extends exactly as far as such re-draftings can be found. That is CALM as a design practice rather than a verdict, sir: before purchasing a parliament for a negation, ask whether the negation can be re-drafted as an accumulation. Sometimes it cannot — finality is finality — and then the invoice is a theorem's invoice, and one pays it with dignity.

## Closing — The Whiteboard, the Half-Term Audit, and the Ledger

The Whiteboard, four items, the course's densest.

Item one. Name the contract, not the vibe. "Consistent" is four professions; in a review, demand the government name — linearizable, sequential, causal, read-your-writes, eventual-plus-what — and for transactions the isolation level by anomaly: does this level admit write skew? The Jepsen atlas on the wall settles strength disputes in ten seconds — and when the review stalls on the word itself, hand each profession its sentence from tonight's opening; most consistency arguments are professions talking past one another through a shared syllable.

Item two. Linearizability speaks of real time; serializability of transactions; strict serializability of both. Neither of the first two implies the other, the side-channel telephone is the test for the first, and the summit's fee is paid in coordination — buy it where clients compare notes, decline it where they cannot, and remember the three questions of the field protocol: who compares notes; what must survive partition; what arbitrates races.

Item three. Snapshot isolation permits write skew, and your invariants that span rows are unguarded by it. Find the constraint that lives across items; then serialize truly, materialize the conflict, or own the anomaly in writing. The two doctors are on every schema, sir; the drill is finding them before the roster does.

Item four. If your merge is a join, you may sleep: semilattice objects converge without coordination, by theorem — spend the design effort making the merge lawful rather than the delivery ordered, and audit the *law* as carefully as the lattice, for the perjurer's register is also a lattice. And where the specification negates — final, exactly, never, winner — budget a parliament, by theorem likewise. Between those two theorems runs the entire economics of this field.
