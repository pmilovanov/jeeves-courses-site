*An Audio Course in Distributed Systems -- Episode Thirteen: The Full-Time Parliaments*

## Part One -- Two Successors and Two Constitutions

Episode five left the industry's arrangement thus: consensus is rented, not built; the landlords are few and intensively engineered; the pattern is the *coordination kernel* -- locks, leases, elections, membership, and small hot metadata, concentrated in one obsessively guarded service so that ten thousand tenants may inherit its guarantees. The monuments were Chubby, proprietary and confessional, and ZooKeeper, the open world's kernel of record. Tonight's two are the monuments' working successors, and their parentage is worth a minute each, because in this field the birth circumstances explain the anatomy.

etcd -- spoken *et-see-dee*, as the lexicon insists, and the name is a small joke of the unix persuasion: the *et-cetera* directory, where a machine keeps its configuration, with a letter appended for *distributed* -- was born in two thousand thirteen at a company called CoreOS, which was building an operating system that updated itself across a fleet and needed somewhere trustworthy to keep the fleet's shared facts -- including, charmingly, a semaphore by which the machines agreed *not to reboot all at once*, coordination in its most domestic possible form. The designers reached for the freshest consensus scholarship of the day -- Raft, then barely a year in print -- and built the small, strict, disk-backed key-value parliament this episode will dissect. Two consequences of that birth. First, etcd's implementation of Raft became, in effect, the reference implementation -- the library at its heart has been borrowed by half the infrastructure projects of the past decade, so when episode four told you Raft was built to be teachable, etcd is where the teaching compounded into industry. Second, an orchestration project then incubating at Google chose etcd as its sole store of record, and that project was Kubernetes; the two have been inseparable since, and the project now resides, with its store, in the keeping of the Cloud Native Computing Foundation. When you hear *control plane*, sir, you may substitute *etcd plus its clients* with very little loss.

Consul arrived the following year, two thousand fourteen, from HashiCorp -- the house of Mitchell Hashimoto and Armon Dadgar -- and its birth was different in a way that decides everything downstream. HashiCorp had already shipped, the autumn before, a curious little tool called Serf: no consensus anywhere in it, just a gossip protocol by which hundreds or thousands of machines maintain a shared roster -- who is in the cluster, who has left, who has died -- with no central authority at all. Consul is what happens when you take that gossiping parish and set a small Raft parliament on top of it: the servers -- three or five, as the crowded club prescribes -- replicate a strict state store through consensus, exactly as etcd's members do; but *membership and failure detection for the whole estate*, every last application machine, travel by gossip underneath. And around that two-storey arrangement Consul builds its actual product: a registry where services announce themselves, are health-checked where they live, and are discovered by name -- the datacenter's directory enquiries, with a key-value store and a lock service attached.

There is the framing device, sir, and I shall lean on it all evening: **same offices, two constitutions.** Both services rent out the identical coordination kernel -- membership, configuration, locks, elections, discovery of who currently holds what office. But etcd is a *unitary* state: one parliament, one ledger, everything and everyone appearing before it; failure detection itself is centralized, as we shall see, in the form of leases filed with the house. Consul is a *federal* state: a parliament for the record, a gossiping parish for presence, the two loosely joined, with the work pushed to the periphery wherever the periphery can carry it. Neither constitution is the correct one. Each buys its virtues with its own currency, and by the comparative anatomy of part seven you will be able to price both from first principles -- which is, after all, the only skill this course has ever been selling.

## Part Two -- etcd, or the Ledger with a Filing Cabinet

Begin where episode five's machinery ends. A client's write arrives at the etcd leader -- call the leader Bingo, for old times' sake. Bingo appends the operation to his Raft log, persists it to a write-ahead file with the synchronous conscientiousness episode five priced, and posts the accept letters; Tuppy writes and acknowledges; the entry is committed by majority, applied in log order at every member, and only then acknowledged to the client. And the amnesia clause of episode four is honoured in the standard manner: the log's tail lives in that write-ahead file, synced before any acknowledgment; the applied state is photographed to disk at intervals; and a restarted member replays the one atop the other -- crash-recovery, not crash-stop, as befits a parliament that intends to be permanent. Nothing new: this is the replicated state machine, the log driving identical copies, precisely as constituted in episode five. If tonight stopped here, etcd would be an exercise. What earns the anatomy is the *machine being driven* -- the state the log feeds -- because etcd's designers made a choice there that quietly organizes everything else: the store keeps history.

Here is the choice, stated as furniture. The keyspace is flat -- no little tree of znodes, just names. Behind the names sits a single counter for the *entire store*, and I ask you to smile on schedule one final time, sir: a number that only rises. Every committed write -- every put, every delete, every batch -- advances this counter by one, and the store files the change *under that number*. The number is called the **revision**, and it is the true name in the manuals and the interface alike, so fix it now: revision. Ask etcd for a key and you receive, besides the value, the revision at which that value was created and the revision at which it was last modified. Ask more curiously and you discover the filing cabinet: the store can answer *as of* any revision it still holds -- the whole keyspace, consistent, as it stood at that numbered instant -- because an update does not overwrite the old edition; it files a new one under the new revision, and the old edition remains, addressable, in the drawer. The trade calls this multi-version storage; the course calls it what it is: *the log, wearing a filing cabinet*.

Stage it once, small, in the house style, because the desk's first drill runs on exactly this arithmetic. The store is young; its revision stands at seven. Bertie writes: set the teapot's price at two pounds. The revision advances to eight, and the teapot's record now reads: created at revision eight, last modified at revision eight, first version of its line. Bertie reconsiders and writes again -- two pounds ten -- and the revision advances to nine: same key, created at eight, modified at nine, second version. Gussie, tidying, deletes the key: revision ten, and the teapot's generation closes. Bertie, remorseful, writes it anew: revision eleven -- a fresh generation, created at eleven, version one again, the ancestry severed on purpose. Now observe what the counter can serve you, sir, each item in turn. A read of the present sees the newborn teapot of revision eleven. A read *as of nine* sees the two-pounds-ten edition, exactly as the world stood at that instant. A watch *from eight* is read the whole biography in order -- modified at nine, deleted at ten, created anew at eleven. And after a compaction decree at ten, the as-of-nine read receives the formal refusal, the drawer having been honestly emptied. One rising counter, and the entire apparatus of history falls out of mere filing discipline. Episode five taught you that the log is the narrow waist -- agree on the sequence and the state is derived. etcd merely declines to throw the derivation away. A read at revision such-and-such is episode two's photograph, sir -- a consistent cut, available retroactively, because the house numbered every event as it happened and kept the negatives.

And history, once kept, must be paid for -- episode ten's lesson about compacted logs, returning in a filing cabinet. The drawers fill. So etcd *compacts*: a maintenance decree names a revision and the store discards all editions older than it, keeping only what a reader as-of-that-revision or later would need. Compaction is the store's honest confession that the past has rent, and the confession has a consequence we shall meet twice more tonight: any client whose business depends on old revisions -- a reader of history, a watcher resuming from where it left off -- may return to find the drawer emptied, and receives not a guess but a formal refusal: *compacted*. Remember the shape of that refusal, sir. Two of the mechanisms still to come depend on it entirely.

Because the filing cabinet's first dividend is the *watch*. You met the watch as ZooKeeper furniture in episode five -- a subscription, one node, tell me when it changes. etcd's version is the same desire executed against the revision store, and the difference is instructive. A watcher does not say *tell me about the future*; it says *tell me everything since revision nine hundred* -- and the store, holding history, simply *reads the ledger aloud to it*: every change to the watched keys after that revision, in revision order, each event stamped, and then, the past exhausted, the subscription rides forward into the present, delivering each new change as it commits. Attend to what this gets you, because it is the honest version of a promise most message systems fumble: the watcher who disconnects -- laptops close, sir; processes pause; we have it in evidence -- reconnects and resumes *from the last revision it processed*, missing nothing, duplicating nothing it cannot recognize by number. There is no gap-anxiety, because the subscription is not a stream of ephemeral notifications; it is a cursor over a kept ledger -- episode ten's consumer offset, wearing coordination clothing. And the price is exactly the confession above: resume from too far back, behind the compaction decree, and the store refuses -- *compacted* -- whereupon the client must do the honest thing: fetch the present state whole, note its revision, and watch from there. Hold that little ritual -- *list, then watch, and on refusal, list again* -- because part three will reveal it as the metabolic cycle of the entire orchestrated world.

The second dividend is the *lease*, and here etcd is paying royalties to episode five directly. A lease is granted with a time-to-live and kept alive by heartbeat; keys may be attached to it; when it expires -- no heartbeat within the term -- the attached keys vanish, in one committed stroke. ZooKeeper's ephemeral node, generalized: presence as data, absence as expiry. The bookkeeping detail worth carrying: expiry is the *leader's* judgment -- the term is kept on the leader's clock, conservatively, and the revocation travels through the log like any other decree, so every member deletes the departed keys at the same slot in history. You know from episode five precisely what such a design assumes -- bounded drift, the pessimistic anchor, the discounted tenancy -- and etcd's manual assumes it with the same straight face. Nothing new under the sun; the point of tonight is watching the sun come up over machinery you own.

Now locks and elections, and the answer to a question episode five left you holding. etcd ships recipes for both, and they are ZooKeeper's recipes re-cut for a flat keyspace: each contender writes a key under a common prefix, attached to its lease; the store's answer includes the revision at which each key was *created*; lowest creation revision holds the lock, or the chair; each waiter watches the key immediately beneath its own -- no thundering herd at the succession, exactly the courtesy episode five admired. But here is the beat that matters, sir. Episode five preached, at sermon length, that a lock without a *fencing token* -- the rising number checked at the storage system, the store having the last word -- is a resurrection waiting for a garbage collector. You may now ask: where, in this working parliament, does one *get* the token? And the answer is: it was never a separate product. The revision *is* the token. The number that stamps every write, total-ordered by the log itself, rising across every handover of every lock -- presented with each downstream write, compared at the resource, done. When the course told you the smallest bone of the skeleton would be sold at a counter someday, this is the counter.

And I can produce the auditor's report saying so, which is a satisfaction this course rarely gets to arrange. In early two thousand twenty, the Jepsen harness -- episode seven's consumer-protection bureau, the dossier episode five told you to demand of any landlord -- was turned on etcd, version three point four point three, at the project's own invitation. The findings, sir, read like the season's syllabus notarized. The key-value operations, including multi-key transactions: correct -- strictly serializable, the store doing exactly what its contract claims, a verdict rarer in that bureau's files than the industry would like you to believe. The watches: faithful -- every change delivered, in order. And the lock interface: *fundamentally unsafe* -- not because of a bug, though a bug was found and mended, but because, in the auditor's own analysis, no lock service whatever can guarantee mutual exclusion to processes that may pause in an asynchronous world -- episode five's theorem, cited back at the vendor -- and the recommendation, in nearly the course's words: treat the lock as advisory, carry the revision as a fencing token, and let the resource refuse the stale. When the season's arguments and the field's most adversarial auditor converge on the same sentence, sir, you may take that sentence as settled.

Between the locks and the reads, one administrative organ, because episode five marked this exact spot on the map: *the dragons winter in reconfiguration.* etcd amends its parliament one member at a time -- the simple scheme, whose post-publication subtlety episode five duly disclosed -- and it has institutionalized the caution the episode preached. A new member may be admitted first as a **learner** -- the manual's own word, so keep it -- an apprentice of the house: he receives the log, replicates it, catches up, and *votes not at all*; only when his ledger has drawn level does a second decree promote him to the franchise. The hazard being retired is one you can now name at sight, sir: admit a blank-slated voter to a small house and, for the whole interval of his catching-up, the quorum arithmetic counts a gentleman who knows nothing -- the club crowded with an empty chair. The learner is episode five's *caught up before he counts*, shipped as a feature with a flag.

One more organ, and it is the one episode five left standing open: the read. Episode five stated the fine print and postponed the machinery -- a leader may be deposed and not know it; his confident local answer may be a posthumous letter -- and episode seven's field guide defined what *current* formally means. Tonight, the working machinery. An etcd read is, by default, *linearizable*: it reflects everything committed before it was asked, no posthumous letters. The naive way to secure that guarantee is to push the read through the log like a write -- correct, and priced like a write, which for a read-heavy kernel is ruinous. etcd instead performs a ceremony the Raft literature calls the **read index**, and it deserves its beat, because it is the lease-and-quorum reasoning of episode five compressed into three moves. Move one: the leader -- Bingo, still in the chair -- notes the index of the latest committed entry: *this much history, at least, must be visible to this reader.* Move two: Bingo confirms he is still leader *now* -- a round of heartbeats, and he proceeds only when a majority has answered *to his term*. Feel the crowded club working: had a rival truly seized the chair, the rival's coronation required a majority, any two majorities share a member, and that shared gentleman -- holding the higher term -- would refuse Bingo's round and say why. A full club answering to Bingo's term is therefore proof, not hope, that his reign extends through the moment of asking. Move three: Bingo waits until his own state machine has applied up through the noted index -- the ledger's ink dry to that line -- and answers from local state, touching the log not at all. One round trip, no disk, and the answer provably reflects all history through a moment at which the answerer provably held the chair. And when even that round trip is too dear, etcd sells the honest discount: the *serializable* read -- the word doing exactly the work episode seven trained it to do -- served from any member's local state, cheap, fast, and possibly stale, the staleness openly declared in the option's name. There is the menu, sir, and note the house's default: etcd serves the strict item unless you ask for the discount. Hold that; Consul chose otherwise, and the comparison will earn its keep.

Close the anatomy with the sign on the door. etcd's manual caps the store at some gigabytes -- a couple by default, eight at the documented ceiling -- and caps any single request at about a megabyte and a half. These are not shy engineering limits, sir; they are the constitution enforced in hardware terms. Episode five: the kernel keeps *small hot metadata*; the parliament is not a warehouse. etcd puts the doctrine in the quota system, so that the tenant who mistakes the ledger for a lake discovers the misunderstanding at the door, which is the cheapest place to discover it. And the cap keeps the estate honest about recovery, too: a book this small restores from its snapshot in minutes, and a watch reel this short replays in seconds -- smallness is not a limitation of the parliament, sir; it is the parliament's fitness for office.

## Part Three -- The Greatest Tenant

Now the tenancy agreement, produced at last, and read aloud.

Kubernetes keeps *all of it* in etcd, sir -- every object in the cluster: every deployment, every pod record, every service, every scrap of configuration, every role and binding, each filed as a value at a key under a registry prefix. The API server -- the front door of every cluster you have operated -- holds *no durable state of its own*; it is a stateless clerk, mediating between the world and the one component in the entire control plane that remembers: the rented parliament. That is the coordination-kernel doctrine executed to its logical conclusion -- not merely *rent your locks*; the entire estate's book of record is the kernel, and everything else in the control plane can be shot and restarted at leisure, because everything else is derived.

And the derivation, sir, is the part I have been saving. Recall the etcd anatomy: revisions, the watch as a cursor over kept history, the compaction refusal, list-then-watch. Now watch Kubernetes wear it. Every object the API server hands you carries a field called the resource version -- an opaque string by decree of the manual, and, in every cluster you have ever touched, the etcd revision underneath, sir; the store's rising number, passed through the clerk with the serial numbers barely filed off. When a controller updates an object, it submits the edit *with the resource version it read*, and the write commits only if the version still stands -- if some rival edited meanwhile, the clerk refuses, the controller re-reads and retries. You have met this pattern twice: it is episode eight's optimistic concurrency, and it is episode five's fencing comparison -- the check at the store, the store having the last word, industrialized into the default write path of the orchestrated world. An entire ecosystem of controllers, sir, contending freely over shared state, serialized by nothing but a rising number and a refusal -- and it works, at planetary scale, for the reason the season taught: the enforcement point is the point of effect.

Then the watches. A cluster of any size runs thousands of watchers -- every controller, every scheduler, every node agent, all needing to know the moment their corner of the estate changes. Pointing thousands of subscriptions at a five-member parliament would flatten it -- this is Chubby's scaling lesson from episode five, the landlord who survived his tenants only by policing their caches. The API server's answer is the *watch cache*: the clerk holds *one* watch against etcd per resource type, maintains from it an in-memory recent history, and fans the events out to every client watcher from that copy -- one subscription at the parliament, thousands served at the clerk's counter. And every client of that clerk speaks, verbatim, the ritual you memorized an hour ago: *list, then watch* -- fetch the current state whole, note its resource version, subscribe from there; and when the reply comes back *too old* -- the clerk's polite rendering of the store's *compacted* -- throw the cache away, list again, resume. The trade calls the client-side machine an informer. It is the compaction contract of a disk-backed log store, surfacing two rentals up, in the daily weather of every cluster on earth.

And now the reveal, and here I shall allow myself to be plainly astonished, because sir has earned it twice over. Ask what a Kubernetes controller *is*, underneath the trade dress. It holds a desired state -- read from the book of record. It observes an actual state -- the world, reported through the same book. And it runs a loop: compare, correct, repeat. It does not process each event as a command -- events are merely *hints that the comparison is worth re-running*; miss one, and the next listing repairs you, because the loop compares *states*, not histories. The trade calls this **level-triggered** -- acting on the present level of the signal -- as against **edge-triggered**, acting on each transition as it flies past; carve the pair into the whiteboard, sir, because choosing between them is half of distributed-systems client design, and we shall see Consul choose the other lane within the half hour. And with that word in hand, stand back and look at the whole machine at once. A strictly serializable, history-keeping log store at the centre. A stateless clerk fanning its ledger outward. A thousand independent reconciliation loops, each reading the level, correcting toward the desired, writing back under a fencing number, retrying on refusal -- no loop trusting any message further than the next relisting, every loop idempotent because episode ten taught the world that retries demand it. Sir: *that is the architecture of the orchestrated industry*, and there is not one atom in it the season did not derive. The replicated state machine of episode five is the memory. The log of episode ten is the nervous system. The optimistic fence of episodes five and eight is the discipline. The level-triggered loop is episode one's three-question catechism -- what if this message is lost, duplicated, delayed? -- answered *architecturally*: it does not matter; we never believed the messages anyway; we believe the book. Ten years of the industry's operational practice, sir, and it reduces, cleanly, to the season's shelf. That was always the course's contention. Tonight it is exhibited.

Two footnotes to the tenancy, both payable in episode five's coin. The election of the controllers -- for there are replicas of the controller manager and the scheduler, and only one may act -- is settled by lease objects in the book of record itself, and the campaign is nothing but the write path you just learned: read the lease object, observe it vacant or gone stale, and write your own name into it *citing the resource version you read*. Two candidates race; both read the vacant lease at the same version; both write; the store commits one and refuses the other as stale; the refused candidate re-reads, finds the throne taken, and settles into renewal-watching. No election protocol at all, sir -- the fence that guards Bertie's teapot adjudicates the throne, which is precisely Herlihy's point from episode four wearing overalls: own agreement at the bottom and every contest above it is a compare-and-swap. Hold by renewal, the very recipe from tonight's part two, run by the tenant on the landlord's furniture. And the fleet's heartbeats -- ten thousand node agents attesting they live -- are *also* leases: one small object per node, in a namespace set aside for the purpose, renewed on a cadence of seconds; the node controller watches the renewals and condemns the silent. Failure detection, in the unitary constitution, is thus *centralized bookkeeping*: presence is a row in the ledger, refreshed; death is a row gone stale. File the shape, sir. The federal constitution is about to do the same job with no ledger at all.

## Part Four -- Consul, or the Federal Arrangement

Cross the estate now to the house of HashiCorp, and begin with the resident on every machine. Consul's unit of deployment is the *agent*: a long-running process on each and every node in the datacenter -- not merely on the coordinating few, but on all of them, application servers included. Most agents run in *client* mode: they hold no replicated state and decide nothing; they are couriers and notaries -- forwarding queries to the servers, running health checks against the services on their own machine, and gossiping. A handful -- three or five, the crowded club's usual pricing -- run in *server* mode, and these are the parliament: a Raft group, leader and followers, log and elections, terms and commitment, every organ episode four built and episode five industrialized. A client's write -- register this service, take this lock, set this key -- is forwarded to the leader, sequenced through the log, applied at each server, acknowledged. Structurally, sir, so far, a sibling of part two, and the season requires nothing further of it.

The filing differs, though, in a way worth thirty seconds, because the Roblox affair will turn on it. etcd keeps its applied state *on disk*, in a B-tree store -- a single-author embedded library of the Go world; the fork etcd maintains is called bbolt, spoken *bee-bolt* -- and keeps history there, as part two lingered over. Consul inverts the arrangement: the applied state lives *in memory*, in immutable radix trees -- snapshots of state cheap to take because nothing is overwritten in place -- rebuilt on restart by replaying the Raft log; and the *log itself* is what lives on disk, in a store called BoltDB. The same B-tree bindery, sir, in both houses -- etcd binding its ledger in the maintained fork, Consul binding its log in the original -- a coincidence of the Go ecosystem's small parts bin that would be mere trivia, except that we shall shortly watch a pathology in that shared bindery take a platform off the air for three days, and the repair turn out to exist in one fork and not the other. Consequence of the inversion, meanwhile, and carry it to part five: Consul's store is *current-edition only*. No revision drawers, no as-of reads, no cursor over kept history. The ledger remembers the order; the state remembers only the present. What that costs, and what it saves, is exactly the watch-philosophy divide we shall price at the registry counter.

But the parliament, sir, is the *upper* storey, and the lower is where Consul earns its own episode. Membership -- who is in the estate, which machines live -- does not go through the Raft servers at all. It travels by gossip, in the parish register: the Serf layer, every agent a parishioner, and underneath Serf a protocol this course has named twice and never once opened. In episode six, describing the Dynamo school's epidemics, I named SWIM in passing and went no further; episode eleven then priced the whole product category -- *the gossip fleet's accusations*, on the bill beside every other suspicion service. Tonight it is performed. SWIM -- spoken as the word, an acronym for scalable, weakly-consistent, infection-style membership -- was published in two thousand two, out of Cornell, by Abhinandan Das, Indranil Gupta, and Ashish Motivala, and its founding observation is a budget line. The naive way for a parish of a thousand to know its living is all-to-all heartbeats: every member attesting to every member, a standing chorus whose cost per member *grows with the parish* -- the arithmetic episode eleven called unpayable. SWIM's proposal: separate the two jobs the chorus was conflating. *Detection* -- noticing a death -- becomes a constant-size errand each member runs privately. *Dissemination* -- telling everyone -- rides as gossip on the errand's own envelopes. Each member's load is then a constant, sir, regardless of whether the parish numbers fifty or fifty thousand. That is the entire trick, and it is enough.

The set piece, then -- the season's last protocol walked as correspondence, and the cast, I am pleased to report, have consented to appear one final time. The parish: Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, Oofy, and Barmy, five names on each member's roster. Time is cut into *protocol periods* -- a few seconds each; every member runs its own metronome, no agreement about the beat required. Hear one period from Bingo's side of the desk.

The period opens. Bingo consults his roster and selects a target -- not at random, mark this, but next in a private shuffled rotation: every name gets its turn before any name repeats, and we shall see in a moment why that pedantry is a theorem. This period the rotation says Gussie. Bingo writes to Gussie: *ping* -- are you with us? -- and starts the period's stopwatch. Weather one, the common case: Gussie replies *ack*, the errand is done, the period closes with two letters total. Weather two, tonight's interest: no reply inside the timeout. Gussie being Gussie, sir -- the roster's habitually slow member since episode one -- silence proves nothing; crashed-versus-slow is the confusion the course has managed, never solved, since the fallacies were first read aloud. The naive detector convicts here, and episode eleven showed you the fleet-wide panic that follows. SWIM instead does what episode six's nod promised: it asks the neighbours to knock. Bingo selects a handful of parishioners -- Tuppy and Oofy, say -- and writes to each: *ping-req, Gussie* -- would you probe Gussie on my behalf and forward any answer? Tuppy and Oofy each ping Gussie by their own routes. If either receives Gussie's ack, it is relayed to Bingo, and Gussie is cleared -- and notice what was actually tested, sir: *three independent paths* -- Bingo's, Tuppy's, and Oofy's -- so a single sulking link between two healthy machines can no longer manufacture a corpse. The indirect probe is episode one's lesson -- the network is a participant, not a pane of glass -- built into the detector's reflexes.

But suppose all three paths come back empty. Even now, no conviction. Bingo instead publishes a *suspicion*: Gussie is suspected, and the notice goes into the parish's gossip -- of which more in a breath. Suspicion, as episode six's nod foretold, is a state distinct from death, and it is a *summons*, not a verdict: the accused, upon hearing through the grapevine that he is suspected, may object. And here, sir, is the loveliest bone in the protocol's body. How does Gussie's objection outrank the accusation, in a parish with no clock and no chair -- where rumours arrive in any order, duplicated and delayed, per the Post's standing charter? The paper's answer: every member carries an **incarnation number** -- his own, personal, rising number, and the *only* hand permitted to raise it is his. A suspicion cites the incarnation it accuses: *suspect Gussie, incarnation seven*. Gussie's rebuttal declares a higher one: *alive, Gussie, incarnation eight* -- and the parish's rule is simply that a higher incarnation outranks every statement about a lower. The refutation defeats the accusation *wherever the two ever meet*, in any order, at any member, however often duplicated -- no arbiter needed, because rank is carried in the message, not in the timing. It is the season's oldest melody, sir -- the ballot, the term, the epoch, the fencing token -- played one last time, pianissimo, on the smallest instrument in the estate: a gentleman's number that only rises, by which he, and only he, may prove himself fresher than the rumours. And if the suspicion timeout passes with no such rebuttal -- because Barmy, whose turn in the rotation comes later this evening, has actually crashed, it being his role since episode one -- the parish confirms the death: *Barmy, confirmed faulty*, a verdict that closes the file; the departed, having no pen, cannot raise his number, and a machine wrongly convicted rejoins as a fresh member with a fresh start, which is the honest price of a register that must, above all, converge.

Two sentences on the plumbing, because both are episode six returning in parish dress. Dissemination is *infection-style*: the suspicions, rebuttals, confirmations, and joins ride piggyback on the pings and acks the detector is sending anyway -- no broadcast, no extra letters, rumour doubling per period exactly as episode six's epidemiology promised, the freshest news given the front seats in each envelope, each item boarding a bounded number of envelopes -- a few multiples of the roster's logarithm -- before it is retired as common knowledge. A newcomer is the same machinery run forward: he presents himself to any parishioner, receives a copy of the register, and is thereafter news himself; and beneath the piggybacked traffic the working implementations add a slower, fuller ceremony -- an occasional complete exchange of registers between two members over a reliable connection -- anti-entropy beneath the epidemic, episode six's two nets, both cast. And completeness -- the guarantee that no death is *ever* missed -- is precisely why Bingo's rotation was shuffled rather than random: pure chance can neglect a corner of the roster indefinitely, but a rotation visits every name within one full cycle, so a crashed member is *directly probed*, by every live member, within a bounded number of periods -- detection is not merely likely but *certain*, on a schedule; the desk states it as the theorem it is, and computes the pleasant expected case -- first discovery typically inside a period and a half -- by the friendly probability of episode nine.

One refinement, and the federal failure-detection story is complete. HashiCorp's own fleets surfaced the residual embarrassment: the protocol's false accusations traced, disproportionately, not to bad links or dead targets but to *unhealthy accusers* -- a prober mired in a garbage-collection pause misses acks that arrived punctually, and slanders the innocent. Episode eleven named the genre -- the detector measuring itself and calling it the world -- and in two thousand seventeen HashiCorp's research arm published the corrective, with a title of admirable candour: Lifeguard, the detector that suspects itself first. Three amendments, each a sentence. A member keeps a *local health* score -- rising with its own missed acks, with the refusals its probe-proxies report back, with every rebuttal it is forced to issue against rumours of its own death -- and the sicker it scores itself, the *slower* it accuses: probe timeouts stretched, patience widened, on the theory that a gentleman who keeps hearing he is dead should doubt his hearing before the parish's. Suspicion timeouts, meanwhile, *shrink as corroboration arrives* -- one accuser waits long; when several independent members concur, the verdict accelerates, evidence pooling exactly as episode five's honest-witness arithmetic taught. And the buddy rule: a prober who holds a suspicion against the very member it is probing says so *to the suspect, first* -- serving the summons directly, so the rebuttal can begin its journey at once rather than waiting on the grapevine. The measured result, published with the paper: false accusations cut by an order of magnitude and better, detection barely slowed. Episode eleven's suspicion services, sir, priced there as a subscription, here acquire their consumer-protection amendment -- and every Consul agent in every datacenter runs it as the factory setting.

## Part Five -- The Offices of the Registry

Now the offices the two storeys jointly operate, taken at a brisker pace, each priced in the season's coin as we pass.

*The catalog, and health.* A service announces itself to the agent on its own machine; the agent files the registration upward into the parliament's catalog, and -- the federal signature -- *checks the service's health locally*, from the same room: a script, a probe of the port, an application heartbeat, run beside the thing measured, no long-haul link in the loop to slander anyone. Verdicts feed the catalog two ways: the machine's own liveness comes from the parish register -- the gossip verdict, the *serf health* check, in the interface's own vocabulary -- while the service's fitness comes from the local checks, synced upward. And the syncing discipline has a name you already own: *anti-entropy*, episode six's word, used in the manuals in exactly episode six's sense -- the agent periodically compares its local truth against the catalog's copy of its node and repairs the differences, the agent authoritative for its own machine, so a lost update heals on the next comparison rather than festering. Contrast, sir, filed for part seven: Kubernetes convicts a node when its lease in the central ledger goes stale -- presence as bookkeeping; Consul convicts when the parish's distributed jury does -- presence as gossip. Same office; two constitutions.

*Discovery, and the menu of reads.* The estate consults the registry by name -- through a domain-name interface, so that ordinary unmodified software can ask *where is the payments service* and receive the healthy instances -- or through the fuller interface, and every such read faces the question episode seven taught you to ask first: *which consistency am I buying?* Consul's menu makes the modes explicit, and the parallel with part two is exact enough to recite. The *consistent* mode is etcd's default by another name: the leader confirms with a quorum round that his reign extends through the present, then answers -- the read-index ceremony, the strict item on the menu. The *stale* mode is the discount rack: any server answers from local state, leader or not, cheap and scalable and openly possibly-behind -- episode seven's vocabulary printed on the label, and the mode that keeps answers coming even while the parliament is between leaders, which for a *discovery* service under fire is a mercy worth its staleness. And the *default* mode, sir -- the default is the interesting entry. The leader answers from local state, *without* the quorum round, trusting a leader lease: the arrangement episode five priced under reads-under-leadership, correct exactly insofar as the drift assumption holds, and the manual is admirably plain about the residue -- within a small window around a leadership change, the deposed may briefly answer with yesterday's truth. Writes are never so exposed; reads, by default, ride the lease. Set the two houses side by side, and the constitutional temperaments are visible in the defaults alone: etcd, the unitary book of record, defaults to *strict* and sells the discount; Consul, the federal directory, defaults to *fast under a lease* and sells the certainty. Neither is wrong, sir. A book of record and a directory enquiries desk *should* price truth differently -- but the review that has not read the label is running on the vendor's temperament rather than its own, and now you have read both labels.

*Watching, federally.* Consul's tenants also wait on changes -- but recall part four: the store keeps no history. There is no revision drawer to cursor over, so the office is run on the other philosophy: the *blocking query*. Every read reply carries an index -- the position in the Raft log that produced the answering state. The client asks again, quoting the index: *answer when anything beyond this*. The desk holds the letter -- minutes, if nothing stirs -- and replies with the *new current state* and its newer index the moment the relevant corner changes, or with the old index at timeout. Long polling, the trade says. And mark what the reply is, sir: the *state*, not the story. Three changes landing in quick succession may return as one reply bearing only the final shape -- intermediate editions coalesced, unobservable, the store having kept no negatives. This is **level-triggered** service -- the word from part three, now doing comparative work: Consul hands you the level; etcd hands you the reel. Neither is a defect, and the desk's problems make you argue both sides: the reel gives replayable history -- audit, exact resumption, the informer's economy -- and pays for it in compaction rent and drawer space; the level gives a store that never owes you the past and cannot disappoint you about it -- nothing to compact, nothing to run out of -- and pays by making every consumer level-triggered whether it likes it or not: the reconciliation posture, mandatory. You will notice with some pleasure that the industry's two flagship coordination services, having made opposite storage choices, were thereby *forced* into opposite watch philosophies -- the anatomy determining the diet, as anatomy does.

*Sessions, locks, and an old acquaintance.* Consul's locks rest on the *session*: a liveness contract binding together a time-to-live, the node's gossip verdict, and any local checks the client nominates -- invalidate any strand and the session dies. Locks are then key acquisition under a session: take the key citing the session, hold while it lives, and on death the lock releases -- Chubby's master-lease tenancy, episode five's furniture, faithfully reproduced. And when a session is invalidated *by the system* -- the holder condemned, rightly or wrongly -- Consul does something episode five's listeners will greet by name. Hear the crypt scene once in federal dress, because the staging is the old one exactly. Bingo holds the payments lock under his session. Bingo pauses, heroically, in the established manner. The parish probes, suspects, convicts; the gossip verdict lands; the session -- that bundle of verdicts -- is invalidated, and the lock releases. But Tuppy, arriving eager, finds the key *unlettable*: for fifteen seconds by default, the house declines to re-grant it -- a *lock-delay*, and the documentation states both the purpose and the pedigree without embarrassment: a safeguard *inspired by Google's Chubby*, buying time for a possibly-still-living Bingo to notice his own invalidation and stand down before a successor begins. You recognize the reasoning instantly, sir, and its limit just as fast: this is the resurrection problem managed by *postponement* -- the window narrowed, not closed; courtesy, not proof. The proof remains where episode five nailed it: at the resource. And Consul offers that too, for tenants who ask: every key carries a modify index -- the store's rising number, by yet another name -- and the interface offers check-and-set writes against it; the desk sets the construction as a problem, because after two anatomies of the same skeleton, sir, you no longer need it walked. Two houses, one sermon: the delay is a kindness; the fence is a guarantee; know which one your recipe is standing on.

*The wider map, briskly.* Consul federates across datacenters in the federal manner: the servers of each datacenter join a second, wide-area gossip pool, so every parliament knows of every other; a query for a remote datacenter's services is *forwarded* to that datacenter's servers, and answered from there. Attend to what is and is not promised: each datacenter keeps its own Raft group and its own catalog -- its own ledger, sovereign; federation *routes* questions, it does not replicate answers. Episode three's triage, applied with both eyes open: within a datacenter, consistency is obtained from the local parliament, at the local parliament's price; across the world, Consul declines the continental consensus that episode eight priced in round trips, and a partitioned datacenter simply continues governing itself -- availability by devolution, staleness across the sea, the choice printed honestly on the architecture. And two closing curiosities from the parish, one paragraph the pair. The gossip layer moonlights as a *surveyor*: every agent maintains coordinates in a small invented geometry -- the Vivaldi scheme, from the networking literature -- nudged at each gossip exchange by measured round trips, until the distance between any two members' coordinates *predicts* their network latency by pure arithmetic, no probe required; ask the registry for the *nearest* healthy instance and it is answering from that invented map. The estate measured itself, sir, by gossiping about the mail. And atop the registry HashiCorp later grew a service mesh -- identity certificates, encrypted sidecar-to-sidecar mail, intentions declaring who may call whom -- the registry office issuing passports; noted here so sir has the term, and declined further, per the season's discipline about doors and corridors.

## Part Six -- The Kernel's Blast Radius

Episode five preached *rent, don't build*, and stands by it. But a doctrine honestly taught includes its own invoice, and the coordination kernel's invoice has a line the season priced abstractly that tonight can price from a published ledger: when the kernel fails, *what exactly have you lost?* The exhibit is the Roblox affair, and it is on the postmortem shelf by right: a joint account, published by the operator with its vendor's cooperation, and unusually candid arithmetic.

The facts, from the published account. Roblox -- a games platform whose daily population is tens of millions -- runs its estate on the HashiCorp stack, with a single Consul cluster at the centre -- the servers, machines of sixty-four cores, lately given a new streaming feature: a cheaper fan-out path for the blocking-query load of part five. On the twenty-eighth of October, two thousand twenty-one, under unusually high read-and-write load, the cluster's performance collapsed -- and the estate followed it down, whole. Service discovery *is* the switchboard, sir: when the registry cannot answer *where is the payments service*, it does not matter that the payments service is in perfect health. The outage ran seventy-three hours -- three days and an evening -- and the recovery's difficulty is itself the exhibit's first lesson: the operator's *telemetry* also depended on the ailing Consul cluster. The estate could not see the failure through the failure -- the observability tenanting with the patient, episode one's partial-failure fog made architectural, and the reason diagnosis consumed days rather than hours.

Two causes, compounding, both instructive to the season's eye. The first: the new streaming path, under that load, developed *contention* -- in the published account, traffic fighting over the concurrency plumbing within the servers -- throughput collapsing not from want of resources but from the fighting itself. The operators did the natural thing, doubling the hardware -- sixty-four cores to one hundred twenty-eight -- and it did not help, sir, *and you know why it did not help*: episode eleven taught that a system degraded by internal contention does not yield to capacity; the queues at the contended point simply refill. Recognize the physiognomy -- a stable degraded state, sustained from within, indifferent to the removal of its trigger: metastability, walking the production floor with its papers in order. The second cause sat lower, in the disk-backed bindery from part four. Consul's Raft log lives in BoltDB, and BoltDB keeps its own registry of reclaimed pages -- a freelist -- which, in the version deployed, it *rewrote to disk in its entirety at every commit*. The load spike had swollen the file; the freelist had grown to millions of entries; and thereafter every small log append -- a few kilobytes of actual parliamentary business -- was paying a bookkeeping tax of *megabytes*, the ledger spending its strength writing out, again and again, the ever-longer catalogue of its own empty pages. The house's own filing system, sir, become the load. Leadership grew unstable under the strain -- elections, in a parliament whose disk is drowning, resolve nothing, for each new leader inherits the same bindery -- and the pair of causes, interleaved, resisted diagnosis precisely because *neither alone explained the weather*. The recovery, in the end, was the honest sequence the postmortem lays out: streaming disabled; the pathology understood; slow leaders circumvented; the estate restarted with care. And the coda that earns tonight's structure its keep: the freelist pathology was a known one, sir, *repaired in bbolt* -- the fork of the very same library in which etcd binds its ledger. The two full-time parliaments keep their books in sibling binderies, and the affliction of one had already been cured in the other's; the remedy existed, published, a fork away. The field's lessons travel by lineage, and sometimes the lineage forks just upstream of your outage.

The findings, in the operator's own subsequent conduct and the season's vocabulary. Too much rested on one cluster: discovery, health, locks, configuration, *and the eyes* -- so the remedy was federal even before it was technical: split the kernel's tenancy by concern, house the telemetry with a different landlord, bound each cluster's blast radius to what its failure can be allowed to cost. Episode five's catechism said rent the kernel; tonight appends the rider in the same hand: *and read the lease's liability clause* -- the kernel is the estate's single point of *correlated* failure by design, that being what centralizing coordination means; the design review that centralizes must therefore also draw the circle of what stops when the centre does, and check, with particular suspicion, that the instruments for diagnosing the centre do not sit inside the circle. The parliament may fail, sir. It is machinery. The estate's obligation is merely to ensure that when it does, somebody can still see, and something is still standing outside the ring.

## Part Seven -- The Comparative Anatomy

The two anatomies side by side, then, read briskly down the table as a design review would, six rows.

*Where the state lives.* etcd: on disk, with history -- the ledger with a filing cabinet, as-of reads and all. Consul: in memory, current edition only, the log alone durable. *How the estate's membership is known.* etcd: it isn't -- etcd minds its own five members and no more; the tenants' fleets are the tenants' problem, which is why Kubernetes built lease-based heartbeats above it -- presence as centralized bookkeeping. Consul: the parish register -- presence by gossip, detection at the edge, constant load per member, Lifeguard's humility included. *How one watches.* etcd: the reel -- every event, in order, resumable by revision, compaction rent due. Consul: the level -- blocking queries, current state at a newer index, coalescing freely, nothing owed about the past. *What a read costs by default.* etcd: the strict item -- linearizable via the read-index ceremony, discount by request. Consul: the leader's lease -- fast, with the manual's own small window, strictness by request. *What guards a lock.* Both houses: a session-or-lease tenancy; both offer the true fence -- the store's rising number, revision in one dialect, modify index in the other, compared at the point of effect; Consul adds Chubby's courtesy delay for tenants who cannot present tokens. *How far it federates.* etcd: one parliament; stretch it and you are stretching consensus itself, paying episode eight's round trips on every decree. And the choosing, which is the review's actual question. If you are in Kubernetes, sir, the choice was made for you, and made well -- but tonight you know *what* was chosen, which is the difference between a tenant and an operator: a strict, history-keeping book of record, sized for small hot metadata, with the informer ritual as the approved way to drink from it. Choose the same temperament wherever the workload is a book of record: configuration that must not fork, elections, the estate's load-bearing facts -- small, hot, and worth strictness at the default. Choose Consul's temperament where the workload is a *directory*: large heterogeneous fleets, services coming and going, health checked where things live, queries answered near and fast, datacenters plural and sovereign -- a registry may be seconds stale and still be doing its whole job, and a constitution that admits this at the counter will run circles, operationally, around one that pretends otherwise. And ZooKeeper, the incumbent of episode five, still governs its historic estates faithfully -- while its most famous tenant, you recall from episode ten, came of age and folded the parliament inside; the industry's plumbing, slowly, agreeing with its theory. What is *not* on offer, from any house, is exemption from the season: every one of these services is a lease on the same mathematics, sir -- the same crowded club, the same fine print on reads, the same corpse at the same door with the same two exorcists -- and the vendor's brochure differs from the theorem only in typeface.

Item one. Neither geometry is safe; each is *shaped*, and the shape belongs in the design, not in the incident review.

Item two. Name the mode *aloud* in the review, look up what the house serves when you fail to specify, and price the default like the contract term it is; the most expensive words in the estate remain *I assumed*.

Item three.
