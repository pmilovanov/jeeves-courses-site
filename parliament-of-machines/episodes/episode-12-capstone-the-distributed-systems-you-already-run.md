# The Parliament of Machines

*An Audio Course in Distributed Systems — Episode Twelve: Capstone — The Distributed Systems You Already Run*

## The Minutes of Sir's Own Seminar

Good day, sir.

The largest coordinated computations in the history of the species are not the banks. They are not the search engines, nor the exchanges, nor the payment networks whose machinery this course has spent eleven evenings dissecting. They are **training runs**: thousands upon thousands of accelerators, in lockstep, for weeks and months at a stretch, exchanging more data per second among themselves than the estates of earlier episodes moved in a day — agreeing, every few seconds, on a single enormous object, and doing it again, and again, millions of times, with a discipline that would make episode four's parliament weep with professional envy. Nothing humanity has built coordinates more machines more tightly for longer — not the telephone network in its imperial noon, not the exchanges at their most feverish, not the planetary databases this course has toured with such affection. The reader of gradients has out-scaled them all, largely without noticing, the way empires are acquired in fits of absence of mind. And here is the observation on which the whole finale balances, delivered with what I hope sir will receive as affectionate impertinence: *you have been chairing this seminar your entire career, and nobody ever showed you the minutes.*

Weigh the claim before granting it, because it is not rhetorical. A frontier training run coordinates tens of thousands of accelerators across thousands of hosts and multiple halls; it moves gradient traffic measured in terabits per second, sustained, for millions of consecutive rounds; it survives — must survive, on the arithmetic Part Five will do aloud — hardware failures on a daily schedule without losing the plot of a computation that has been running since spring. The banks' settlement systems are marvels of care over modest traffic; the search engines are marvels of scale over forgiving queries, where a dropped result is a shrug. The training run alone demands both at once: the scale of the largest estates and the lockstep coherence of the tightest, with a single logical object — the model — that every machine must agree about, continuously, or the entire investment diverges into noise. If this course's subject has a summit, sir, you are standing on it during working hours.

For eleven evenings this course has worn the costume of other people's infrastructure — carts and ledgers, brokers and shards — while sir, of all listeners, operates the genuine article daily: the training cluster, the data pipeline, the checkpoint schedule, the serving fleet. Tonight the costume comes off. Every instrument this season has built — the rounds and the quorums, the logs and the photographs, the stragglers and the storms, the wager and the join — is running, at this hour, inside the machine-learning estate, mostly under assumed names, and the finale's business is to read the aliases aloud. The itinerary: the dictionary, item by item, each entry landing on machinery from a named episode — the wonder beat of the evening, placed first, because everything after it is elaboration; data parallelism, with the ring all-reduce narrated as the set piece, parcels passed around a circle until the arithmetic delivers its punchline; asynchrony's bargain, in which the season's oldest debt — commutativity forgives disorder, issued the very first evening — makes its final payment; the geometry of model and pipeline parallelism, which is episode nine wearing silicon; failure at scale — the mean-time-between-failures arithmetic that makes checkpointing a schedule rather than a virtue, the Young–Daly cadence, the return of the racing duplicates, and the season's one deliberately deferred horror, the core that miscomputes without confessing; a short word on serving, where a consistency bug earns a profit-and-loss line; and then, sir, the grand audit — the register ruled and closed, every debt paid or declared — and the arc retraced from a hill outside a besieged city to a hall of ten thousand accelerators, before the door is closed. A word on how to listen, since the finale's grammar differs from every evening before it. There are few new theorems tonight — two, honestly: a bandwidth bound and a square root — and no new impossibilities at all; the finale's work is *recognition*, and recognition is best performed at conversational speed, nodding where the machinery is familiar, which by now is everywhere. Where the previous evenings asked sir to learn, tonight asks sir merely to *see* — and then, at the audit, to watch the accounts balance, as one does at the end of a well-kept year. No teaser tonight. Tonight the course keeps its last promise, which was to end.

## Part One — The Dictionary

The device is borrowed, avowedly, from a sibling course's finale, and it earns its keep here twice over. I shall read the machine-learning estate's vocabulary as a dictionary, sir, each entry translated into this season's terms, with the episode number attached like a pressed flower. Attend to the cumulative effect, because it is the wonder beat: not any single entry, but the dawning, somewhere around the fifth, that *there is nothing else in the building* — that the field sir works in is not adjacent to distributed systems but is made, joist by joist, of this course. And permit the observation of why the disguise held so long: the machine-learning trade hired its infrastructure vocabulary from a different lineage — high-performance computing, with its collectives and its ranks and its barriers — a tradition that grew up on reliable, private, exquisite networks and therefore never needed this course's paranoia until, at modern scale, on commodity failure schedules, it suddenly did. Two dialects, one mathematics; the dictionary merely performs the introduction the two communities were too busy to arrange for themselves.

**A training step is a round.** The synchronous world of episode four's rented order: compute, exchange, agree, advance, in lockstep, the whole fleet moving through numbered rounds — steps — with a barrier at each boundary. Episode one taught that the world is not synchronous and episode four taught what synchrony costs to rent; the training loop rents it wholesale, round after round, millions of times, and pays in the renter's eternal coin: the round lasts as long as its slowest participant, which is why the straggler is this estate's national enemy and Part Five's whole business. Sir has, in effect, been running the most synchronous large system on earth — the model of computation episode three used as a *thought experiment* is sir's Tuesday.

**Gradient accumulation is batching** — the oldest trade in the ledger: fold several rounds of local work into one round of agreement, amortizing the barrier and the parcels across more computation; every estate this season made the same purchase, from group commit in episode five's log to the batched sequencers of episode eight, and the exchange rate — latency for throughput — has never once varied.

**An all-reduce is agreement on a sum** — and here the dictionary earns its first fine distinction, one this evening will spend a whole part on: agreement on a sum is *strictly weaker than consensus*. Episode three forbade deterministic asynchronous consensus because consensus must arbitrate — pick one value, exclude the others. A sum excludes nobody: every contribution is folded in, order irrelevant, and there is nothing to arbitrate — no race, in episode six's language, and the race test was always the border. Weaker demands, cheaper machinery: the all-reduce runs at wire speed precisely because it is not a parliament, and the estate keeps an actual parliament on retainer — the job controller — for the questions that *are* decisions: who is a member, which checkpoint is blessed, who leads. Two agreement products, two prices, one building; knowing which of the two you are paying for, at every layer, is the whiteboard's second item tonight.

**A checkpoint is a crash-recovery snapshot** — the Chandy–Lamport spirit's third appearance, after episode two's theory and episode ten's streaming costume: a consistent photograph of the model's state, taken so that death becomes rewind. The training loop's version is disarmingly easy — the barrier between steps is a *global quiescent instant*, episode ten's alignment problem pre-solved by the synchrony already purchased; the season's hardest photograph, of a river that would not hold still, is here a formality, because this river agrees to hold still sixty times a minute — and the interesting question moves from how to photograph to *how often*, which is Part Five's arithmetic, and *how cheaply*, which is Part Five's engineering.

**A straggler is episode ten's unluckiest machine**, reborn on silicon: the accelerator that runs a few percent slow — thermals, a failing memory module, a noisy neighbour on the interconnect — and, in a barrier-synchronized world, sets the pace for ten thousand peers. The backup-task remedy planted in episode ten comes due tonight, with interest.

**Parameter staleness is replication lag.** The worker computing gradients against weights three versions old is episode six's stale follower, serving reads behind the primary; every convergence argument about bounded staleness is a session-guarantee negotiation wearing optimization clothes, and the asynchronous schools of Part Three are, in the field guide's exact taxonomy, eventual-plus-what with the what denominated in steps of drift — the catechism of episode seven, answered in an optimizer's currency.

**Elastic training is a view change.** Members join, members fail, the job continues: episode four's reconfiguration and episode five's dragons, at rack scale, with the membership epoch stamped on every collective so that a straggler from the old view cannot corrupt the new — the fencing token, sir, in its final uniform, and the season's most-travelled instrument: five episodes, five costumes, one idea, which is that authority must expire visibly before it may be reassigned safely.

**And the data pipeline is the log** — episode ten entire: the shuffled, sharded, replayable stream of training data, read by cursors, rewound for reproducibility, the input leg of the recovery trinity holding up the largest computations on earth.

Three shorter entries, for the pleasure of the cadence. **The job scheduler is episode nine's placement church**: deciding which accelerators host which shards of which job, against failure domains and interconnect locality — the seating chart, drawn at gigawatt scale, with the same commandment about not putting a parliament's replicas on one switch. **The feature store is a materialized view** — episode ten's inside-out doctrine sold as a product: one definition, one log, every consumer a cursor. **And the training metrics are the watermark**: the loss curve is the computation's own confession of progress and health, monitored the way episode ten monitored time and episode eleven monitored everything — the invariant that tells you, before any machine confesses, that something in the estate has begun to lie.

Ten entries, ten episodes, and the dictionary could run to fifty. The estate's every noun translates, sir, and the translation is not metaphor — it is *identity*, the same mathematics under different invoices, which is precisely why this course has claimed since its founding hour that the training cluster is the distributed system sir already runs. And the dictionary confers a practical privilege beyond the pleasure of recognition: it makes forty years of other people's postmortems *sir's* postmortems — every lesson the databases and the queues and the caches purchased with outages now reads directly onto the cluster, name by translated name, and an estate that treats its stragglers like episode eleven's timeouts and its checkpoints like episode ten's photographs inherits the corrections without paying for the incidents. The remainder of the evening simply reads the longest entries in full.

## Part Two — Data Parallelism, and the Parcels Passed Round

Begin with the workhorse: **data parallelism**, synchronous stochastic gradient descent across the fleet. Every worker holds a complete copy of the model — replication, episode six, full factor; each round, every worker computes a gradient on its own shard of data — partitioning, episode nine, of the work rather than the parameters; and then the fleet must agree on the *average* gradient before anyone may step. Pause on why the average is the entire conversation, because it is the estate's one point of true coupling: the mathematics wants the gradient of the whole batch, which is exactly the mean of the workers' shard-gradients — so the fleet's agreement object is a *sum*, arrived at by pure accumulation, no arbitration, no winner; and once agreed, every replica applies the identical update to identical weights and the copies stay identical *by determinism rather than by reconciliation* — episode five's state machine replication, verbatim: agree on the input, derive the state, never ship it. One logical model, ten thousand physical copies, coherent for months without a single anti-entropy repair — the determinism diet's masterpiece. And there is the round barrier, episode four, at which all of tonight's economics live. The naive agreement is a parameter server: ship every gradient to a central estate, sum there, ship the result back — episode nine's directory church, and workable; Li and colleagues' two thousand fourteen paper made it an architecture, with the server tier itself sharded — each server owning a range of the parameters, episode nine again — and the workers pushing and pulling shards in parallel. Price it with the course's arithmetic: a thousand workers, each shipping a full gradient up and a full model down each round, means the server tier moves two thousand ledgers per round while each worker moves two — the fan-in is the ceiling, the ceiling is bought in server bandwidth, and it does not rise as workers join unless the server estate grows in proportion: a tax collected per round, forever. The trade wanted the sum computed *by the fleet itself*, with no central post office — and the answer, elegant enough that the constitution reserves it the set piece, came from an unglamorous corner: high-performance computing had been passing parcels around rings since before the web.

Now the set piece, sir: **the ring all-reduce**, narrated with the company in a circle, parcels in hand, and the arithmetic done aloud with small numbers until the punchline detonates. Stage: Bingo, Tuppy, Gussie, and Barmy, four machines in a ring, each holding its own gradient — think of each gradient as a ledger of, say, four hundred pages, and cut each ledger into four parcels, one parcel per machine in the ring. The algorithm has two acts. **Act one, reduce-scatter, three passes.** On the first pass, each machine sends *one parcel* — a hundred pages — to its clockwise neighbour: Bingo sends parcel one to Tuppy, Tuppy sends parcel two to Gussie, Gussie parcel three to Barmy, Barmy parcel four to Bingo — all four links busy at once, note, nobody idle, no central post office. Each recipient *adds* the arriving parcel to its own corresponding pages — a running sum — and on the second pass forwards the *sum* onward: Tuppy sends Bingo-plus-Tuppy's parcel one to Gussie, who adds his own and, on the third pass, sends the three-way sum to Barmy, who adds his and now holds parcel one *complete* — the full four-machine sum of those hundred pages. And by the symmetry of the ring, after those three passes *each* machine holds one complete parcel: Barmy has the grand total of parcel one, Bingo of parcel two, and so round the circle — the sum, scattered. **Act two, all-gather, three passes more:** each machine sends its completed parcel around the ring, no more adding, just copying — Barmy launches the finished parcel one to Bingo, who forwards it to Tuppy while receiving parcel two's total from around the other way, every link still humming — until after three passes everyone holds all four completed parcels: the entire summed gradient, everywhere, and every machine may step its copy of the model in the same instant, which is the barrier's release and the round's end. Six passes, two acts, one sum, no post office: the ceremony the estate performs sixty times a minute for a season, and the reason the season finishes.

Pause on what just happened, sir, before the arithmetic, because the choreography's two tricks are easy to admire past. First: at no moment did any machine hold, or need to hold, anybody's *full* ledger but its own — the sums travelled as parcels, each link carrying a fraction, which is how the scheme dodges every fan-in ceiling: there is no place where everything converges, because the convergence itself was partitioned — episode nine's doctrine applied not to data at rest but to the agreement in flight. Second: every link worked every pass — four machines, four links, all four humming simultaneously in both acts; the ring wastes neither wire nor waiting, and that full employment is precisely where the punchline's economy comes from. Now the arithmetic, slowly, because the punchline deserves its drumroll. Each machine sent one parcel per pass — a hundred pages — for six passes: six hundred pages sent, against a ledger of four hundred. Add machines, sir: with ten machines, parcels of a tenth of the ledger, nine passes of reduce and nine of gather — eighteen tenths: one point eight ledgers. With a hundred machines: one hundred ninety-eight hundredths. With ten thousand: within a whisker of exactly two. **Total traffic per machine approaches twice the size of the gradient — regardless of how many machines join the ring.** Say it again, plainly: ten machines or ten thousand, each participant sends and receives roughly two ledgers' worth, no more, and every link in the ring is busy every pass — no bottleneck machine, no idle wire, the sum of ten thousand contributions computed for the postage of two. And hear the optimality argument's spine aloud, because it fits in the ear and turns the punchline from a trick into a law. Every machine must *receive* the parts of the sum it did not compute — very nearly one full ledger's worth of information, since its own contribution is only one part in the fleet; and every machine's contribution must *leave* it — another ledger, outbound, for its pages to reach the total everyone must hold. One ledger in, one ledger out, at minimum, for any algorithm whatsoever that ends with everyone holding everything: the two-ledger bound is not the ring's cleverness but the problem's own floor, and the ring's achievement is merely — merely! — to reach the floor while keeping every link busy every pass. The desk does it properly, with the near-tight constants; the proof is elementary and satisfying, exactly as the constitution promised. The courier service that runs this ceremony on real racks is NCCL — spoken *nickel*, sir, as the industry insists and the lexicon has recorded since episode one — a library whose entire profession is the season's Part Two conducted at nanosecond stakes: discovering the fleet's topology, choosing among rings and trees (the tree variants trading a little bandwidth for logarithmic latency, the ring's mirror bargain, chosen when the parcels are small and the ring's passes would be all overhead), pipelining the parcels so the wires never breathe, and doing it all again when the membership epoch turns. Scale the parcel arithmetic to the frontier once, for awe's sake: a large model's gradient weighs hundreds of gigabytes; the two-ledger bound prices each round at roughly twice that per machine; and the loop demands a round every few seconds — sustained, per machine, day and night for a season, which is why the interconnect is the training estate's costliest citizen after the accelerators themselves, and why an algorithm that reaches the bandwidth floor is not an optimization, sir, but the difference between the computation existing and not. Sir has been linking a distributed-systems textbook into every training binary for years; it merely declined to introduce itself — and its schedulers layer rings within rings — a blistering ring across the pod's internal fabric, a slower ring across the fibres between pods, reduce within, exchange between, gather back down — hierarchical all-reduces whose budgeting is one of the desk's problems, and whose design rule is episode nine's oldest: put the heavy traffic on the short wires. One honest caveat completes the set piece, and sir has been waiting for it: the ring is a barrier in miniature — every pass waits for the slowest link and the slowest machine, so the ring's magnificent bandwidth is bought at the price of maximal exposure to Part Five's straggler; the parcel arithmetic assumed everyone keeps step, and keeping ten thousand machines in step is precisely the discipline the rest of tonight must fund.

The parameter server, then — Li and colleagues, two thousand fourteen — was it merely the wrong church? Not at all, and the comparison is episode nine's fan-out sentence at silicon speed: the ring buys bandwidth optimality and pays in *synchrony* — a ring pass is a barrier in miniature, every member in step, one straggler slowing all; the parameter server buys *asynchrony* — workers push and pull at their own pace against the central copy — and pays in fan-in bandwidth and in the staleness Part Three prices. And the server church keeps one virtue the ring cannot counterfeit: **elasticity**. A ring is a topology — a member's death breaks it, a member's arrival re-forms it, each transition a view change with a pause; the server pattern is a shopfront — workers come and go as customers, unannounced, and the estate barely glances up, which is why the elastic and the preemptible provinces still worship there. Topology is destiny, sir: the collective for the tight synchronous core, the server pattern where elasticity and asynchrony matter more than the last drop of wire — and modern estates, predictably, deploy both, per layer, which is the whiteboard's first item forming.

## Part Three — Asynchrony's Bargain, and the Season's Oldest Debt

Now the payment eleven evenings in the making. The synchronous loop's tax is the barrier; the tempting escape is to stop waiting: let each worker read the current weights, compute its gradient, and apply it — *whenever it arrives*, against whatever weights now stand, no barrier, no round, no agreement at all. Surely, sir, after this course, every instinct rebels: updates applied against versions they were not computed from, interleavings no one ordered, the lost-update crime scene of episode six re-enacted at every step? Picture it staged, because the image is the argument: a single great ledger open on the counter, and every teller in the bank writing corrections into it simultaneously — no queue, no clerk of order, pens colliding, entries landing on entries — a scene that eleven episodes of this course have trained sir to flee from. The astonishment — published as **Hogwild** by Recht and colleagues in two thousand eleven, the name a confession — is that it *works*: run the updates with no locks whatsoever, let them collide, and under honest assumptions about sparsity and step size, convergence survives, at nearly full speed; the tellers' pandemonium and the tidy single-file counter arrive, statistically, at the same book. The paper's own conditions deserve their clause, because the astonishment is disciplined, not magical: the analysis leans on *sparsity* — each update touching few parameters, so that colliding updates mostly touch different pages of the ledger and true interference is rare — plus the step sizes' built-in humility; dense, violently overlapping updates enjoy no such indulgence, and the trade learned the boundary empirically where the theorem's assumptions ran out. But within its province the result stands, and the reason, sir — the reason is the season's oldest promissory note, presented at last. **Addition commutes.** A gradient update is an *increment*, not an overwrite: apply mine then yours, or yours then mine, and the sum is the sum; the interleaving that destroys a read-modify-write ledger merely *reorders contributions* to an accumulation, and an accumulation does not care. Episode one's replicated counter forgave the Post's disorder because addition commutes; episode seven's kettles wed under a join that forgot order because forgetting order was the design; and here, at the summit of the industry, the largest optimization processes ever run survive raw, lockless concurrency for *exactly the same reason the shopping cart survived it*. Debt number four, issued in the first hour of the season: the first half paid at the kettle wedding with a semilattice; the remainder paid tonight, wearing gradients.

And the honesty clause, sounded as deliberately as the rhyme, because the constitution requires the difference stated as plainly as the likeness. This is *rhyme, not theorem*. Gradients are not a semilattice: an update is not idempotent — apply mine twice and the model has moved twice; there is no join, no once-absorbed-always-absorbed, and episode seven's convergence theorem does not apply. What holds instead is an *analytic* tolerance: the optimization, being itself a noisy, stochastic, error-correcting process, absorbs bounded disorder as one more source of noise — staleness within limits behaves like a slightly clumsier learning rate, and the guarantees are probabilistic convergence results with conditions attached, not algebraic identities. The desk states them with pointers rather than proofs, per the honesty clause: the mathematics is real and belongs to another course. A word on where the bargain's fashion has settled, because the pendulum has a familiar arc. The asynchronous school flourished when fleets were small, heterogeneous, and rented; the frontier estates, running purpose-built pods with engineered uniformity, marched back to full synchrony — the barrier's tax fell as the hardware homogenized, and coherence bought things the loss curve rewards: cleaner convergence, comparable experiments, and — the season's recurring cuisine — *determinism*, or its close approximation, for debugging runs that cost more than office buildings. Episode eight's Calvin aside, cashed: agree on inputs, compute predictably, and the largest labs concluded, like the databases before them, that the cleanest deal in the building was worth its narrowness. And one caveat the practitioner meets before the theorist does: **floating-point addition does not associate.** Sum the same gradients in a different order and the last bits differ; the ring's reduction order, the number of workers, the very topology change the arithmetic — two runs from the same seed on differently shaped fleets diverge, bit by bit, then visibly, and the engineer who spends a week hunting a "bug" that is actually the associativity of nothing is a rite of passage the trade could label better. Whence the discipline: bitwise-reproducible training requires fixing the reduction order — and episode five's determinism diet, of all things, turns out to matter in a field whose mathematics pretends addition is addition; the seeded universe of episode eleven would approve. Commutativity forgives disorder, sir — approximately, in the mean, with conditions — and the mature position is the negotiated middle the trade calls **stale-synchronous**: workers may run ahead of the slowest by at most a bounded number of steps — a staleness window, enforced at the barrier — so the ring's coherence and Hogwild's tolerance meet in a contract sir can now read at sight: bounded staleness is episode seven's honest tier; the window is a session guarantee for gradients; and the knob is priced, as every knob this season has been, in the only trade that exists — wait for coherence, or drift for speed, with the drift's cost measured in convergence rather than in customer complaints. Episode seven's hierarchy, one more time, wearing an optimizer's clothes. And asynchrony keeps honest provinces where its premises hold, stated for balance: fleets too heterogeneous to march — spot instances, mixed generations, the rented and the begged — where a barrier would idle the strong behind the weak all day; and the federated frontier, learning across devices that are episode one's documentary weather personified — phones sleeping, dropping, lying about their clocks — where synchrony is not expensive but *meaningless*, and the parameter-server pattern, staleness budgets and all, remains the only sane church. Per layer, as ever, sir; the whiteboard is already writing itself.

## Part Four — The Geometry: Model, Pipeline, and the Optimizer's Estate

When the model itself outgrows one machine's memory, the season's ninth evening takes over entirely, and this part is short because sir can now derive it. The frontier estates run all three geometries at once, composed like episode nine's matrix grown a dimension: tensor shards within a pod, pipeline stages across pods, data-parallel replicas across the hall — every accelerator addressed by its coordinates in a three-axis seating chart, and the collectives arranged so that each axis's chatter stays on the wires that can afford it. The composition is the design problem; the axes themselves sir already owns. **Tensor parallelism**: shard the individual weight matrices across accelerators — range partitioning of the parameter space itself — so that each layer's arithmetic becomes a small collective among the shard-holders, fired *within* every forward and backward pass: agreement not per round but per layer, thousands of little all-reduces a second, which is why this sharding lives strictly inside a pod, on the interconnect measured in terabits, and dies of latency anywhere else. Locality of reference now means *which accelerators share a matrix*, and the placement rule — keep the chattiest shards on the fastest interconnect — is episode nine's co-location doctrine with the speed of light billing per centimetre. **Pipeline parallelism**: shard the model by *layers* — stage one holds the early layers, stage two the middle, and so on — and stream micro-batches through the stages like parcels on a conveyor, the schedules themselves — when each stage runs forward work, when backward, one of each in steady alternation in the classic arrangement — being small masterpieces of episode ten's discipline: dataflow scheduling with the operators pinned to silicon and the buffers audited to the megabyte. The tax appears immediately and has episode ten's shape: the **pipeline bubble** — stages idle at the head and tail of every round while the conveyor fills and drains. Do the shape of the arithmetic once: with eight stages and eight micro-batches in flight, the conveyor spends roughly half its area filling and draining — a utilization catastrophe; with eight stages and sixty-four micro-batches, the bubbles shrink toward an eighth of the round — the classic remedy of keeping many parcels airborne, bought with activation memory, which is the same buffer-versus-latency ledger episode ten kept for its rivers, with the operators nailed to specific silicon and the backpressure enforced by the conveyor itself. And **the optimizer's estate**: the revelation, industrialized as ZeRO — spoken *zero*, per the lexicon's founding entries — and its fully-sharded descendants, that the *heaviest* tenants were never the model's weights but the optimizer's bookkeeping — the momenta, the variances, the master copies, several times the model's own weight — replicated, in the naive data-parallel arrangement, on every one of ten thousand workers: the same pages, ten thousand times, the most redundant library since episode six taught replication for *durability*, a rationale conspicuously absent here. The repair proceeds in the staged discipline of a good rebalance: shard the optimizer state first — each worker keeps only its slice of the bookkeeping, gathering nothing, since each slice updates from the summed gradient everyone already holds; then the gradients; then the weights themselves, gathered layer by layer just in time for each layer's arithmetic and released behind it — a working set streamed through memory the way episode ten streamed rivers, with the collectives doing the fetching on a schedule. The memory bill falls by the fleet size; the payment is more traffic on wires the ring already taught you to budget. Partition the state, not merely the data, sir: where things live, one final time, deciding what fits at all.

## Part Five — Failure on a Schedule

Now the arithmetic that turns this course's whole climate into a calendar entry, done aloud as the constitution instructs. The estate's components are individually excellent — that must be said first, because the arithmetic that follows is not an indictment of any part but of multiplication itself. Take a single accelerator-and-its-attendants and grant it a mean time between failures of, say, five years — generous, and the roundness will not matter. Put ten thousand of them in one job. Five years is roughly sixty months: the fleet suffers, on average, ten thousand failures per sixty months — better than a hundred and sixty a month, north of five a day: **one failure every four or five hours, on schedule, forever.** And the estimate is generous twice over, sir: the parts age, the new parts arrive with fabrication's infant troubles, and the failures that matter include everything episode one catalogued beyond the silicon — the optics, the switches, the software of the attendants — so the practitioners' observed cadence at frontier scale runs faster than this arithmetic, not slower. The direction of the error only sharpens the conclusion. Failure at this scale is not an if but a cadence, sir — episode one's climate, quantified — and a job that loses everything at each interruption never finishes at all. Run the schedule against a job, sir, to feel it: a month-long run on that fleet expects on the order of a hundred and eighty interruptions — the job will be killed, on average, every working afternoon of its life, and it must treat each death as a lunch break. And the photograph itself, at this weight, is a distributed systems exercise of its own: petabytes of optimizer state cannot march through one door, so the checkpoint is *sharded* — each worker writes its own slice, in parallel, to nearby storage, the photograph partitioned exactly as the state it depicts, with a manifest — a small court of record, episode eight's habit — declaring which slices constitute a blessed, complete cut; restore is the gather, and a torn checkpoint, half-written at the moment of death, is excluded by the manifest the way a coordinator's unforced decision never happened. Whence the photograph, and the question the estate actually argues about: *how often*. Checkpoint too rarely and each failure burns hours of lost work; too often and the checkpointing itself — petabytes of optimizer state marching to storage — becomes the tax that never stops. The answer is a genuine optimization with a genuinely lovely closed form, and its logic fits in the ear even if its derivation belongs to the desk. Two costs pull against each other: the standing tax — checkpoint cost divided by the interval, paid always — falls as the interval lengthens; the expected rework — about half an interval's work lost per failure, times the failure rate — rises with it. One falls as the interval grows, one rises in proportion; their sum bottoms out where the two match, and matching a term proportional to one-over-the-interval against a term proportional to the interval delivers, with the inevitability of all good square roots, the **Young–Daly interval**: checkpoint at roughly the square root of twice the checkpoint cost times the mean time between failures. The desk derives it as a guided problem, and the derivation is one honest page: balance the expected rework per failure against the standing checkpoint tax, differentiate, and the square root appears with the inevitability of all good square roots. With tonight's numbers — a checkpoint costing five minutes, a fleet failing every four hours — the formula prescribes roughly every fifty minutes: call it hourly; a standing tax of about a tenth in checkpoint time against expected rework of under half an hour per incident, and the *sensitivity* is the practical gift: halve the checkpoint cost — asynchronous checkpointing, sharded writes, the photograph taken without stopping the river, episode ten's whole craft — and the optimal interval tightens by only a square-root factor while the tax halves outright, which is why the engineering effort goes into cheapening the photograph rather than gambling on the interval. Arithmetic, sir, not folklore, and the whiteboard will say exactly that.

The stragglers return next, with the interest promised. Episode ten's backup tasks raced duplicates against the slow at batch scale, where tasks were pure and the loser's work was discarded free of charge. The training loop's version is harder — the barrier makes every straggler everyone's straggler, and a gradient worker is stateful in ways a map task is not — so the estate deploys the whole of episode eleven instead: phi-grade detection on step-completion times, because a slowing accelerator announces itself statistically long before it fails — the round barrier is, conveniently, a fleet-wide heartbeat with perfect regularity, the failure detector's dream client; hedged scheduling of the input pipeline, where the work *is* stateless and the duplicates race free; and hot spares standing by the ring. Walk the swap once, because it is the season's machinery in one motion: Gussie's accelerator degrades mid-run; the detector's dial crosses the acting threshold; at the next round barrier — a natural quiescent point, no Chandy–Lamport gymnastics required — the membership epoch increments, the ring re-forms with Barmy the spare spliced into Gussie's arc, Barmy loads the current weights from a peer or the last photograph, and the fleet resumes: a view change, episode four; fenced by the epoch on every collective, episode five, so that Gussie's late parcels — stamped with the dead epoch — are refused at every doorstep rather than folded into sums they no longer belong to. The input pipeline rides through the same swap untroubled, note: Barmy resumes Gussie's data shard from the committed cursor, episode ten's bookmark, and the log neither notices nor cares which machine is drinking from it — replayable input, the trinity's first leg, holding the door while the membership changes. Seconds of pause, not a restart from the photograph, and the doppelgänger's oldest trick answered by the season's oldest token. And who, sir, decrees the epochs? A small controller runs the job — tracking membership, blessing checkpoints, ordering the view changes — and it is, of course, a little parliament: replicated, elected, fenced, episode five's consensus kernel in a lab coat, holding the tiny truths (the member list, the golden checkpoint's manifest, the current epoch) while the herd of accelerators holds the tonnage. The coordination dividend, collected one last time: the estate's biggest computation governed by its smallest component, exactly as the constitution of every episode since the fifth would predict. Debt twenty-four, sir: presented at batch scale in episode ten, paid at silicon scale tonight.

And then the deferred horror, which this course has been keeping in a locked drawer since episode five with a label reading *not yet*. Episode five's Byzantine hour concerned machines that *lie*, and this course said, honestly, that in the datacentre one mostly pays for crash tolerance and reserves the Byzantine premium for the border — advice that stands, for the protocols, since lying-tolerance is bought per computation at ruinous multiples. The drawer opens tonight because the fleet studies of Meta and Google — published, sober, and quietly hair-raising — documented the exception: **silent data corruption**. Not disks lying about writes — cores lying about *arithmetic*: individual processor cores, one in some thousands, that under particular operands, temperatures, and voltages return the wrong answer to a correct computation and raise no flag whatsoever — mercury cores, defective from fabrication or aged into error, miscomputing a multiply once a day or once a month, silently. In an estate of episode-scale, that is a standing population of liars, sir — Aunt Agatha, tenured, in the arithmetic unit — and their products are not crashes but *poisoned numbers*: a gradient with one wrong element, a checkpoint with a corrupted page, an index computed wrong once and never again, propagating through a computation whose entire posture assumes the arithmetic is honest. The published studies' sobering clauses: the rate is far above what the industry's certification models predicted; the errors are data-dependent and intermittent, so the offending core passes every standard test and fails only on the operands of a particular Tuesday; and the damage is characteristically *quiet* — the training loss goes subtly wrong, or a compressed file unpacks to garbage a month later, and the investigation consumes weeks precisely because every component reports itself healthy — gray failure, episode eleven, driven down into the transistors. The defences are the season's closing lesson in miniature, and their economics are the honest part: full duplication — every computation run twice on different cores and compared — is the gold standard and a doubling of the estate's electricity bill, purchasable only for the steps that cannot be wrong; so the trade triages. Fleet-scale screening hunts mercury cores with targeted test patterns during idle cycles — cheap, catching the reproducible offenders, blind to the Tuesday-operand kind. Checksums guard everything that *moves* — episode one's discipline applied inside the chassis — catching corruption in flight and at rest, blind to corruption at birth. Spot-duplication verifies a random sliver of kernels, buying statistical detection at a few percent; checkpoint-time validation recomputes summary statistics before each photograph is blessed, so that a poisoned run is caught within one Young–Daly interval rather than one training epoch; and invariant monitors on the loss itself stand as the last tripwire, because a poisoned gradient eventually confesses in the metrics if anyone has arranged to be listening. Sir will recognize the deepest pattern: the arithmetic unit was the last *middle* anyone thought to distrust, and the remedy — verify at the level that owns the meaning, by ends-side comparison — is the end-to-end argument's final word, delivered one layer beneath where anyone expected to need it.

## Part Six — Serving, Briefly: The Consistency Bug With a Profit Line

One short part before the audit, because the estate's other half deserves its translation — and because the trade's own postmortems suggest that more machine-learning value dies in the gap this part names than in all the training outages combined; the training failures are loud, sir, and this one whispers. A model is trained; a model is served; and between the two runs a replication boundary that the machine-learning trade has learned, expensively, to name. **Training-serving skew** is a consistency violation between two replicas of the same computation, and one staging suffices: the training pipeline computes "customer's average purchase this month" in batch, from the log, with the month complete and the late records settled; the serving path computes "the same feature," reimplemented by a different team in a different language, online, from a cache, mid-month, tunnels and all — and the two disagree in a hundred small ways nobody itemized: null handling here, timezone there, the late data included in one and dropped by the other. The model, trained against the batch replica's world, is examined daily against the online replica's, and its answers degrade for a reason no model metric will name, because the bug is not in the model — it is *between two copies of a computation that no one declared to be replicas*, and therefore no one monitored for divergence. Episode seven taught sir the vocabulary: two replicas, no single source of truth, divergence unmonitored; and the modern remedy is episode ten verbatim — *one* feature definition, materialized to both consumers from the same log, the inside-out doctrine ending the schism by abolishing the second codebase, exactly as Kappa ended Lambda's — with the residual monitored like any replication pair: sampled online features logged and diffed against their batch recomputation, divergence alarmed before it is a revenue line. The serving fleet itself, sir can now read at sight: model replicas behind a balancer — episode six at its most benign, for inference is stateless and reads divide; rollouts as view changes with canaries — a new model version admitted to a sliver of traffic, watched, then promoted, the blast-radius discipline of episode eleven applied to weights instead of code; and rollback as the rewind, kept cheap by keeping the old version warm, which is the log's oldest lesson wearing a serving mesh. And **feature freshness is replication lag with a profit-and-loss line**: the recommendation model reading a user's behaviour thirty seconds stale is episode six's follower read, except that here the staleness has a measurable revenue gradient, and the trade budgets it — bounded staleness with a number, episode seven's honest tier — in currency, per second of lag. Consistency, sir, in production clothes, with the invoice finally denominated in the only unit the business meeting respects — and the episode-seven catechism transplants whole: eventual, plus what? Plus which session guarantees for the user's own actions, plus what staleness bound at what revenue cost, signed by whom. The vocabulary was always going to end up in the pricing meeting; this course merely delivered it there with its proofs attached.

## Closing — The Whiteboard, the Reading, and the Door

The Whiteboard, four items, the season's last.

Item one. Choose synchrony per layer: synchronous gradients where the mathematics wants coherence, asynchronous data pipelines where the log absorbs the slack, checkpointed everything, always. The barrier is a purchase, the ring is its efficient form, and the straggler is its standing invoice — detect, hedge, and swap by view change, with the epoch on every collective; a fleet without hot spares has decided, implicitly, that every straggler is worth ten thousand machines' idleness, and should at least decide it explicitly.

Item two. An all-reduce is not consensus. Agreement on a sum arbitrates nothing, excludes nobody, and therefore costs wire speed instead of a parliament; the moment the estate needs a *decision* — membership, leadership, which checkpoint is golden — the race test fires and episode four presents its bill. Know, at every layer, which of the two you are paying for, and be suspicious in both directions: a parliament asked to move gradients is a bottleneck by design, and a collective asked to arbitrate membership is a split brain on order.

Item three. Checkpoint cadence is arithmetic, not folklore: square root of twice the checkpoint cost times the mean time between failures, then argue with the formula rather than with opinions. The failure rate at fleet scale is a schedule; treat rework as a budget line and the interval falls out — and spend the engineering on cheapening the photograph, where the returns are linear, rather than on stretching the interval, where the square root blunts them. A blessed checkpoint needs a manifest; a torn one needs to be impossible to mistake for blessed.

Item four. Skew is a consistency bug wearing a machine-learning costume, and freshness is replication lag with a revenue line. One definition, one log, both consumers — and monitor the divergence between training and serving the way episode eleven taught you to monitor anything: before it is a metric the business notices. Two computations nobody declared to be replicas will diverge exactly as thoroughly as two that were declared and neglected; the declaration is the whole of the fix, because everything after it is episode six's standing machinery.

The reading, the season's last, kept short because the desk carries the long version. The parameter-server paper — Li and colleagues, two thousand fourteen — for the church the collectives displaced and the elasticity arguments that keep it standing in the asynchronous wings; read the consistency-model section and smile at episode seven staring back. "Cores that don't count" — the Google fleet study of silent data corruption, with Meta's companion study read alongside — soberly, once, so that the phrase *the arithmetic is honest* never again passes sir's design reviews unexamined. And, for pleasure rather than duty: Kreps on the log, reread after episode twelve, and Lamport's little time-clocks paper from nineteen seventy-eight, reread last of all — the season's first primary source and, sir will find, the one that reads most differently now that the whole arc hangs behind it. The desk's chapter twelve carries the consolidated further-reading essay — the whole season's shelf, organized and annotated, pointing onward from every episode at once — along with the ring optimality proof in full, the Young–Daly derivation as a guided problem, the staleness statements with their honest pointers, the elastic-membership design problem built on episodes four and five, and the final sabotage: a gradient-averaging scheme that applies late gradients to advanced weights without correction, whose drift the ambitious listener is invited to characterize before the solutions do it for them. The volume, sir, thereafter stands complete at twelve chapters — awaiting only the assembly session's continuity audit, consolidated ledgers, and whatever errata sir's own listening surfaces.
