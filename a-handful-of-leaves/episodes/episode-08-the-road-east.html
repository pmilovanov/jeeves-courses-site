Episode Eight — The Road East

A Handful of Leaves — An Introduction to Buddhism

The Monk Who Broke the Law

Good day, sir.

In the year six hundred and twenty-nine a monk of the Tang capital, twenty-seven years old, applied to the emperor for permission to travel to India, and was refused. The dynasty was new, the western frontier was unsettled, and the border was closed. So he went anyway. His name was Xuanzang — said, roughly, Shwen-dzahng; I shall say it the plain way — and he had a reason that a man in your trade will recognise: the Chinese translations of the scriptures he had spent his youth on contradicted each other, and he had concluded that the only remedy was to go to the source and fetch the originals himself. He slipped out of the capital by night, was smuggled past the frontier watchtowers by sympathetic guards, and rode into the Gobi alone, on an old horse he had bought from an old man who told him the horse knew the road. In the desert he dropped his water-bag and lost it all. His biography says he went four nights and five days without water, lying on the sand, reciting the name of the bodhisattva of compassion, until the horse — the old man had not lied — turned off the track of its own accord and brought him to a spring.

He was seventeen years on the road. He crossed the Tian Shan, was nearly kept as a court chaplain by the king of Turfan, passed through Samarkand and the Hindu Kush, and came down into India, where he spent five years at Nalanda — the university we lost last week — under its aged abbot, and toured the whole subcontinent, and at the end debated before the emperor Harsha at Kanauj, in an assembly the Chinese sources say ran to thousands, and was not refuted. And in six hundred and forty-five he came home, to the capital he had left as a fugitive, with six hundred and fifty-seven texts on twenty packhorses, and the emperor — the same emperor whose ban he had broken — came out to receive him, and offered him a ministry, which he declined, and built him a pagoda to hold the books, which stands in Xi'an today, and gave him a bureau of translators, at which he worked for the remaining nineteen years of his life. He is the greatest translator in the history of Buddhism, and one of the greatest in the history of anything. And nine centuries later a novelist made him into a comedy. Journey to the West, the most-read novel in the Chinese language, is the story of the monk Tripitaka — Xuanzang — travelling to India to fetch the scriptures, and being protected on the way by a monkey with a magic staff, a pig, and a river-ogre; and the monk in the novel is pious, timid, and perpetually rescued by the monkey. Miss Xenia, sir, will meet that monkey long before she meets the monk; every child in China does; and I mention it because it is the last thing tonight's evening is about: what a civilisation does to a teaching when it takes it in. It makes it its own, and then it makes it into a story with a monkey.

Tonight is the third border, and it is the largest the teaching ever crossed. India and Sri Lanka spoke cousin languages and shared a world; the Great Vehicle, for all its novelty, was Indian. China was another planet: a civilisation older than the Buddha's, with its own classics, its own philosophers, its own word for nothing — and a family ethic that the whole idea of a monk offended. The teaching that came out the other side is the Buddhism of a billion people, and it has a temple in every Chinatown on earth, and it looks, at first sight, like nothing I have described. One idea tonight, sir, and the word for it is translation: of the words, first; then of the institution; and then of the teaching itself into the school that decided the whole business could be settled by sitting down and staring at a wall. Four parts and a coda: the words; the schools; the wall; Japan; and a short evening in Korea and Vietnam.

Part One — The Han Court, and the Problem of Words

This part is about the arrival, and the two things China could not at first swallow: the vocabulary and the monk.

The legend first, since the Chinese have one for everything. In the year sixty-seven of the common era, it is said, the emperor Ming of the Han dynasty dreamed of a golden man flying over his palace, and his ministers told him that this was a sage of the West called the Buddha; so he sent envoys, who came back with two Indian monks, a white horse, and a load of scriptures, and the emperor built them a monastery at the capital and called it the White Horse Temple, which is there yet. The history is plainer. Buddhism came into China along the Silk Road, in the first century, in the baggage of Central Asian merchants and the monks who travelled with them, and for its first hundred years it was a foreigners' religion, practised in foreign quarters by people with foreign names, and mistaken by the Chinese for a branch of their own Daoism, since it involved sitting still and breathing. That mistake is the whole of Part One.

Consider the problem of the words. The scriptures were in Sanskrit, or in the northwestern dialects: inflected languages, with cases and tenses and an alphabet, and a vocabulary of technical terms two centuries deep. Chinese is none of those things: uninflected, monosyllabic, written in characters that each carry a meaning of their own — so that one cannot simply transliterate a foreign term without every character in the transliteration whispering its own sense. The first translators — a Parthian prince named An Shigao, in the second century, is the earliest we can name — did the only thing possible: they reached for the nearest Chinese concept and used it. The method was called, later, matching concepts, and its results were catastrophic and fertile in equal measure. Emptiness, the Great Vehicle's central word, was rendered as wu — the Daoist nothing, the non-being from which the Way brings forth the ten thousand things. Nirvana was rendered as wuwei — the Daoist non-action, the effortless flow of the sage. The arahant became an immortal. And so the first Chinese who read Buddhism read it as a slightly exotic Daoism, a teaching about returning to the primal nothing, which is not what Nagarjuna meant by emptiness at all — he meant, you will recall, empty of own-being, arising from conditions, and specifically not a ground of things. For two centuries the translation was the misunderstanding, and the misunderstanding shaped the schools; and when Kumarajiva and then Xuanzang produced accurate versions, the schools had already grown. That is the first lesson of the border, sir, and it is the one an engineer will feel in his bones: the first translation becomes the specification, whatever the original said.

Now the monk, and the fetter of the first evening returns from the other side of the world. Chinese civilisation was built, as nothing else was, on filial piety: the duty of a child to its parents and ancestors, extended into a whole ethic of family, state, and cosmos. The Classic of Filial Piety, which every literate Chinese learned as a child, opens by saying that the body, the hair, and the skin are received from one's parents, and not to injure them is the beginning of filial piety. And here came a religion whose ideal person shaved his head — injuring the hair his parents gave him — left his family, which the Classic says is the root of all virtue, did not marry, so that his line ended and no son would offer the sacrifices that kept his ancestors fed in the afterlife, and begged, which no gentleman did. The objections were put in writing early: there is a treatise, attributed to a scholar named Mouzi and dated, by the scholars' quarrel, somewhere between the second and fifth centuries, in which a Confucian puts thirty-seven objections to Buddhism and a convert answers them, and the family objections are at the heart of it. Why do the monks shave their heads? Why do they abandon their parents? Why do they refuse to marry and beget? And the convert answers, in effect, that a son who attains the Way saves his parents and his ancestors more surely than a son who merely feeds them; and that renunciation is the higher filial piety. You will notice that this is exactly what the Buddha did for Suddhodana, coming home to Kapilavastu with a bowl; the Chinese simply demanded it be argued.

And the argument changed the religion. Over the centuries, Chinese Buddhism became filial — not by abandoning the monk, but by surrounding him with a family religion the Indians never had. Sutras were composed in China on the kindness of parents and the debt of children. A festival grew up, celebrated to this day across East Asia, around the story of a monk named Mulian who descends into hell to rescue his mother and is told by the Buddha that only the merit of the whole sangha, offered on the fifteenth day of the seventh month, can free her: the ghost festival, on which the living feed the dead. And the merit economy of the sixth evening was turned, in China, almost wholly toward the ancestors: one made merit for one's parents, as Wang Jie printed his Diamond Sutra for his. The fetter that Kapilavastu forgave, China would not; and so the teaching, crossing this border, grew the family it had walked out on.

Two men, and then Part One is done. The first is Kumarajiva, and he is the translator before Xuanzang. He was born in the oasis kingdom of Kucha, on the northern Silk Road, in three hundred and forty-four, the son of an Indian father and a Kuchean princess, and by his twenties he was the most famous scholar in Central Asia. In three hundred and eighty-four a Chinese general took Kucha and carried him off as a prize; and the general, being a brute, kept him for seventeen years in a garrison town in the far west, forced him to break his vows — shut him in a room with a princess and locked the door — and used him as a fortune-teller. What Kumarajiva did with the seventeen years was learn Chinese, perfectly. In four hundred and one a new ruler brought him to the capital and gave him a bureau of hundreds, and in the twelve years left to him he translated the Lotus Sutra, the Diamond Sutra, the Perfection of Wisdom, the sutra of the layman Vimalakirti, and Nagarjuna's works — and his versions are so clear and so Chinese that they are still, sixteen centuries later, the ones chanted in every temple in East Asia; Xuanzang's more accurate renderings never displaced them. When he lay dying, the story says, he told his disciples that if his translations contained no errors his tongue would not burn; and when they cremated him the body burned and the tongue did not. Hold that story beside Xuanzang's horse, sir; the Chinese tradition marks its translators with miracles, because it knew what it owed them.

The second man is a monk named Huiyuan, and he lost an argument that decided the shape of Chinese Buddhism for ever. In four hundred and four he wrote a treatise whose title is its thesis: A Monk Does Not Bow Before a King. In India, you will recall, the sangha stood outside the state; the king was its protector, and even Ashoka petitioned it rather than commanded it. Huiyuan argued that the same must hold in China. He lost — slowly, over centuries, but completely. The Chinese state licensed monks, sold ordination certificates for revenue, appointed the abbots of the great monasteries, and, when it chose, closed them; and the monk bowed. The throne that Sri Lanka lashed on beside the order, China set above it. Remember that when we reach the year eight hundred and forty-five.

Part One in a sentence: Buddhism entered China as a foreigners' cult, was translated with Daoist words so that the first translation became the specification, offended the family ethic so deeply that it had to grow a family religion of its own, and lost the argument about bowing to kings.

Part Two — The Chinese Schools

This part is about what China built once it had the books, and I shall announce the cast before anything moves: four schools — Tiantai, Huayan, Pure Land, and Chan — of which the fourth gets Part Three to itself.

The first problem the Chinese faced, once Kumarajiva and his successors had given them the library, was that the library disagreed with itself. The old discourses said one thing, the Perfection of Wisdom another, the Lotus a third, the Nirvana Sutra a fourth — and all of them were, by their own account, the Buddha's word. India had absorbed this by living with it; the Chinese, a bureaucratic people, ranked it. The school called Tiantai, after the mountain where its founder Zhiyi taught in the sixth century, produced the great solution: the doctrine of periods. The Buddha, it said, had taught different things at different stages of his career, to audiences of different capacity — the Lotus Sutra's skilful means, applied to the whole canon — and the scriptures could be arranged in order from the elementary to the final; and the final teaching, the one he gave at the end, was the Lotus Sutra. Every Chinese school after this did the same, with a different sutra at the top; and the habit of reading the canon as a curriculum — graded, with the school's own text as the last lesson — is the most Chinese thing in Chinese Buddhism.

The second school, Huayan — the Flower Garland school, after the enormous sutra it built on — gave the tradition its most beautiful image, and it pays a debt I issued last week. Emptiness, you will recall, means arising from conditions: nothing has its own being; everything is what it is because of everything else. Huayan turned that from a negation into a vision. Imagine, it said, the net of the god Indra, stretched across the whole of space: at every knot of the net hangs a jewel, and every jewel reflects every other jewel, and each reflection contains the reflections of all the rest, without end. That is Indra's net, and it is the universe: every thing containing every other, total interpenetration, so that — the school's masters loved to say — a single mote of dust holds the whole cosmos, and the whole cosmos is present in every mote. The great master of the school, Fazang, is said to have demonstrated it to the empress Wu, around the year seven hundred, by setting a Buddha image and a candle in a room lined with mirrors on every wall, the floor, and the ceiling, so that the image appeared in every mirror, and every mirror in every other, infinitely. Emptiness, said with a smile, becomes everything is in everything. You will meet it in the last evening in the mouths of the environmentalists; it is the most-borrowed idea in Chinese Buddhism, and it was born as a way of making Nagarjuna sound like good news.

The third school is the one most Chinese Buddhists actually practised, then and now, and it pays the other debt from last week: Pure Land. In one of the Great Vehicle's sutras, a bodhisattva named Dharmakara makes a series of vows, and in the eighteenth of them he swears that if he attains Buddhahood, any being anywhere who sincerely calls his name, even ten times, shall be reborn in his land — a paradise in the West, called the Land of Bliss, without pain or hindrance, in which awakening is easy and assured. He did attain Buddhahood, and his name is Amitabha, the Buddha of Infinite Light, and the practice of the Pure Land school is, simply, to call it: Namo Amituofo — homage to Amitabha Buddha — said, chanted, murmured, counted on beads, ten times or ten thousand. Now, why would a religion of the physician, of the eight factors and the four foundations, produce this? Because of a doctrine you have not yet met, and which I must give you now, because it drives the rest of the evening. The Chinese had received, with the sutras, an Indian theory that the Buddha's teaching decays: that after his death there is an age of the true dharma, and then an age of the counterfeit, and then the age of the end of the dharma, in which the texts survive but no one can practise them or attain anything by them. And by the sixth century the Chinese had calculated that they were in the last age. If that is your situation, sir — the physician gone, the prescription unfillable, the patient too sick to walk — then the only hope is that someone else has already made the crossing and will pull you across: other-power rather than self-power; the raft rowed for you. Pure Land is Buddhism as a religion of grace: the vow is made, the name is given, and the only thing asked of you is to say it and mean it. A seventh-century master, Shandao, drew the picture that every Pure Land believer knows: a traveller on a narrow white path between a river of fire and a river of water, with bandits behind him, and a voice from the far bank saying come. The Buddha of the Deer Park did not teach this. But the Buddha of the Lotus Sutra, who arranges skilful means for every capacity, had made room for it; and it became, by a wide margin, the most popular Buddhism in the history of the world.

The fourth school is Chan, and it is Part Three; but I must first tell you what happened to the other three, because it is the hinge of the evening, and it is the year I asked you to remember.

In eight hundred and forty-five the emperor Wuzong, a Daoist by conviction and a bankrupt by circumstance, closed Chinese Buddhism down. The figures his own ministry reported are these: four thousand six hundred monasteries demolished; forty thousand smaller shrines; two hundred and sixty thousand five hundred monks and nuns returned, by decree, to lay life and the tax rolls; and the bronze images and bells melted for coin. Huiyuan had lost, you see, and this is what losing meant: the monk who bows to the king may be un-monked by him. It lasted only a few years — the next emperor relented — but it broke the back of the scholarly schools. Tiantai and Huayan were religions of great libraries and great monasteries, and the libraries were burned and the monasteries pulled down, and they never fully recovered; their texts survived, in some cases, only in Korea and Japan. What survived in China was the two schools that needed nothing: Pure Land, which required only a name and a mouth to say it, and Chan, which had made a point, for two centuries, of needing nothing at all.

Part Two in a sentence: the Chinese ranked the disagreeing scriptures into a curriculum, turned emptiness into Indra's net, and, believing themselves in the age of the end of the dharma, built the religion of Amitabha's name; and the persecution of eight hundred and forty-five broke the schools of the library and left the two that needed nothing.

Part Three — The Wall

This part is Chan — Zen, in the Japanese pronunciation by which the West knows it — and I shall tell it as the tradition tells it, and then tell you what the historians say, and then say why it does not matter.

The name first. Chan is the Chinese attempt to say dhyana, the Sanskrit for meditation — the absorptions of the fifth evening. The school is the meditation school, and its founding claim is that the whole of the teaching can be had by doing the thing, and that the books are, at best, a finger pointing at the moon, and at worst a way of never looking up. The legend gives it a founder, an Indian monk named Bodhidharma, who is said to have arrived in south China around five hundred and twenty and to have been received by the emperor Wu of Liang, a devout man who had built temples and ordained monks by the thousand. What merit have I earned? asked the emperor. No merit whatever, said Bodhidharma. What, then, is the first principle of the holy teaching? Vast emptiness. Nothing holy. Then who is it that stands before me? I do not know. And he left, and crossed the Yangtze, the pictures say, on a reed, and went to the monastery at Shaolin, and sat facing a wall for nine years, and when a monk named Huike stood in the snow outside begging to be taught, and was ignored, and cut off his own arm and held it out, Bodhidharma turned round. Every word of that is legend, sir, and every Zen monk knows it is legend, and it is the truest picture of the school there is: no merit; nothing holy; I do not know; nine years facing a wall. The tradition later summed itself up in four lines: a special transmission outside the scriptures; not depending on words and letters; pointing directly at the human mind; seeing one's nature, and becoming Buddha. The last line is the premise you met last week — the Buddha-nature, awakening already present under the afflictions — and the first three are the method: stop reading about it, and look.

The tradition's second founding story is the poem contest, and it is the most famous story in Zen. In the seventh century the fifth patriarch of the school, wishing to choose a successor, asked his monks to write a verse showing their understanding. The senior monk, Shenxiu, the obvious heir, wrote his on a corridor wall by night, not daring to sign it: The body is the tree of awakening; the mind is a bright mirror on its stand. Polish it diligently, hour by hour, and let no dust alight. And an illiterate woodcutter from the south named Huineng, who worked in the monastery kitchen and could not read the verse, had it read to him, and had his reply written beside it: Awakening is no tree; the bright mirror has no stand. Originally there is not a single thing — where could dust alight? And the patriarch, reading the second verse, saw who had understood, and gave the woodcutter the robe of succession secretly, at midnight, and sent him south to hide from the jealousy of the monks. Huineng became the sixth patriarch; his sermons became the Platform Sutra, the only Chinese text the tradition allows the title sutra; and his verse is Chan's charter: originally not a single thing. Not a mirror to be polished — not a gradual path of purification — but a nature already clean, to be seen, at once. The southern school of sudden awakening against the northern school of gradual; and the southern school won, and every Zen lineage on earth descends from the woodcutter.

Now the historians, and I owe you them. The story was, in all probability, constructed — by a monk named Shenhui, a generation after Huineng, who conducted a long and successful campaign to have his own master recognised as the sixth patriarch and Shenxiu's line, which was the respectable court Buddhism of the day, discredited as gradualist. The oldest manuscript of the Platform Sutra, found — where else — in the Library Cave at Dunhuang, contains two versions of Huineng's verse, and neither is the one now recited; the famous verse was polished later, like the mirror. Shenxiu, by the evidence, taught nothing so crude as the story gives him. The great modern scholar of the matter, McRae, has a rule for reading Chan history that I commend to you: it's not true, and therefore it's more important. By which he means that the tradition remembered its origins as it needed them — as a woodcutter beating a scholar, the sudden beating the gradual, the kitchen beating the library — and that the need tells you what the school was for. A religion founded on not depending on words and letters wrote its own history as a parable, and I find that, on reflection, entirely proper.

What did the school actually do? It sat. And it invented a literature of its own, in spite of itself, which is the most distinctive thing in East Asian Buddhism: the recorded sayings of the masters, and out of them the koan — the Chinese word means a public case, a legal precedent — a short exchange, usually baffling, given to a student to hold in the mind until the mind gives way. The most famous is four words long. A monk asked the master Zhaozhou — the Japanese say Joshu — Does a dog have the Buddha-nature? Now, sir, the doctrine, which the monk knew and the master knew he knew, says that all beings have the Buddha-nature; the answer is yes. Zhaozhou said: Wu. No. The word means no, or nothing, or not; and a whole tradition was built on refusing to explain it. The great koan collection, compiled in the thirteenth century under the title The Gateless Gate, puts it first, and instructs the student to concentrate on that single syllable with every bone and every pore, day and night, like a man who has swallowed a red-hot iron ball and can neither get it down nor spit it out, until, it promises, the whole apparatus of yes and no collapses and one sees what the master saw. The koan is not a riddle with an answer at the back of the book. It is a device for wearing out the discursive mind by giving it a problem that discursion cannot solve, on the theory that what remains when discursion stops is the Buddha-nature that was there all along. Why are you looking for the ox you are riding? the masters asked — that is the debt from last week, paid — and the koan is a way of making the student feel the ox.

The masters themselves were, by any standard, alarming. One, Linji — Rinzai in Japanese — taught by shouting, and by hitting, and left the saying that has scandalised the pious for a thousand years: If you meet the Buddha on the road, kill the Buddha. Meaning: whatever you make into an idol, including him, is one more thing in the way. Another, Baizhang, gave the school the rule that saved it: a day without work is a day without food. Chan monasteries grew their own crops — a thing the Indian code forbade, since a monk who farmed killed worms — and so, when eight hundred and forty-five came and the endowments were seized, the Chan monks were hoeing their own fields with no library to lose, and went on hoeing. That is why the school survived, and why Chinese Buddhism after the ninth century is, in the main, Chan and Pure Land, frequently in the same temple and often in the same monk: sitting in the morning, and Amitabha's name at night.

Part Three in a sentence: Chan is the meditation school — no merit, nothing holy, nine years at a wall — whose charter is the woodcutter's originally not a single thing, whose history it wrote as the parable it needed, and whose koans wear out the discursive mind until the Buddha-nature shows; and it survived the persecution because it had made a point of needing nothing.

Part Four — Japan

This part is the last leg of the road east, and the cast is large, so hear the names once before they move: Prince Shotoku; Saicho and Kukai; then, in the age of the warriors, Honen, Shinran, Eisai, Dogen, and Nichiren.

Buddhism reached Japan by way of Korea, in the sixth century, as a diplomatic gift — a Korean king sent an image and some sutras to the Japanese court, with a covering letter recommending the religion — and it was adopted, at first, as a matter of state, by a regent named Prince Shotoku, who in six hundred and four issued a constitution in seventeen articles whose second article commands the nation to sincerely revere the three treasures — the Buddha, the teaching, and the order — and who is the founding saint of Japanese Buddhism, on every temple's wall. For three centuries it was a court religion, of great temples and a giant bronze Buddha at Nara. Then two monks went to China, in the same year, and came back with the two schools that would rule the Heian age: Saicho, who brought Tiantai — Tendai, in Japanese — and founded a monastery on Mount Hiei above Kyoto that became the seminary of the whole country; and Kukai, who brought something else, which I shall name and then defer: the esoteric Buddhism of mantra and mandala and initiation — the tantric Buddhism of late India, which reached Japan in the year eight hundred and six, in the very generation it was taking root in Tibet, and which is, in Japan, the school called Shingon. Hold that; it is next week's mountain.

The great age is the thirteenth century — the age of Kamakura, when the warriors took power from the court, and the country believed, as China had, that it lived in the age of the end of the dharma, and five founders, every one of them trained on Mount Hiei, walked down the mountain and made the schools that Japanese Buddhism still is.

The first two are the Pure Land, taken further than China had dared. Honen taught that in the last age nothing availed but the name: say Namu Amida Butsu, and nothing else is required, not the sutras, not the precepts, not the monastery. He was exiled for it. His disciple Shinran took the step that no Buddhist had taken: he married, openly, and had children, and founded a school with a married clergy — the True Pure Land, today the largest Buddhist body in Japan — on the grounds that if the name alone saves, the monk's celibacy is one more work of self-power, and pride. And he drew the conclusion that his followers recorded in a little book called the Tannisho, and which I want you to hear, because it is the most startling sentence any Buddhist has said: Even a good person attains rebirth in the Pure Land — how much more so a wicked person! The good person, Shinran explains, trusts in his own goodness and so does not throw himself wholly on the vow; the wicked person, having nothing to trust, does; and it is the throwing that saves. Grace before works, sir, three centuries before a German monk nailed the same argument to a church door; and I shall let you draw your own conclusions about what a religion of self-reliance had turned into on the far side of its largest border.

The next two are Zen, and they are the ones the West knows. Eisai went to China and came back in the eleven-nineties with the Rinzai school — Linji's line, of the shouts and the koans — and with tea, which he recommended to the warriors for staying awake in meditation, and which they took up with such enthusiasm that a whole aesthetic grew round it. And Dogen went to China a generation later, and came back with the other line, Soto, and a doctrine that I find the most beautiful in Japanese Buddhism. His teacher in China had told him simply to sit — just sitting, the school calls it; no koan, no object, no goal — and Dogen, who was a philosopher of the first rank and wrote a vast book about it, drew out what that means: that sitting is not a method for reaching awakening, as one takes a road to reach a town; sitting is awakening, practice and realisation are one thing, and the person on the cushion is not trying to become a Buddha but being one, badly, and then less badly. His most famous passage runs like this, and I give it whole. To study the Buddha way is to study the self. To study the self is to forget the self. To forget the self is to be confirmed by the ten thousand things. To be confirmed by the ten thousand things is to drop away body and mind, one's own and others'. The anatomy of the third evening, sir, said as a single breath.

The fifth founder is Nichiren, a fisherman's son and a prophet, who decided that the Lotus Sutra alone was the truth for the last age, that all other schools were leading the nation to ruin, and that the practice was to chant the sutra's title — Namu Myoho Renge Kyo — with total faith; he was exiled twice and nearly executed and founded the most militant tradition in Japanese Buddhism, whose modern lay offshoot has millions of members and a political party. I give him his sentence because he shows you the range: within one century, one mountain produced a religion of grace, a religion of silence, and a religion of the shouted title.

Two more things about Japan, one lovely and one not. The lovely thing is what Zen did to the arts, under the patronage of the warriors who found in it a discipline for men who might die tomorrow. The tea ceremony, brought to its severe perfection in the sixteenth century, in which four people spend an hour watching one person make a bowl of tea, and the point is the attention. The rock garden of the Ryoan-ji temple in Kyoto, made around fifteen hundred: fifteen stones in raked gravel, arranged so that from no point on the veranda can all fifteen be seen at once, which is either a doctrine or a joke and is probably both. Ink painting, in which the empty paper is the subject. The haiku, in which a poet of the seventeenth century said, in seventeen syllables, an old pond, a frog jumps in, the sound of water — and stopped, because the rest was the sound. And the master Hakuin, in the eighteenth century, reviving the koan school and giving it its second most famous case: You know the sound of two hands clapping. What is the sound of one hand? Every one of those things, sir, is the woodcutter's verse made into an object: originally not a single thing, and then the one stone, the one frog, the one hand.

The thing that is not lovely is this. The samurai who patronised Zen took from it, in part, a training in facing death without flinching, and that training was, in the twentieth century, put at the service of the Japanese empire by the Zen establishment itself. A scholar and Zen priest named Brian Victoria documented it, in a book called Zen at War, in the nineteen-nineties: the great masters of the day preaching that the sword that kills is the sword that gives life, that a soldier who dies for the emperor with no-mind attains the Way, and that the war in China was a bodhisattva's compassionate act. Some of the schools have since apologised. I tell you this because the physician of the second evening would want it told, and because the last evening of this course is going to ask what the teaching does and does not do to the people who hold it, and the honest answer begins here: it did not make the masters good. Nothing holy is a dangerous thing to say to a man with a sword.

Part Four in a sentence: Japan received Buddhism as statecraft, took its schools from China through the seminary on Mount Hiei, and in the age of the warriors produced from one mountain a religion of grace with a married clergy, two schools of Zen — the koan and just sitting — and a religion of the chanted title; and Zen made the arts, and then, in the twentieth century, made excuses for a war.

Part Five — Korea, and Vietnam

A short coda, sir, because the road east had two more branches, and each will be needed later.

Korea took Buddhism from China in the fourth century and gave it back, once, in a form that saved it: when the persecution of eight hundred and forty-five burned the Chinese libraries, some of the great texts survived only in the Korean copies, and in the thirteenth century the Koreans carved the entire canon onto more than eighty thousand wooden printing blocks, which sit today, every one of them intact, in a temple in the mountains, the most complete Buddhist canon in East Asia. Korean Zen — Seon — was shaped by a master named Jinul, who refused to choose between the woodcutter and the library and taught sudden awakening, gradual cultivation: one sees the nature at once, and then spends a life growing into what one saw; which is, I think, the sanest thing anyone said in the whole sudden-and-gradual quarrel. And the Korean order that descends from him has, in the last fifty years, trained a great many of the West's Zen monks.

And Vietnam. Buddhism came there by both roads, from India by sea and from China by land, and its Zen — Thien — is old and quiet. Two of its monks the world knows. On the eleventh of June, nineteen sixty-three, at a crossroads in Saigon, an elderly monk named Thich Quang Duc sat down in the lotus position, was doused in petrol by his companions, and set himself alight, in protest at the persecution of Buddhists by the Catholic government of the day; and an American photographer named Malcolm Browne took the picture, and the picture went round the world and helped bring the government down within the year. The monk did not move as he burned. I do not know what to say about that, sir, and I have decided not to try; I record it, as the tradition records the Fire Sermon, and let it stand. The other is Thich Nhat Hanh, who opposed the war from inside it, was exiled for it, and spent fifty years in France teaching a Buddhism he called engaged — mindfulness turned outward, to the world's suffering — and who is, with the Dalai Lama, one of the two Buddhists most Westerners can name. He is the last evening's business, and I leave him at the border.

The third border, then, whole. What was kept: the sutras, translated entire; the code, adapted; the Buddha-nature, raised from a doctrine to the premise. What was added: the family the teaching had walked out on; the state above the order; the curriculum; Indra's net; a religion of grace that the Deer Park never taught; the wall; the arts; and a married clergy. And the pattern of the sixth evening held once more: when the homeland burned its libraries, the neighbours had the copies.

The Raft, So Far

The planks lashed on tonight, sir, said once more.

First: a monk broke the law to fetch the books, and came home to a pagoda and a translation bureau, and nine centuries later a novelist gave him a monkey — which is what a civilisation does to a teaching it takes in. Second: the teaching entered China as a foreigners' cult and was translated with Daoist words, so that the first translation became the specification; it offended the family so deeply that it grew a family religion of its own; and it lost the argument about bowing to kings. Third: the Chinese ranked the scriptures into a curriculum, made emptiness into Indra's net — every jewel reflecting every other — and, believing themselves in the age of the end of the dharma, built the religion of Amitabha's name, the raft rowed for you; and the persecution of eight hundred and forty-five left standing only the schools that needed nothing. Fourth: Chan sat facing a wall, wrote its charter as a woodcutter's verse — originally not a single thing — and its history as the parable it needed, and wore out the discursive mind with koans until the Buddha-nature showed. Fifth: Japan made from one mountain a religion of grace with a married clergy and Shinran's wicked man, two Zens — the koan and just sitting — a chanted title, the tea and the stones and the frog; and then excuses for a war; while Korea kept the canon on eighty thousand blocks and Vietnam gave the world a burning monk and an exile.

The one book tonight, and I shall give you two roads again. Sam van Schaik's The Spirit of Zen tells the story of the school from the manuscripts in the Library Cave — the very cave of last week's scroll — and is honest about the legends in the way I have tried to be. And for the practice rather than the history, the book that taught two generations of Westerners to sit: Shunryu Suzuki's Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, a set of talks by a Soto priest in San Francisco, which you may read in an afternoon and, if the tradition is right, spend a life on. Its title is its teaching: in the beginner's mind there are many possibilities; in the expert's, few.

Three accounts paid tonight — Indra's net, Amitabha's name, and the ox — and two carried forward: the esoteric Buddhism Kukai brought to Japan, which is the great matter of next week; and the exile in France, who is the last evening's.

Next week, then, sir: Saint Petersburg, nineteen hundred and nine, and a Buryat lama who is tutor to the Dalai Lama and back channel to the Tsar lays the foundation of a Tibetan temple on the Neva, under the eye of the police; and from there to the roof of the world — the great debate that chose India over China, the diamond vehicle and what it claims, the four schools and the reincarnate lamas, the book that guides the dead, and the Buddhists of the Russian empire, who were, and are, the only Buddhists in Europe.

Strive on with diligence, sir.

— End of Episode Eight.
