# Episode Seven -- The Great Vehicle

*A Handful of Leaves -- An Introduction to Buddhism*

## A Book from a Cave

Good day, sir.

In March of nineteen hundred and seven a Hungarian-born archaeologist in British employ named Aurel Stein arrived, with camels, at an oasis called Dunhuang, on the far western edge of China, where the Silk Road came out of the desert and split into its northern and southern branches around the Taklamakan. Cut into a cliff outside the town were several hundred caves, painted floor to ceiling with Buddhas over a thousand years by the merchants and monks who had passed that way; and in one of them, seven years earlier, a Daoist caretaker named Wang, sweeping sand, had found a walled-up chamber. Behind the wall was a room the size of a large cupboard, packed to the ceiling with manuscripts -- tens of thousands of them, sealed in around the year one thousand and untouched since. Stein spent weeks on Wang. He praised Wang's restoration of the shrine; he invoked, through his Chinese secretary, the great pilgrim Xuanzang, whom Wang revered and whom we shall meet next week; and in the end he rode away with twenty-four cases of manuscripts and five of paintings, for a donation to the shrine's repair that has embarrassed the British Museum ever since. He did not read Chinese. He did not know what he had.

What he had, among the rest, was a scroll about sixteen feet long, made of seven sheets of paper pasted end to end, with a picture at its head of the Buddha preaching to an old monk, and a text printed -- not written; *printed*, from carved wooden blocks -- in clear, beautiful characters. And at the end, a colophon: *Reverently made for universal free distribution by Wang Jie, on behalf of his two parents, on the fifteenth day of the fourth month of the ninth year of Xiantong.* That date is the eleventh of May, eight hundred and sixty-eight. It is the oldest dated printed book in the world, sir -- nearly six centuries before Gutenberg -- and it was printed by a son for his parents, to be given away free, to make merit for them. You will recognise the economy from last week. And the text? The text is a scripture called the *Diamond Cutter of Perfect Wisdom*, the Diamond Sutra, and in it the Buddha turns to his disciple Subhuti and says: *Subhuti, do not say that the Tathagata has the thought, "I have taught the dharma." There is no dharma that the Tathagata has taught.* And it ends with a verse that every East Asian Buddhist knows by heart: *As a star, a flaw in the eye, a lamp, an illusion, a dewdrop, a bubble, a dream, a lightning-flash, a cloud -- so should one view all that is conditioned.*

Hold the object in your mind, sir, because it is the whole of tonight. A book printed as an act of devotion, by a son, for the merit of his parents -- and inside it, the Buddha denying that he ever taught. Devotion on the outside, emptiness within: the two faces of the movement that calls itself the **Great Vehicle**, the *Mahayana*, which is the Buddhism of China and Japan and Korea and Vietnam and Tibet, of the great majority of Buddhists alive, and which is, in almost every visible respect, a different religion from the one I have described for six evenings.

Last week I said that every time the teaching crosses a border it changes, and I showed you the first crossing, to Sri Lanka, which kept the code and the word and lashed on a throne. Tonight's border is not on a map. It is a border in *time*, crossed inside India itself, in the centuries around the beginning of the common era, when new scriptures began to appear in the monasteries -- scriptures that claimed to be the Buddha's word, that nobody at the First Council had recited, and that changed the goal, the founder, and the philosophy. One idea tonight: what the Great Vehicle changed, and what it argued. Four parts and a fall: the larger aim; where it came from; emptiness; mind and the Buddha within; and the end of it all in India.

## Part One -- The Larger Aim

This part is about the first and most visible change: the destination.

Begin with the name, because it is a boast, and the boast has a shadow. *Mahayana* means *the Great Vehicle* -- the great carriage, the great ferry; you will hear the raft in it -- and it was chosen by people who wished to contrast their way with another, which they called the **Hinayana**, the *inferior vehicle*, the little cart. I want to retire that second word tonight in your hearing, sir, and I shall tell you why. No school ever called itself the Hinayana; it is a term of abuse, coined by one party for the other, and the party it was aimed at was the *old* Buddhism, the Buddhism of the arahant and the three baskets, of which the tradition of the elders that we visited last week is the surviving heir. Western books used it for a century as if it were a neutral label. In nineteen fifty the World Fellowship of Buddhists, meeting in Colombo, resolved unanimously that the word should be dropped; and it should. There is the old Buddhism, and there is the Great Vehicle, and the elders are not the little cart. So.

What the Great Vehicle changed first was the *goal*. In the old teaching, the goal for a disciple was to become an **arahant** -- a *worthy one* -- a person who has walked the path to its end and put out the three fires and will not be reborn: Kondanna in the deer park, the five within a week, the five hundred at the First Council. Becoming a *Buddha* was another thing altogether: a Buddha was one who found the path *without a teacher*, in an age when it had been lost, and taught it -- a feat achieved once in an aeon, by one man, and not a career open to the rest. The Great Vehicle said: no. It said the arahant's goal was *too small* -- a private escape, one's own suffering ended and the rest of the world left burning -- and that the true aim for every follower was to become a Buddha oneself, *for the sake of all beings*. The person who has taken that aim is a **bodhisattva** -- an *awakening-being*, a Buddha-in-the-making -- and the Great Vehicle is, before it is anything else, the religion of the bodhisattva's vow.

The vow is worth hearing, because it is the most extravagant promise in the history of religion. In its classic form it runs: *Beings are numberless; I vow to save them all. The afflictions are inexhaustible; I vow to end them all. The teachings are boundless; I vow to learn them all. The Buddha's way is unsurpassable; I vow to attain it.* Numberless beings, and I vow to save them all. The bodhisattva, on the strength of that, *defers* his own final release: he will not go out like a lamp while a single being remains in the fire. And the path he walks is not the eightfold path, though it contains it, but a set of six **perfections** -- I shall say them twice -- generosity, ethics, patience, energy, meditation, and wisdom. Once more, with their names from the old language: *dana*, giving; *sila*, virtue; *kshanti*, patience; *virya*, vigour; *dhyana*, meditation; *prajna*, wisdom. You will notice that the list begins with giving and ends with wisdom, and that the middle of it is *patience* -- the patience to be reborn, again and again, for ever, until the last being is out.

I shall put it where you can feel it, sir, since you gave me leave. You already know the vow. You took it in June, without the Sanskrit. A parent is a person who has made a promise to a being that cannot yet understand the promise, and who will not go out like a lamp while that being is in the fire, and who finds the promise has quietly extended itself to the other children on the ward, and then, if he is honest, to the whole tired maternity floor. The bodhisattva's vow is that promise made with no boundary. The Great Vehicle's claim against the arahant was, at bottom, a parent's complaint against a bachelor: *you cannot simply leave.*

Where did the idea come from? Not from nowhere; the old tradition had it already, as the Buddha's *past*. The tales of his previous lives -- the *Jatakas*, some five hundred and forty-seven of them in the Pali collection, and the favourite bedtime stories of every Buddhist country -- are all about the being who would become the Buddha, life after life, practising the perfections: the hare who, having nothing else to give a hungry traveller, leaps into the fire to offer his own body; the prince who lies down before a starving tigress so that she may feed her cubs; the prince who gives away his kingdom and then his children; the king who gives away his eyes. The old tradition told those stories about *one* being, the one who was on his way to being *the* Buddha. The Great Vehicle's revolution was to say: that is the road for *everyone*. The Jatakas are not the biography of a unique hero; they are the syllabus.

And with the goal, the *founder* changed. If Buddhahood was the aim of all, then a Buddha could not simply be a man who died at Kusinara and was cremated by the Mallas. The Lotus Sutra -- the most influential scripture in East Asia, of which more shortly -- says outright that the Buddha's death was a *performance*: a skilful show put on for beings who would grow lazy if they thought the teacher would always be there, while in truth the Buddha's life is immeasurable and he has been teaching since before time. The Great Vehicle arranged this into a doctrine of **three bodies**: the *truth-body*, which is reality itself, what a Buddha *is* when he is not appearing; the *enjoyment-body*, the glorious celestial form in which Buddhas dwell in their own *pure lands* and teach the bodhisattvas; and the *transformation-body*, the appearance in history -- Shakyamuni, the man, a projection, so to speak, of the first into the world. And once Buddhas were cosmic, there could be many. The Great Vehicle populated the universe with them: Amitabha, the Buddha of Infinite Light, in his pure land in the West, whom we shall meet next week as the most-invoked name in East Asia; Akshobhya in the East; the Medicine Buddha; and beside them the great celestial bodhisattvas -- *Avalokiteshvara*, the lord who looks down in compassion, who became a woman in China and is called Guanyin, and whom the Tibetans call Chenrezig and hold to be incarnate in the Dalai Lama; *Manjushri*, wisdom, with his sword that cuts through ignorance; and *Maitreya*, the Buddha of the future, whom even the old tradition expected. The pantheon of a Chinese temple, sir, is this list. A Theravada temple has one Buddha, and he is a man; a Mahayana temple has a hundred, and they are a cosmos.

Part One in a sentence: the Great Vehicle changed the goal from the arahant, who ends his own suffering, to the bodhisattva, who vows to become a Buddha for all beings and defers his release until the last is free; and with the goal it made the Buddha cosmic and many.

## Part Two -- Where It Came From

This part is about the new scriptures: what they were, how they claimed to be the Buddha's word, and what the historians think actually happened.

The Great Vehicle arrives in history as *texts*. From the last century before the common era, new *sutras* -- the Sanskrit word; *sutta* was the Pali -- begin to appear, and they are unlike the old discourses in every way. The old discourses are short, repetitive, set in named places, and addressed to named monks. The new ones are vast, ornate, set in cosmic assemblies with millions of listeners, lit by rays from the Buddha's forehead, and addressed, very often, to bodhisattvas. The earliest of them, by general agreement, is the *Perfection of Wisdom in Eight Thousand Lines*; and the first hard date we have for the whole movement is a translation of that text into Chinese, made at the Han capital by a monk from the northwest named Lokakshema, in the year one hundred and seventy-nine. Scraps of a version in the language of Gandhara, in the far northwest, have since been dated to the first century. So: new scriptures, in existence by the first century, in Chinese by the second.

How did they claim to be the Buddha's word, when nobody had recited them at Rajagaha? The tradition had answers. The Perfection of Wisdom, one story says, had been entrusted by the Buddha to the *nagas* -- the serpent-spirits who live under the sea -- because the world was not ready, and had been fetched up from their palace centuries later by a great philosopher, of whom more in Part Three. Or the teachings had been given to a more advanced audience, in a higher realm, than the monks of the Ganges country. The historians say something more interesting, and it is this. Some of the new sutras describe their own origin: a meditator, in deep concentration, *sees* the Buddhas of the present in their pure lands and *hears* them teach -- there is a scripture, one of the earliest Lokakshema translated, that is essentially a manual for doing so. The new word, that is, came by *vision*: it was heard in meditation, by monks who held that a Buddha's teaching was not confined to what one man had said at one time, and who wrote down what they heard. Whether you think that is revelation or composition depends on your view of meditation; the texts themselves would say the distinction is empty.

What the Great Vehicle was *not* -- and this surprises everyone who comes to it from the Christian pattern of schism -- was a *sect*. There was no Mahayana order, no separate discipline, no break with the code. When Chinese pilgrims walked through India in the seventh century they found Mahayana monks and old-school monks living in the *same* monasteries, keeping the same rules, eating from the same bowls, distinguished only by which scriptures they studied and whether they bowed to the bodhisattvas. The Great Vehicle was a *movement within* Buddhism, carried by texts, and it lived beside the old schools for a thousand years without displacing them. And it was, for a very long time, small. A scholar named Schopen, in the nineteen-seventies, did the unglamorous thing and went through the *inscriptions* -- the donor records carved on stupas and monastery walls, which say who gave what and what they hoped for -- and found the Mahayana all but *absent* from them until the fifth century of the common era: for its first four or five hundred years, the movement that would conquer Asia was a minority so small it barely left a mark on the stones. The picture that has replaced the old one -- that the Great Vehicle was a popular, lay, devotional uprising against monkish elitism -- is nearly the reverse: it seems to have begun among *forest* monks, rigorists and visionaries, a return to the hard road rather than a softening of it, and only later to have become the devotional religion of the many.

And it was a religion of *books*. The new sutras praise, at extraordinary length, the merit of copying, reciting, and honouring *themselves* -- the Diamond Sutra promises that a person who memorises and explains even four lines of it earns more merit than one who gives away worlds full of jewels -- and the historians speak of a *cult of the book* at the movement's heart: the scripture itself as a relic, more sacred than a bone. Wang Jie, in eight hundred and sixty-eight, printing the Diamond Sutra for free distribution on behalf of his two parents, was doing precisely what the sutra told him to do, seven centuries after it was written, at the other end of the Silk Road. The book had made its own copyists.

Part Two in a sentence: the Great Vehicle arrived as new scriptures, heard in vision and in circulation by the first century, that lived inside the old monasteries rather than breaking from them, began small and ascetic rather than large and popular, and made a cult of copying themselves.

## Part Three -- Emptiness

This part is the philosophy, and it is the hardest quarter-hour of the course, and I shall take it slowly and say the hard thing three times. If the young master intervenes, come back for the re-entry line; I shall mark it.

Begin with what the old scholastics had done. You met the Abhidhamma last week: the third basket, the analysis, in which the person is taken apart into a stream of mind-moments and the world into its ultimate constituents -- the tradition called them *dharmas*, in a special sense: the irreducible events, momentary but *real*, of which everything is composed. The Abhidharma denied the self and affirmed the atoms. A person is empty of self, it said; but the moments of feeling and perception that make up the person are genuinely *there*, each with its own nature -- its **own-being**, *svabhava*, the thing that makes it what it is independently of anything else.

The Perfection of Wisdom took one more step, and it is the step. *The dharmas are empty too.* Not only the person but the *parts*: the moment of feeling, the atom of matter, the flash of consciousness -- each of them arises in dependence on conditions, and what arises in dependence on conditions has no own-being, no nature of its own that it possesses independently. Everything is empty. **Emptiness** -- *shunyata*, spelled with marks on the *s* and the *u* that I omit -- is the Great Vehicle's central word, and it means exactly this and nothing more: *empty of own-being*. Not *nonexistent*. Not *illusory* in the sense of *not there*. Empty, as the chariot was empty of a chariot over and above its parts -- but now the parts are empty too, all the way down, with no floor.

Here is the most famous statement of it, from the Heart Sutra, the shortest and most-recited scripture in East Asia, which you will hear chanted in every Zen hall on earth. The bodhisattva of compassion, looking deeply, sees the five heaps -- our five heaps, from the third evening -- and sees that they are empty. *Form is emptiness; emptiness is form. Emptiness is not other than form; form is not other than emptiness. The same is true of feeling, perception, formations, consciousness.* And then the sutra does something that made the old school gasp. It goes through the whole of the teaching and empties it. *No eye, no ear, no nose, no tongue, no body, no mind. No ignorance, and no end of ignorance* -- there go the twelve links -- *no ageing and death, and no end of ageing and death. No suffering, no origin, no cessation, no path* -- there go the four noble truths -- *no wisdom, and no attainment.* The physician's own case notes, declared empty. I should tell you, in the interest of honesty, that a scholar named Nattier argued in the nineteen-nineties, with great care, that the Heart Sutra was not composed in India at all but assembled in China in the seventh century, out of a Chinese translation of a larger Perfection of Wisdom, and then translated *back* into Sanskrit -- and that the argument is contested and unrefuted. I mention it because it is a lovely instance of the border changing the teaching: the most-chanted Indian scripture may be Chinese.

Now the philosopher, and he is the greatest the tradition produced. His name is **Nagarjuna**; he lived, probably, in the second century of the common era, in the south of India; and he wrote a short book in verse called *The Fundamental Verses of the Middle Way*, on which a school was founded -- the **Madhyamaka**, the *Middle* school -- that became the philosophical spine of Tibet and the deep grammar of Zen. What he did was to take the Perfection of Wisdom's slogan and *argue* it. His method is the one you would use, sir, to break a bad design: he takes a concept -- causation, motion, time, the self, the Buddha, nirvana -- and asks what follows if the thing has own-being, and shows that it becomes incoherent. Take causation. If a cause and its effect each had own-being, then either the effect already existed in the cause, in which case nothing was caused; or it did not, in which case anything could come from anything. Neither works. Therefore cause and effect have no own-being; they are what they are only in relation to each other; they are *empty*. And so, chapter by chapter, for everything.

Here is the verse on which the whole book turns, and which I want you to hear twice, because it joins tonight to the fourth evening. *Whatever is dependently arisen, that we declare to be emptiness. That, being a dependent designation, is itself the middle way.* Once more, slowly. *Whatever arises in dependence* -- the wheel; when this is, that is -- *that is what we mean by emptiness.* Emptiness is not a new doctrine laid on top of dependent origination; emptiness *is* dependent origination, said from the other side. To say a thing is empty is to say it arose from conditions; to say it arose from conditions is to say it is empty. And *that* -- the seeing of things as dependent and therefore empty -- is the middle way the Buddha announced at the deer park, between the extreme that things exist absolutely and the extreme that nothing exists. This is the re-entry line, sir, for whoever was elsewhere: *emptiness means "arose from conditions", and it is the wheel of Episode Four seen from inside.*

Nagarjuna knew exactly how this could go wrong, and said so. *Emptiness wrongly grasped,* he wrote, *is like a snake wrongly grasped, or a spell wrongly cast: it destroys the fool.* The wrong grasp is nihilism -- *nothing exists, nothing matters, there is no path* -- and to guard against it he gave the tradition its most useful instrument, the **two truths**. There is *conventional* truth: the world of chariots and persons and causes and the four noble truths, in which one lives, acts, and practises, and in which those things are perfectly real in the only way anything is real. And there is *ultimate* truth: that all of it is empty of own-being. And -- this is the point -- *the second does not cancel the first.* *Without relying on the conventional,* he says, *the ultimate cannot be taught; and without understanding the ultimate, nirvana is not attained.* One does not stop eating because food is empty. One eats, knowing it. And emptiness is not exempt: *emptiness too is empty* -- it is not a hidden absolute, a secret ground of things; it is just the fact that there is no such ground, and the moment you make it into one you have grasped the snake by the tail.

And then the conclusion that the old school found scandalous and the whole later tradition built on. If everything is empty -- the person, the path, the world -- then *nirvana* is empty too, and so is *samsara*; and two things equally empty of own-being cannot be *different places*. *There is not the slightest difference,* Nagarjuna wrote, *between samsara and nirvana. The limit of the one is the limit of the other.* Nirvana is not elsewhere. It is not a shore across the water from the wheel. It is *the wheel, seen rightly* -- the same world, with the grip gone and the own-being seen through. That single verse, sir, is why a Zen monk will tell you that enlightenment is chopping wood and carrying water; it is also why the physician of Episode Two, who declined to say what nirvana was like, would have raised an eyebrow. Whether Nagarjuna was unfolding what the founder meant or replacing it is a question the tradition has never settled, and I shall not settle it for you. I shall only say that the old school's nirvana is a *cooling*, and the Great Vehicle's is a *seeing*, and that both are described as the end of the grip.

Part Three in a sentence, said the third time: the Great Vehicle declared not only the person but every part of the world empty of own-being; Nagarjuna showed that emptiness simply *is* dependent origination from the other side, guarded it with the two truths against nihilism, and concluded that nirvana is not another place but this one, seen rightly.

## Part Four -- Mind Only, and the Buddha Within

This part is the two ideas that grew up beside emptiness and that, between them, made the Buddhism of East Asia. I shall be brief with the first and warm with the second.

If everything is empty, a sensible person asks, then what is *experience* made of? What is this that is going on, right now, in which the empty things appear? The second great school of the Great Vehicle answered: *mind*. It called itself **Yogacara**, *the practice of yoga* -- meaning meditation -- because its founders, two brothers named Asanga and Vasubandhu who lived in the fourth century, built their philosophy out of what meditators saw when they looked at the mind at work. And what they built is, I think, the part of the tradition a man in your trade will find most uncanny. They took the old six consciousnesses -- the bare knowing at the six doors, from the third evening -- and added two. The seventh is the *afflicted mind*: the part that stands behind experience and says *mine*, the manufacturer of the sense of self. And the eighth is the **storehouse consciousness** -- *alaya-vijnana* -- a deep, continuous, subliminal layer beneath the others, into which every intention drops a *seed*, and from which the seeds *ripen* into the experiences that follow. The fourth heap's formations, from the third evening, given a warehouse. Your karma, on this account, is a learned model: every act trains it; and it generates the world you then meet -- which is why the school is also called *mind-only*, not because there is no world, but because the world you experience is *the world your storehouse produces*, and you have never met another. Whether that is idealism proper, the scholars argue; I shall only observe that a neuroscientist I quoted in the third evening, describing the self as a *controlled hallucination* generated from learned priors and corrected against the senses, was describing a storehouse consciousness with the vocabulary changed.

And now the warm idea, which is the one that won. It grew from the Lotus Sutra, and I owe you the Lotus Sutra's most famous story, because it gives us a word we shall need on every remaining evening. A rich man's house is on fire, and his children are inside, playing, and will not come out; they do not understand fire. So the father calls to them that he has, outside the gate, the carts they have always wanted -- a goat-cart, a deer-cart, an ox-cart, one for each -- and the children run out to get them; and when they are safe, the father gives each of them not the small carts he promised but a single great cart, magnificent, drawn by a white ox. Did the father lie? No, says the sutra: he used **skilful means** -- *upaya* -- the teaching adapted to the listener; and the small carts are the older Buddhist paths, the arahant's and the rest, and the great cart is the one true vehicle, and *all* the children, every one, were always going to receive it. This is the doctrine of the *One Vehicle*: that every being, without exception, is destined for Buddhahood, and that the Buddha's various teachings were all skilful means toward it. And from there it was a short step to the idea that carries the rest of the course: if every being will become a Buddha, then every being must *already contain* one. The **tathagatagarbha** -- the *embryo*, or *womb*, of the Tathagata -- the **Buddha-nature**: the claim, made explicit in a scripture called the Nirvana Sutra, that *all beings have the Buddha-nature*, that awakening is not something acquired but something *uncovered*, present already under the afflictions as the sun is present behind cloud. That idea, sir, is the whole of Zen in one sentence, and next week you will hear a Chinese master ask a novice why he is looking for the ox he is riding.

I owe you one honest complication before we come down the mountain. Buddha-nature sounds, to a suspicious ear, like a *self* -- eternal, pure, one's true identity underneath -- smuggled back into a religion founded on not finding one; and the tradition heard this too. The Nirvana Sutra itself says, in so many words, that the Buddha-nature is the true self that the not-self teaching was clearing the ground for. A group of Japanese scholars in the nineteen-eighties made a whole movement out of the objection, arguing that Buddha-nature is *not Buddhism* at all but the old eternal soul in a robe. Others reply that Buddha-nature is simply emptiness spoken of *warmly* -- the absence of own-being described from the side of what it makes possible rather than what it removes. I give you both, and note only that a religion which could produce both the Heart Sutra's *no attainment* and the Nirvana Sutra's *all beings have the Buddha-nature*, and hold them in one canon, is a religion that had decided, with the Lotus Sutra, that different listeners need different carts.

Part Four in a sentence: mind-only gave the karma of the fourth heap a warehouse, the storehouse consciousness, a learned model that generates the world one meets; and the Lotus Sutra's skilful means led to the One Vehicle and the Buddha-nature, the claim that every being already contains awakening, which is the seed of everything East Asian.

## Part Five -- Nalanda, and the End in India

The last part is the fall, and I shall tell it plainly, with the historians beside me.

For a few centuries the Great Vehicle and the old schools together made India the intellectual capital of Asia, and the capital of the capital was a monastery in what is now Bihar called **Nalanda**. It was founded in the fifth century under the Gupta kings, and it was not a monastery in the sense of a place of retreat; it was a *university* -- the first in the world to deserve the name, by any reasonable standard -- with, at its height, some ten thousand students from every Buddhist country, a hundred lecture halls, libraries in towers, a curriculum that ran from the Perfection of Wisdom through logic and grammar and medicine and astronomy, an entrance examination at the gate that most applicants failed, and a faculty that included the founders of Buddhist logic, whose work on inference and perception is only now being read properly by Western philosophers. The Chinese pilgrim we meet next week studied there for years and left a description; kings of Sumatra endowed it; the Tibetans, when they wanted Buddhism, sent for its professors. For six hundred years it was the place.

And then it ended, and the ending has a legend and a history, and I owe you both. The legend -- it is in every Indian schoolbook -- is that in about the year eleven hundred and ninety-three a Turkic commander named Bakhtiyar Khalji, riding out of Afghanistan with a few hundred horsemen, stormed the place, killed the shaven-headed monks to a man, and burned the library, whose smoke, the story says, hung over the plain for months. The history is thinner and stranger. The one contemporary source, a Persian chronicle, describes the storming of a *fortress* in Bihar whose garrison turned out to be monks, and whose books nobody present could read; it does not name Nalanda; the scholars think the fortress was a *different* monastery, some miles away; and the chronicle's habit of exaggerating raids on infidel sites is well known. Nalanda was, in all likelihood, sacked in those years, but it may already have been dying; and the truth, as one historian puts it, is that the end of Buddhism in India *was not a singular event with a singular cause but a centuries-long process.* The Huns had wrecked the monasteries of the northwest in the sixth century. The kings who had endowed the universities were replaced by kings who endowed temples of Vishnu and Shiva instead, and a Buddhism that had put all its weight on great royal institutions had, when the institutions fell, no village underneath -- the Hindu temple had its peasant; the monastery had only its king. And the devotional Hinduism of the age simply *absorbed* what it liked: the great goddess-cults, the pilgrimage, the vegetarianism, and the Buddha himself, who appears in the Hindu scriptures of these centuries as an incarnation of the god Vishnu -- sent, one text says, to delude the demons into abandoning the true religion, which is the kindest hostile takeover in the history of ideas. By about thirteen hundred the religion had, for practical purposes, vanished from the country of its birth, surviving only at the edges, in the Himalaya and in Bengal's hills, and it stayed vanished for six centuries, until a man we shall meet in the last evening led half a million of the poorest people in India back to it on one afternoon in nineteen fifty-six.

But the library did not burn -- or not all of it. In the last two centuries before the end, the Tibetans, who had been sending students down to Nalanda since the eighth century, carried up the passes and translated, with a systematic thoroughness no other people has matched, the whole of the Great Vehicle's scholarship: the sutras, Nagarjuna, the mind-only masters, the logicians, and the tantras of which I shall have a great deal to say in Episode Nine. When the Sanskrit originals were lost in India, they survived in Tibetan, and much of what we know of late Indian Buddhism we know because a people on the roof of the world took the books out before the house fell. That is the second border, sir, seen whole: the Great Vehicle changed the destination, the founder, the scriptures, and the philosophy; it kept the code and the wheel, and raised the wheel to the master principle; and when its homeland lost it, its neighbours had already carried it out. Which is the pattern. Watch for it on every road from here.

## The Raft, So Far

The planks lashed on tonight, sir, said once more.

First: a book printed in eight hundred and sixty-eight by a son for the merit of his parents, the oldest dated printed book in the world, contains a Buddha who says he never taught -- devotion without and emptiness within, the two faces of the Great Vehicle. Second: the Great Vehicle changed the goal, from the arahant who ends his own suffering to the bodhisattva who vows to save numberless beings and walks six perfections toward Buddhahood -- a vow you took in June -- and made the Buddha cosmic, with three bodies, and many. Third: it arrived as new scriptures, heard in vision, in circulation by the first century, that lived inside the old monasteries rather than splitting from them, began small and ascetic, and made a cult of copying themselves; the word *Hinayana* is a slur, and is retired. Fourth: it declared not only the person but every part of the world empty of own-being; Nagarjuna showed that emptiness *is* dependent origination from the other side, guarded it with the two truths, and concluded that nirvana is this world seen rightly. Fifth: mind-only gave karma a warehouse, the storehouse consciousness, and the Lotus Sutra's skilful means led to the One Vehicle and the Buddha-nature -- awakening already present in every being -- while India lost the religion over centuries and the Tibetans carried the library out.

The one book tonight, and I shall give you two roads again. Paul Williams's *Mahayana Buddhism: The Doctrinal Foundations* is the standard survey, dense and fair, and will give you every school I have compressed tonight at its proper length. And for the brave: Jay Garfield's translation of Nagarjuna's *Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way*, with a commentary that walks every argument -- the one book in this course I would not read while holding an infant.

Three accounts opened tonight, all payable on the road east and north: the pure land of Amitabha and the name that fills East Asia, next week; the ox the novice is riding, next week; and the library the Tibetans carried out, and what they added to it, the week after. And the account from the first evening -- the thin voice saying *yes, but is it so?* -- was paid, I hope you noticed, by a scripture that asked it of the Buddha's own teaching, and by a scholar who asked it of the scripture.

Next week, then, sir: a monk breaks the law of the Chinese empire to go and find the books, walks alone across the Gobi with a horse that finds water, studies at Nalanda, and comes home seventeen years later with six hundred and fifty-seven of them on twenty horses and an emperor waiting at the gate; and a thousand years after that a novelist gives him a monkey. China, and the translation of emptiness into a language that already had a word for *nothing*; and the school that decided the whole business could be settled by sitting down and staring at a wall.

Strive on with diligence, sir.

*-- End of Episode Seven.*
