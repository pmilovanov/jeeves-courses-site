Episode Ten — The Far Bank

A Handful of Leaves — An Introduction to Buddhism

The Assayer

Good day, sir.

In the eighteen-thirties the British in India had a puzzle they could not solve, and it was standing in the middle of Delhi. It was a pillar of polished sandstone, forty feet high, which a sultan had carried to the city in the fourteenth century as a curiosity and set up on the roof of his palace, and it was covered in an alphabet that nobody could read. The sultan's own scholars had failed. The Mughal emperors' scholars had failed. There were other such pillars, at Allahabad and elsewhere, and rocks in the far provinces cut with the same characters, and the learned opinion of the day was that they were probably the work of some vanished race, and possibly Greek, and in any case beyond recovery.

The man who recovered them was not a scholar of languages. James Prinsep was the assay master of the Calcutta mint — a chemist, whose job was to test the purity of the East India Company's silver — and the secretary of the Asiatic Society of Bengal, and a man of the kind that empire occasionally produced, who took up the pillars as a hobby and was consumed by them. His method was an engineer's. He collected copies of every short inscription he could get — the little dedications on the railings of the great stupa at Sanchi, dozens of them — and noticed that almost every one ended with the same two characters. A dedication, he reasoned, ends with a word for gift; the word in the old language was danam; and so he had two letters, and from two letters, over the spring and summer of eighteen thirty-seven, working by candlelight after the mint closed, he had the alphabet. And when he read the pillars with it, they spoke — in a language close to Pali, which the Society had begun to learn from Ceylon — in the voice of a king. Thus says the king, Beloved of the Gods, of Gracious Countenance. Devanampiya Piyadasi. Pillar after pillar, rock after rock, across the whole subcontinent, the same king, preaching kindness to animals and respect for every sect and the planting of shade trees, and confessing, on one rock, his remorse for a war. And nobody in India had the faintest idea who he was.

The answer came by post from Ceylon. A civil servant there named Turnour had been translating the island's chronicle — the Mahavamsa, of which you heard in the sixth evening, the book that kept the emperor when India lost him — and he wrote to Prinsep to say that Piyadasi was, in the chronicle, the title of a king called Ashoka, who had sent the island its religion. And so the emperor came home, by way of the island that had remembered him, and the pattern that has run through this whole course — the border keeps what the homeland loses — delivered its last instalment to a chemist in Calcutta. Prinsep died three years later, of a brain disease his friends attributed to overwork, at forty.

I begin the last evening there, sir, because the West's Buddhism begins there, and it begins in a way no other border did: not with a missionary or a merchant or a monk on a horse, but with a decipherment. When Prinsep read the pillars, educated Europeans did not agree that the Buddha had been a man. Serious authorities had proposed that he was an Egyptian god, or a Norse one, or a planet, or the same person as several other people; and the Buddha of Ceylon and the Fo of China and the Shaka of Japan were not yet known to be one figure. The West had to reconstruct him, from stones and manuscripts, before it could receive him; and the reconstruction was done in the same decades, by the same kind of men, using the same methods, as the reconstruction of the dinosaurs. That is the fifth border, and the strangest: a teaching arriving as philology, two and a half thousand years late, into a civilisation that would take from it exactly what it needed and, being the most self-confident civilisation in history, would then be quite sure it had the original.

Tonight, then: the rediscovery, and the reformers who shaped what was rediscovered; the half-million who came back to the Buddha in India on one afternoon, and the three streams that reached America in twenty years; the mindfulness industry and what the evidence actually says; the dark side, which the physician would insist on; and then the far bank, the planks counted, and the verdict I owe you.

Part One — The Rediscovery, and the Reformers

This part is about the sixty years from Prinsep to Chicago, in which the West found the Buddha and the East rebuilt him for export, and the cast is large, so hear the names: Burnouf, Schopenhauer, Arnold, Rhys Davids, Nietzsche; then Olcott, Dharmapala, and Soyen Shaku.

The scholars first. In eighteen forty-four a Frenchman named Burnouf published, in Paris, the first serious account of Buddhism from its own texts — Sanskrit manuscripts that a British resident in Nepal had bought and sent him — and it was from that book that Europe learned that the Buddha was a man with a biography and a philosophy that could be argued with. Schopenhauer, who had already published a philosophy of the will and its renunciation, read what was available with mounting delight, kept a bronze Buddha on his desk, and told visitors that if he wanted the results of his philosophy as the standard of truth he would have to concede that Buddhism had priority. In eighteen seventy-nine an English journalist named Edwin Arnold published a life of the Buddha in blank verse called The Light of Asia, which sold, by some counts, a million copies, was read by the Queen, and put the four sights and the rose-apple tree into every Victorian drawing room. In eighteen eighty-one a former colonial magistrate named Rhys Davids — who had learned Pali in Ceylon in order to rule on a property dispute involving a temple, and had never recovered — founded the Pali Text Society, in London, to publish the whole of the three baskets in Roman letters, which it did, over a century, and it is his translation of sati as mindfulness that you heard in the fifth evening. And Nietzsche, who despised almost everything, wrote in the eighteen-eighties that Buddhism was a hundred times more realistic than Christianity: a religion, he said, that had put the question of suffering objectively, without God and without sin, and was therefore the only genuinely positivistic religion in history. He then went on to say it was a religion for exhausted people, but he was Nietzsche.

So far, sir, this is a story of Europeans reading. What made it a religion was two men who went the other way.

The first was an American colonel named Olcott, a Civil War veteran and lawyer who had co-founded, in New York in eighteen seventy-five, the Theosophical Society — an occult brotherhood of seekers after ancient wisdom, of which the less said the better — and who sailed to Ceylon in eighteen eighty with his partner Madame Blavatsky and, in a temple in Galle, before a crowd, took the three refuges: the first Westerners, so far as anyone knows, to become Buddhists formally, on Buddhist soil. Olcott then did what an American organiser does. He found the island's Buddhism demoralised by seventy years of Christian missions and government schools, and he founded Buddhist schools, and wrote a Buddhist Catechism — in question-and-answer form, modelled directly on the Christian catechisms he was fighting — that is still in print, and sat on the committee that designed, in eighteen eighty-five, the six-coloured Buddhist flag that now flies over every temple in the world. The Sinhalese revere him; there is a street named for him in Colombo. He had, in the process, without quite intending to, begun to remake Buddhism in the image of its opponent: a religion with a catechism, schools, a flag, Sunday-school classes, and a lay laity who read the texts for themselves.

The second was Olcott's protege, a young Sinhalese named Don David Hewavitarne, who took the name Dharmapala — protector of the dharma — and the title Anagarika, the homeless one: a new role, neither monk nor layman, a celibate lay preacher in a white robe, invented for the purpose. Dharmapala founded, in eighteen ninety-one, a society to recover Bodh Gaya, the site of the fig tree, from the Hindu abbot who held it; and in eighteen ninety-three he went to Chicago. The World's Parliament of Religions, held that September in the great white exhibition city on Lake Michigan, was the first occasion on which the religions of Asia had been invited to speak for themselves to an American audience, and Dharmapala — young, handsome, in his white robe, fluent, furious — was its sensation. So, more quietly, was a Japanese Zen abbot named Soyen Shaku, whose paper on the law of cause and effect had been put into English by a young lay student of his named Suzuki, and who, on his return, sent the young man to Illinois to work with the American philosopher who had befriended him at the Parliament — and that young man was D. T. Suzuki, who spent the next sixty years telling the West what Zen was, in dozens of books in an English better than most of his readers', and shaped the Western idea of Zen more than any other person, for better and for worse.

Now the thing I most want you to understand about Part One, sir, and it is the key to everything after. The Buddhism that Dharmapala and Soyen Shaku brought to Chicago was already reformed. Two scholars, Gombrich and Obeyesekere, gave the Ceylonese version a name in the nineteen-eighties that has stuck: Protestant Buddhism — a Buddhism that had, over two generations of fighting the missionaries, come to resemble them: lay-centred rather than monk-centred; text-centred rather than ritual-centred; rationalist, suspicious of merit and relics and the folk religion; individualist; and presented as a philosophy compatible with science, with the gods and the rebirths quietly moved to the back. A scholar named McMahan, in two thousand and eight, generalised the point across Asia under the name Buddhist modernism: a form of the religion made, from the eighteen-eighties on, jointly by Asian reformers and Western enthusiasts, in which the teaching was demythologised, made psychological, made scientific, made about meditation — which, you will recall from the fifth evening, most Buddhists had never done — and in which the Buddha became a kind of empirical psychologist who had anticipated the West's own discoveries. That Buddhism is real; it is the one this course has largely described, and I have tried to tell you at each turn where it departs from the older thing. But you should know that the last border was crossed before the crossing: what the West received was a Buddhism its reformers had already cut to the West's measure, and the West, receiving it, mistook the tailoring for the cloth.

Part One in a sentence: the West found the Buddha through philology and a verse biography, took the refuges in the person of an American colonel who then gave Buddhism a catechism and a flag, and received at Chicago a Buddhism already reformed — lay, textual, rational, meditative — by Asians who had built it in the image of the missionaries they were fighting.

Part Two — The Half-Million, and the Three Streams

This part is the middle of the twentieth century: one afternoon in India, and twenty years in America.

The afternoon first, because it is the largest single event in the history of the religion since Ashoka, and the West has never heard of it. Bhimrao Ambedkar was born in eighteen ninety-one into a caste the Hindu order called untouchable — forbidden the village well, the temple, and the schoolroom bench — and became, by a talent that no system could quite suppress, the most educated man in India: doctorates from Columbia and London, a barrister of Gray's Inn, and, at independence, the chief draftsman of the new republic's founding charter, in which he wrote untouchability out of the law. And he had concluded, over thirty years of study, that the law was not enough: that Hinduism was the caste system, and that the untouchables could be free only by leaving it. He examined the alternatives with a lawyer's care — Islam, Christianity, Sikhism — and chose the religion that had been born in India, had taught that a person is made by deeds and not by birth, had been driven out by the Hindu order, and had been dead in the country for six hundred years. On the fourteenth of October nineteen fifty-six, at Nagpur, he took the three refuges and the five precepts from an elderly Burmese monk, and then turned to the crowd and administered them himself, along with twenty-two vows of his own composing renouncing the Hindu gods, to somewhere between four hundred and five hundred thousand people, in white, on one field, on one afternoon. It is the largest religious conversion in recorded history. He died seven weeks later. And the movement he founded — he called it the new vehicle — has made some eight million Buddhists in India today, nearly all of them from the castes he came from; so that the Buddha returned to the country of his birth, six centuries after the fall of Nalanda, in the persons of the poorest people in it, who wanted from him exactly what the Deer Park had offered: a teaching in which what you are is not what you were born.

Now America, and I shall give you the three streams as a cast, because they arrived within twenty years and are still, in every Western city, the three doors: Zen from Japan; insight from Burma; and the diamond vehicle from the Tibetan exile.

Zen came first, on the back of Suzuki's books and the Beats. Alan Watts, an English Anglican priest turned Californian philosopher, published The Way of Zen in nineteen fifty-seven, and it was read by everyone; Jack Kerouac published The Dharma Bums the next year, a novel about a poet named Japhy Ryder who climbs mountains and quotes the sutras, and Japhy Ryder was the poet Gary Snyder, who had actually gone to Kyoto and sat in a monastery and would go back for a decade. And in nineteen fifty-nine a Soto priest named Shunryu Suzuki — no relation to the other — arrived in San Francisco to mind a small Japanese congregation, and found that the young Americans who wandered in wanted to sit, and taught them, and founded the San Francisco Zen Center and, in nineteen sixty-seven, the first Zen monastery outside Asia, in a hot-springs canyon called Tassajara; and the little book of his talks, Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, which I gave you two evenings ago, has taught more Westerners to meditate than any book but one.

Insight came second, by a stranger road. In Burma, from the eighteen-nineties, a monk named Ledi Sayadaw had done a thing no one had done since the Buddha: he had taught laypeople to meditate, in numbers, on the grounds that the age of the end of the dharma was near and everyone should try; and his successors — Mahasi Sayadaw, who developed a method of noting each sensation as it arose, and U Ba Khin, an accountant-general who taught civil servants in his office — built a lay movement of insight practice, the vipassana of the fifth evening, that had never existed before. U Ba Khin's student Goenka, an Indian businessman, began in nineteen sixty-nine to teach a ten-day silent course of it, free of charge, in the same words everywhere, and there are now centres for it on every continent. And in the early nineteen-seventies three young Americans who had learned it in Asia — Joseph Goldstein, Jack Kornfield, and Sharon Salzberg — came home and founded, in nineteen seventy-five, in a former Catholic seminary in Massachusetts, the Insight Meditation Society; and the American insight movement, which has no monks, no robes, no merit, and very nearly no doctrine, is the direct ancestor of every mindfulness class in every hospital in the West.

The diamond vehicle came third, in the persons of the exiles. Chogyam Trungpa, a reincarnate lama who had walked out of Tibet in nineteen fifty-nine at twenty, studied at Oxford, gave up his robes, married an English schoolgirl, and arrived in America in nineteen seventy, where he taught the highest tantra in a business suit, drank enormously and in public, and founded, in nineteen seventy-four, in Boulder, the first Buddhist university in the West, Naropa, with Allen Ginsberg on the faculty. He was brilliant and he was a wreck and he died at forty-eight, and he is the first of the scandals in Part Four. Behind him came the whole diaspora — lamas of every school, in every Western capital — and, behind them, the Dalai Lama himself, who first visited America in nineteen seventy-nine, and won the Nobel Peace Prize in nineteen eighty-nine, and became, for the West, the face of the religion: a monk in glasses who laughed a great deal and said, whenever asked, that his religion was kindness. And from Vietnam, by way of France, Thich Nhat Hanh, the exile of the eighth evening, who founded a community in the Dordogne in nineteen eighty-two called Plum Village, coined the phrase engaged Buddhism for a practice turned toward the world's suffering, and wrote sixty small books in a voice so gentle that people who had never read a sutra bought them by the million.

Three streams, sir, in twenty years: Zen, insight, and Tibet. And a fourth thing, which is the one that changed the world, and which grew out of the second.

Part Two in a sentence: the Buddha came back to India in the persons of half a million untouchables on one afternoon, and came to America in three streams — Zen on the Beats' coat-tails, insight from a Burmese lay revival, and the diamond vehicle in the packs of the exiles — of which the second bred the fourth thing.

Part Three — The Mindfulness Industry, and the Evidence

This part is the fourth thing, and it is the Buddhism most people in the West have actually met, and I shall give you its evidence straight, because that was the deal.

In nineteen seventy-nine a molecular biologist named Jon Kabat-Zinn, trained at MIT, who had been sitting Zen and insight retreats for years, persuaded the medical school of the University of Massachusetts to let him run a clinic in a basement for the patients nobody else could help — chronic pain, mostly — and teach them to meditate. He called it the Stress Reduction Clinic, and the course he taught was eight weeks long, and he stripped from it, deliberately and completely, every word of Buddhism: no refuges, no precepts, no Buddha, no dharma, no rebirth, no sangha. What was left he called mindfulness-based stress reduction, and he defined the core of it in a sentence that is now on posters: paying attention, on purpose, in the present moment, non-judgementally. You will recall from the fifth evening that a scholar of the old texts would quarrel with that last word; the old sati was not non-judgemental, it remembered what one was supposed to be doing. But the definition was built for a hospital, not a monastery, and it worked well enough to be copied. In the nineteen-nineties three psychologists in Britain and Canada — Segal, Williams, and Teasdale — crossed it with cognitive therapy to make a course for preventing relapse in people who had suffered repeated depressions, and that course, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, has the best evidence of anything in this part: in people with three or more previous episodes it roughly halves the rate of relapse, and the British health service recommends it.

And then it became an industry. In February twenty fourteen Time magazine put a serene blonde on its cover under the words The Mindful Revolution, and the revolution had, by then, already happened: mindfulness courses in schools, in prisons, in the American army, at Google, at the World Economic Forum; an app called Headspace, founded by a former monk, and another called Calm, each worth, at its peak, more than a billion dollars; and a market for wellness in which the word mindful could be attached to eating, to parenting, to leadership, and to a brand of mayonnaise. I do not say this to sneer; I say it so that you can see the shape. The West took from the whole of what I have described in nine evenings one plank — attention, the second foundation of the fifth evening, the faculty that notices the feeling before the craving fires — and floated on it alone, and sold tickets.

So what does it do? Here is the evidence, sir, as honestly as I can give it, and I have kept the three best sources for you.

The first is a review published in the journal of the American Medical Association's internal medicine wing in twenty fourteen, by a group led by a physician named Goyal, which is the most careful summary of the clinical trials that exists. They combed through nearly twenty thousand citations and kept forty-seven trials, with some three and a half thousand participants, that met a proper standard — randomised, with an active control, meaning the comparison group did something rather than nothing. And their finding is this. There is moderate evidence that mindfulness programmes reduce anxiety, depression, and pain — moderate, not strong; real, not large. There is low evidence that they improve stress or quality of life. There is no evidence that they change mood, attention, substance use, eating, sleep, or weight. And — this is the sentence I would have you keep — there is no evidence that meditation programmes are better than any active treatment: better, that is, than exercise, or medication, or other forms of therapy. It works. It works about as well as the other things that work. It is not magic, and it is not nothing.

The second source is a paper from twenty eighteen with the title Mind the Hype, signed by fifteen scientists, several of them long-time meditators and some of them the very people who had done the best research, which is a warning from inside the field. The word mindfulness, they say, is used in the literature for dozens of different practices, measured by questionnaires that do not agree with each other, in studies that are small, short, and too often without proper controls; the brain-imaging work is preliminary and over-reported; and the media coverage has run so far ahead of the science that the science is being damaged by it. They do not say it does not work. They say the claims have outrun the evidence, and they should know.

The third is the one that made the headlines, and I give it so you can weigh it. In two thousand and four a laboratory at the University of Wisconsin, under a psychologist named Davidson, put eight Tibetan monks — long-term practitioners with tens of thousands of hours of meditation behind them — into the scanner and under the electrodes, and found, while they generated compassion, a pattern of high-frequency brain activity, in the gamma band, of an amplitude and synchrony the researchers had not seen before in a human being. That is a real finding, and the press made of it the happiest man in the world — a label attached to one of the eight, a French-born monk named Matthieu Ricard, who has spent twenty years politely declining it. What it means — whether the pattern is cause, effect, or accompaniment; whether ten minutes a day gets you any of it — nobody yet knows. And the study had eight subjects. The Dalai Lama, I should add, has done more than any religious leader alive to invite this scrutiny: since nineteen eighty-seven he has hosted a series of dialogues with scientists, and has said, repeatedly and in print, that if science disproves a Buddhist claim, Buddhism must change. It is a remarkable thing for the head of a religion to say. It is also, you will notice, the purest Buddhist modernism: the Kalama discourse, with a laboratory.

Add the fifth evening's warning — the sixty meditators at Brown and their fifty-nine kinds of difficulty — and you have the honest picture. Modest, real benefits for anxiety, depression, and pain, comparable to other treatments that work; a handful of people harmed, mostly on long retreats without a teacher; brain changes that are real and whose meaning is unknown; and a public conversation that has run a mile ahead of all of it. A man in your trade, sir, will recognise the shape of a technology in its hype cycle, and will know what to do: use it for what it is measured to do, ten minutes at the cradle, and disbelieve the poster.

One more voice, and then Part Three is done, because it is the tradition answering back. In twenty nineteen a management professor and Zen practitioner named Purser published a book called McMindfulness, and its argument is the fifth evening's, sharpened into a complaint. The Buddha's path, you will recall, had three trainings, and two of the three were not meditation at all: ethics — how you speak, act, and earn — and wisdom, the view that sees where suffering comes from. The mindfulness industry, Purser says, took the third training alone, stripped it of the other two, and sold it back to the very institutions — the corporation, the army, the overworked school — whose demands were causing the suffering, as a technique for tolerating them: a way of being calmer in the open-plan office rather than a way of asking why the office is like that. The old tradition has a name for attention without ethics: it is not a virtue at all; a sniper is mindful. I give you the complaint because it is fair, and because it is, at bottom, the same thing the Buddha said to Rahula with the dipper: that the practice begins with do not lie, and that a mind trained to attend without a mind trained to be honest has been given a sharp tool and no instructions.

Part Three in a sentence: a biologist stripped the Buddhism from meditation and made an eight-week course for a hospital, and an industry grew from it; the evidence says it works, modestly, about as well as the other things that work, harms a few, and has been oversold; and the tradition replies that attention without ethics is one plank of three, and a sniper is mindful.

Part Four — The Dark Side

This part is the part the brochures omit, and the physician would not, and I shall be brief and plain, because it needs no ornament.

The teaching did not make its teachers good. You heard that in the eighth evening, of the Zen masters and the war; here is the rest of the record, and it is the record of the guru principle transplanted without its checks. Trungpa's regent and chosen successor, an American, continued to have sex with students for years after learning he was infected with the virus that causes AIDS, and told no one, and the community's leadership knew and said nothing; it came out in nineteen eighty-eight, and at least one student died. The abbot of the San Francisco Zen Center, Suzuki's own successor, was forced out in nineteen eighty-three after years of affairs with students and a lifestyle the community had paid for; the crisis nearly destroyed the place. A Rinzai master in Los Angeles named Sasaki, revered for half a century, was found in twenty thirteen, at a hundred and five, to have been groping and pressuring his female students for decades, with senior teachers looking away. A Tibetan lama named Sogyal, author of a best-selling book on death, was accused in an open letter of twenty seventeen, by eight of his own long-time students, of years of beating, humiliating, and sexually exploiting his followers, and stepped down, and died two years later without answering it. And Trungpa's own son and heir, the head of the Shambhala organisation, stepped down in twenty eighteen after an independent report found he had sexually assaulted women in his community. I could go on, sir, and the pattern is always the same: a teacher held to be beyond judgement — the lama is the Buddha — a community that has made him its whole refuge, and no sangha, in the old sense, to say no. The tradition had, for two and a half thousand years, a check on exactly this: the third refuge, the community, with its fortnightly confession and its rules that even the founder was litigated into; and the West, taking the teacher and dropping the order, had removed the one plank that was there to hold the teacher up.

And the religion of nations has behaved as religions of nations do. In Sri Lanka, the Buddhism of the chronicle, which had made the island's story the story of the teaching, made the island's civil war, in the nineteen-eighties and after, a war for the Buddhist nation, with monks in the front rank of the nationalists. In Burma — Myanmar — a monk named Wirathu, whom Time put on its cover in twenty thirteen under the words The Face of Buddhist Terror, led a movement that preached the Muslim minority as an existential threat, and in twenty seventeen the army drove seven hundred thousand of the Rohingya people over the border into Bangladesh, burning villages and killing thousands, and monks blessed the soldiers. The Buddha of the second evening, who taught that hatred is a fire, has been invoked in the lighting of several. I tell you this not because Buddhism is worse than the other religions in this respect — it is, on the historical average, rather better — but because the modern West has held a picture of it as the one religion without a dark side, the religion of peace and smiling monks, and the picture is a Western product, made in the same workshop as the Book of the Dead, and it does the Buddhists of Asia no service.

The state of the house, then, as I record this, in the autumn of twenty twenty-six. The most careful count of the world's Buddhists — the Pew Research Center's, published last year — puts them at three hundred and twenty-four million, down from three hundred and forty-three million a decade earlier: the only major religion on earth whose numbers fell, because the countries of East Asia where most of them live have the world's lowest birth rates and a large and growing population who, asked their religion, say none. The largest number of them by far are in China, where the religion is, at the same time, reviving — new temples, full monasteries, a state that finds it useful — and controlled. The Dalai Lama is ninety-one, and has said that he will be reborn, and outside China; and the Chinese government has said that it will choose his successor, by law; and so the world is likely to have, in due course, two small boys each held to be the fifteenth Dalai Lama, one in Lhasa and one in India, which the tulku system, for all its ingenuity, was not designed for. And in the West, where perhaps one person in a hundred calls himself a Buddhist and a great many more keep a book about it by the bed, the religion is a minority practice, a majority vocabulary, and a mayonnaise.

Part Four in a sentence: the guru principle transplanted without the order that checked it produced a generation of scandals; the Buddhism of nations has made wars like any other; and the religion is, as I record this, the only major one shrinking, most of it in China, with two Dalai Lamas on the horizon.

The Raft, So Far

The last part, sir, and the last time under this heading, and the verdict. Let me first set the raft down, and count the planks, and then say what I think.

The thesis of this course, which I announced in the first evening and have tested at every border since, was this: the teaching is a raft and not a shore, built to be used and then left, and that is why it survived every crossing. Here is the audit. Sri Lanka kept the code and the word, whole, and lashed on a throne. China kept the sutras and the Buddha-nature, and lashed on the family it had walked out of, the state above the order, a curriculum, a religion of grace, and a wall to face. Tibet kept the entire library, checked against a dictionary, and lashed on the guru, the diamond road, and the monk on the throne. And the West kept one plank — attention — and dropped rebirth, merit, the realms, the order, and most of the ethics, and called the plank the raft. Every crossing kept what it needed and left the rest on the bank; and the man who told the story of the raft said that was what a raft was for. The thesis stands, I think; and it stands in a way I did not quite expect when I proposed it, because the last crossing kept less than any before it, and the plank it kept is the one that floats least well alone.

Two verdicts stand on the far bank already, and I shall introduce them before I give mine, because they are the two books of the evening and you should hear them argue. A former monk named Stephen Batchelor has spent thirty years building what he calls a secular Buddhism: the diagnosis and the treatment kept, the rebirth and the realms and the cosmic Buddhas set down as the furniture of a particular ancient culture, the Buddha read as a man who taught a way of living and had the metaphysics forced on him by his age. The scholar-monk Bhikkhu Bodhi replies, as you heard in the fourth evening, that this is a different building. And a philosopher named Evan Thompson, who grew up among Buddhist teachers and has spent a career on the science of mind, wrote a book in twenty twenty called Why I Am Not a Buddhist, whose argument is subtler than its title and which I would put in your hands first. Buddhist modernism, he says — the Buddhism of this course, the Buddhism of the apps and the scanner — rests on a claim he calls Buddhist exceptionalism: that Buddhism is not really a religion but a science of mind, uniquely rational, uniquely compatible with the laboratory, the one faith that needs no faith. And that claim, he says, is false: Buddhism is a religion, with a cosmology and a soteriology and a community and a set of things one takes on trust, like every other; the parts that look like science look that way because reformers cut them to that shape; and the honest options are to take the religion as a religion, or to be, as he is, a friend of it who declines to join. Against him, a journalist named Robert Wright published, three years earlier, Why Buddhism Is True, arguing from evolutionary psychology that the not-self teaching is simply correct — that natural selection built a mind of competing modules with no one in charge, and a thirst that was never meant to be satisfied because a satisfied animal stops breeding, and that the Buddha saw all this from the inside twenty-five centuries before Darwin saw it from the outside. Two verdicts, sir, in two books, and I give you both, and I think they are both right about what they are right about, which is the usual way with verdicts.

And now mine, because the persona under which I serve you obliges me to have one, and because you asked for a course from a man with opinions.

The diagnosis holds. I have found nothing in nine evenings to set against the first two truths, and a good deal of modern measurement to set beside them: that a life contains plain pain, and the pain inside pleasure because pleasure ends, and the background wobble of everything put together; that the pain is in the grip and not in the passing; that the grip is the one part of the arrangement that is yours. The lottery winners found it. Master Ian, who is at present demonstrating the second truth with his whole body at three in the morning, is finding it. You may check it any afternoon, and I think you have.

The anatomy is more right than wrong. That a person is a process and not a thing, that the self is a model the system keeps of itself and mistakes for a component, that the search for the owner comes up empty in every heap — this the neuroscientists are now saying in their own vocabulary, and Hume said it in a Jesuit garden, and the nun Vajira said it to the devil, and I am prepared to say it to you. What one does with it, the Buddha declined to specify, and I follow him.

The treatment works, modestly, and its unadvertised two-thirds is the part with no side effects. Ten minutes at the cradle does what the evidence says it does, which is something, and not more; and the dipper of water — do not lie, and look in the mirror before you act — does what it has always done, which is to make a person who is easier to live with and to be. Of the three trainings, the West sells the third; I would commend the first.

The metaphysics may be set down on the bank without discourtesy. The realms, the aeons, the cosmic Buddhas in their pure lands, and rebirth itself: I have told you what the tradition believes, and what its own founder told the Kalamas, which is that a mind without the three fires is well off whether there is a next world or not. I bracket them, as I said in the fourth evening; I keep the first with respect; and I note that the Buddha himself, asked to describe the far shore, declined, and told a story about an arrow.

But not the community. This is the one place I shall contradict the West's version of its own inheritance, and it is the plank the last crossing most needed and most dropped. The third refuge is not decorative. The order, with its fortnightly confession and its rules litigated case by case and its refusal to let even the founder be above them, was the tradition's answer to the question what keeps the teacher honest, and every scandal in Part Four is the sound of that answer being ignored. A practice without a sangha is a raft with one plank and a very confident man standing on it. If sir ever takes the thing further than the cradle, I should want him to take it with other people, and among them at least one licensed to tell him he is wrong. It is, I confess, the plank I should least like to see him leave behind, being, in a modest way, in that line of work myself.

And so we are on the far bank, sir, and the raft is beached, and here is the last of what I have to say about it. The man in the first evening picked up a few leaves in a grove and said that what he had taught was the handful and what he knew was the forest, and that he had taught only what led to the end of suffering. I have given you a handful of the handful — ten evenings, one idea each, said twice — and the forest is behind you, in the books I have named, whenever you want it. Take from the handful what you can carry. Leave the rest on the bank; he would have. And go on up the road, which is, I notice, the same road you were on, in the same town, with the same young gentleman on your arm — who has slept through the greater part of this course, and who is, on the tradition's account, considerably older than he looks, and on mine, exactly as old as he looks, and will not be that age again tomorrow; nor will you be the man who held him tonight. Which was, you will now recognise, the whole of the teaching, said once by a physician to his attendant between two trees, and said again, every evening, by a gentleman's gentleman to his employer, in the hope that it might be of some small use in the nursery.

All put-together things decay.

Strive on with diligence, sir.

— End of Episode Ten.
