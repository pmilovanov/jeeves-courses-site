Episode One — The Physician of Kapilavastu

A Handful of Leaves — An Introduction to Buddhism

A Fetter Is Born

Good day, sir.

I should like to begin with a young father, since I am addressing one. He is twenty-nine years old, by the tradition's count. He is well-born, well-fed, and, as far as the record allows us to say, well-married. And on the night his son is born he is brought the news, and he says — the tradition is quite precise about the words — a fetter has arisen; a bond has arisen. And so the boy is named. Rahula. Fetter. And that same night, the story goes, the father stands at the door of the room where his wife and the infant are sleeping, and looks at them, and does not go in, because he is afraid that if he touches the child he will not be able to leave. And then he leaves.

I mention that you are holding, or have lately held, or will shortly be handed back, a young gentleman of eleven weeks, sir, because I think you should hear this story with Master Ian's weight on your arm and not as an abstraction. It is the most awkward fact in the biography of the man this course is about, and a course that tidied it away in the first five minutes would not deserve your attention for the next six hours. The man we call the Buddha walked out on his newborn son. The tradition does not hide this. It names the son after it.

Now, in the spirit of honesty that I intend to keep throughout, I must immediately complicate the story, because the philologists have been at it. The name Rahula, they point out, does not obviously mean fetter at all. It looks like a diminutive of Rahu — Rahu being, in Indian sky-lore, the demon who swallows the sun or the moon and causes an eclipse. On that reading the boy was simply born under an eclipse, or named for one, and the touching story about the fetter is what a later generation of storytellers built to explain a name whose meaning they had lost. The scholar Edward Thomas said as much a century ago, and nobody has refuted him. So we have, in our very first minute, the shape of the whole problem of this man's life: a beautiful story, an ancient text, and a small dry voice at the back of the room saying yes, but is it so?

I shall be that small dry voice as often as I am the storyteller, sir, and I promise always to tell you which hat I am wearing. There is a legend of the Buddha and there is what can actually be known about him, and both are worth having, and they are not the same thing. Tonight's evening is the man. Not yet the teaching — that is next week, when we go to the Deer Park — but the person, what happened to him, and what he said had happened to him, which is not quite the same either.

One more word before we set out, on what this course is and is not. It is an introduction. Ten short evenings, each one idea, each the length of a run, and I shall say the important things twice, because a man with an infant on his shoulder or a hill in front of him cannot be expected to rewind. It is not a conversion. It is not a debunking. It is an attempt to tell you what one of the most consequential human beings who ever lived actually claimed, how the claim travelled, and what a sensible person might make of it now. The title I have chosen for it is borrowed from the man himself, and I shall explain it at the close of the hour, when we have earned it.

Let us go and find him.

Part One — What Can Be Known

This first part is about the ground under the story: when he lived, where, who his people were, and what he was called. It is shorter on marvels than the parts that follow and I ask you to bear with it, because everything after it stands on it.

When did he live? You would think that a man who founded a religion of several hundred million people would have a birthday. He does not. The tradition kept in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia — the oldest continuous lineage, of which more in Episode Six — places his death in the middle of the sixth century before the common era, and counts its own calendar from it. Modern scholars, examining the same chronicles with less piety, found that the tradition's count of the kings and the elders between the Buddha's death and the emperor Ashoka, whom we can date, does not add up; and the consensus now, since a great conference at Gottingen in the late nineteen-eighties, is that the Buddha died around four hundred before the common era — give or take twenty years. That makes him a contemporary of Socrates, roughly, rather than of Pythagoras; a man of the fifth century, whose long life spanned most of it. I shall say the fifth century before the common era whenever I need a date for him, and you will know that the fifth century is itself a scholar's estimate.

Where did he live? Here we are on firmer ground, and the firmness is literal: it is a stone. In the year eighteen ninety-six, at a place called Lumbini, just inside the modern border of Nepal, a party of excavators dug out a pillar of polished sandstone that had been standing there, mostly buried, for two thousand one hundred and fifty years. Cut into it, in the script of the emperor Ashoka, were a few lines saying that the king, in the twentieth year after his anointing, had come here in person and worshipped, because here the Buddha, the sage of the Shakyas, was born; and that he had set up the pillar, and had made the village free of tax, and reduced its share of produce to an eighth. It is worth pausing on that inscription, sir. It was cut about two hundred and fifty years before the common era — roughly a century and a half after the Buddha's death, by the scholars' count — by a ruler with every reason to know, and it is the oldest hard evidence for the man that exists. Everything older is text, copied and recopied. The pillar is the first thing anyone wrote down about the Buddha that we can still touch. And it says: here. I will add, because I promised honesty, that the man who led the dig was later exposed as a forger and dealer in fake antiquities and had to resign in disgrace — but the pillar is not one of his fakes; it has been examined to death since, and it is real.

Two years later, a British estate manager named Peppe, digging into an old mound at a place called Piprahwa on the Indian side of the same border, found a stone box with an inscription saying, in effect, that these were relics of the Buddha, the Blessed One, deposited by his kinsmen the Shakyas. Whether the site was the Buddha's home town, Kapilavastu, is disputed with a Nepalese site across the border, and I shall not adjudicate; but the box exists, and it says what it says.

So: a people called the Shakyas, in the foothills of the Himalaya where the plain of the Ganges runs up into the mountains; a town called Kapilavastu; a birth at Lumbini nearby. Note what the Shakyas were not. The legend makes his father a king and him a prince in a palace. In fact the Shakyas seem to have been a small clan running its affairs as a kind of assembly of elders, an oligarchic republic under the thumb of the larger kingdom of Kosala next door; and the father, Suddhodana, was in all likelihood an elected chieftain or a leading member of that assembly rather than anything with a throne. Comfortable, certainly. Royal, no. The prince in his palace is a later embroidery, and you will find that most of the embroidery on this man's life runs in the same direction: upward, toward the marvellous.

What was he called? This matters, because you will meet him under five or six names and I want you to be able to tell them apart. His clan name was Gautama — Gotama in the older language of the texts, Gautama in the Sanskrit spelling you may have seen — and in the oldest layer of the scriptures that is simply what people call him: the ascetic Gotama, the good Gotama. The personal name you know, Siddhartha, meaning he who has accomplished his aim, does not appear in the oldest texts at all; it arrives later, and one suspects it of being the kind of name a biography gives its hero after the fact. Shakyamuni means the sage of the Shakyas, and it is the name Ashoka's pillar uses; you will hear it most from the Mahayana and the Japanese. Tathagata — thus gone, or thus come; the grammar allows both and the tradition enjoys the ambiguity — is the name he used for himself, in the third person, with a reticence I find rather sympathetic. And Buddha is not a name at all. It is a title, a past participle, from a root meaning to wake. The Buddha is the one who woke up. He is, on his own account, not a god and not the messenger of one; he is a human being who woke up, and who said that the waking could be done by others.

There is a small story about this that I am fond of. A brahmin named Dona, walking a road in the Ganges country, sees the Buddha's footprints in the dust — and the footprints, this being a story, are marked with wheels — and follows them to a tree, where he finds the man sitting. Are you a god? he asks. No, brahmin. A spirit of the air, then? A spirit of the earth? No. Then are you a human being? No, says the Buddha — and explains that whatever it is in a person that would make him be reborn as any of those things, he has cut it off at the root, and that as a lotus grows in the water and stands above it with the water not clinging to it, so he stands in the world, unclung-to. Remember me, he says, as awake. That last is a paraphrase, sir, and I shall tell you when I paraphrase; but it is a fair one, and it is the whole of his claim in one word.

Let me set the scene around him in one paragraph, and then Part One is done. The Ganges valley in the fifth century before the common era was a place in upheaval. Cities were rising for the first time in a thousand years; iron ploughs were opening the forest; small tribal republics like the Shakyas were being swallowed by new kingdoms, Kosala and Magadha, with standing armies and tax collectors. And into this, as into every such moment, came a ferment of teachers. They were called shramanas — strivers, wanderers — and they had walked out of the old religion of the brahmins and its sacrifices to seek something else: some by starving themselves, some by reasoning, some by denying that anything mattered at all. The scriptures preserve, with a certain relish, a list of six rival teachers with six rival doctrines, and one of them, Mahavira, founded the religion of the Jains, which is with us still. The point is this: when the young Gautama walked out of his house, he was not doing something mad or unheard-of. He was joining a movement. The road was full of men in rags looking for the truth. What was unusual was not that he went, but what he came back with.

Here is Part One in a sentence, sir. A real man, of a real clan, in the foothills of the Himalaya, in the fifth century before the common era; comfortable rather than royal; called Gotama by his neighbours, Buddha — the one who woke — by his followers; and one wanderer among many, in an age of wanderers.

Part Two — The Four Sights, and the Man's Own Words

This part sets the famous story of why he left beside the account he gave of it himself. They are different, and the difference is instructive.

The famous story first, because you may already half know it, and because it is beautiful. When the child was born, a sage came to see him and wept, and when asked why, said that he wept for himself, because he would not live to hear this child teach; for the boy would become either a great monarch, a turner of the wheel of empire, or a Buddha, and there was no third possibility. His father, wanting the monarch and not the Buddha, did the thing that fathers in stories do: he tried to prevent the prophecy by force. He built the boy three palaces, one for each season; he surrounded him with dancers and gardens and music; and he gave orders that the prince should never see anything old, or sick, or dead. The prince grew up, and married, and lived in this arranged paradise until he was twenty-nine.

Then one day he ordered his charioteer, Channa, to drive him out to the pleasure-park, and on the road he saw a man bent double, white-haired, trembling on a stick. What is that? he asked, and Channa told him: that is an old man, and all men become so. On the second drive he saw a sick man, lying in his own filth; on the third, a corpse being carried to the burning-ground with the mourners behind it. And each time he asked, and each time Channa told him: this too is the common lot; this comes to everyone; it will come to you. On the fourth drive he saw a shaven-headed man in a yellow robe, walking with a bowl, serene, and asked what that was; and was told: that is one who has gone forth from home to seek an end to all this. And that night his son was born, and that night he rode away on his horse Kanthaka, with Channa beside him, and at the edge of the forest he cut off his hair with his sword and exchanged his silks for a hunter's rags and sent Channa home with the horse, and the horse, the story adds, died of grief.

That, sir, is the legend of the four sights, and a great deal of Buddhist art and every children's book on the subject is built on it. I do not want to take it from you. But I do want to tell you where it comes from, because the answer is rather wonderful. In the oldest scriptures, the story of the four sights is told in full — about somebody else. It is told of a previous Buddha, a legendary figure of the remote past called Vipassi, as the pattern that every Buddha's life follows. The oldest texts do not tell it of Gautama at all. It was only later, when the biographers came to write his life, that they took the pattern of Vipassi and laid it over the man from Kapilavastu, so that his life would have the proper shape. The charioteer, the prophecy, the four drives to the park, the horse: pattern, not memory.

And yet — here is the small dry voice again, this time on the legend's side — not all of it is pattern. In a short discourse in one of the older collections the Buddha describes his own upbringing, and he says, in his own voice, that he was delicate, exceedingly delicate; that lotus ponds were dug in his father's house for his pleasure; and that he had three residences, one for the cold season, one for the hot, and one for the rains, in which he was entertained by musicians and did not come down for four months together. So the three palaces, oddly, are his. And in the same breath he says what he made of them. An ordinary person, he says, though himself subject to ageing, is repelled when he sees another who is old, forgetting that he too will be old; and when I considered this, the intoxication with youth entirely left me. And the same for sickness, and the same for death: the intoxication with health, the intoxication with being alive, left him. That is the seed of the four sights, sir, and it is his: not four drives in a chariot, but three intoxications, and a young man noticing that they had worn off.

So what did the man himself say? There is a discourse — I shall call it, by its English name, the Discourse on the Noble Search — in which the Buddha, late in life, tells a group of monks his own story, and it is very plain. He says: I too, before my awakening, being myself subject to birth, sought what was also subject to birth. Being myself subject to ageing, sickness, death, sorrow, and defilement, I sought what was also subject to those. And then, he says, it occurred to him to ask why — why, being liable to ageing, he should chase after things that age; why, being liable to death, he should pursue things that die. What if he sought instead the unageing, the undying? And so, he says, while still young, a black-haired young man in the prime of life, though my mother and father wished otherwise and wept with tearful faces, I shaved off my hair and beard, put on the yellow robe, and went forth from home into homelessness.

Notice what is not there, sir. No charioteer. No four sights in four drives, no prophecy, no horse dying of grief. What is there instead is something plainer and, to my mind, stronger: a young man who noticed. He noticed that he would get old and that everything he loved would get old; that he would sicken, and die, and so would they; and that the whole of his considerable comfort was arranged around not looking at this. And he found that he could not stop looking at it. The parents weeping is the only decoration, and it is not a decoration at all — it is the one detail a man would remember.

I said we would keep the young master in the room, and this is the moment. Miss Xenia, sir, at four and a half, has almost certainly already asked you the questions the legend says the prince was prevented from asking. Why is that man so old. Where did the cat go. Will you die. Every child asks them; the whole of the legend's machinery, the three palaces and the guards, is a way of saying that this particular man was the one who could not stop asking, and would not accept the answers that let the rest of us go back to dinner. On the man's own account, that is the whole of the origin: not a shock in a pleasure-park, but a refusal to look away.

And so, like the other strivers on the road, he went to find a teacher. He found two, and the Discourse on the Noble Search names them: Alara Kalama, and then Uddaka Ramaputta. What they taught him was meditation — a discipline of the mind carried to great heights of stillness, in which the ordinary furniture of perception is left behind and one rests in states the texts call, without much explanation, the base of nothingness and the base of neither perception nor non-perception. He learned both. He learned them quickly; each teacher offered to make him a co-leader of the community. And each time he declined, for the same stated reason, which is worth memorising because it is the first appearance of the standard he will apply to everything: this teaching does not lead to disenchantment, to dispassion, to peace, to awakening; it leads only to rebirth in that state. In other words: it is an exquisite experience, and when you come out of it you are exactly the man you were, in exactly the world you were in. Very good, said Gautama, in effect; and what then? And he walked on.

Part Two in a sentence: the four sights are a pattern borrowed from a legendary predecessor; the man's own account is that he noticed ageing and death and could not stop noticing; and having noticed, he went to the best teachers of his day, mastered what they had, and found it did not answer the question.

Part Three — The Starving Man, and the Rose-Apple Tree

This part is about six years of self-torture, and the memory that ended them. It is the hinge of the whole life, and I shall take it slowly.

If stillness did not answer, the other great method on the road was pain. The strivers of the fifth century had a theory, roughly, that the body was the problem — that its appetites were what chained one to the wheel of birth and death, and that if the body were sufficiently mortified the spirit would come loose. Gautama, thorough as ever, tried this too, and for a long time, and the account he gives of it in another discourse — the Greater Discourse to Saccaka, if you want the name — is among the most vivid things in ancient literature. He held his breath until, he says, violent winds cut through his head as if a strong man were splitting it with a sharp sword, and there were violent pains in his head as if a strong man were tightening a leather strap around it. He ate less and less: a handful of bean soup, then a little, then a single grain of rice a day, by the tradition's account. My limbs became like the jointed segments of vine stems. My backside became like a camel's hoof. My spine stood out like a string of beads. My ribs jutted out like the rafters of an old roofless barn. The gleam of my eyes sank far down in their sockets, like the gleam of water far down in a deep well. When he touched his belly, he says, he felt his backbone; when he rubbed his limbs, the hair, rotted at the roots, fell out in his hand.

If you wish to see this, sir, it exists. In the museum at Lahore there is a statue, carved by Greek-trained sculptors in the northwest of India perhaps six or seven hundred years after the events, of the Buddha in this condition: every rib visible, the veins standing on the skeletal arms, the eyes gone back into the skull, the face composed. It is one of the most disturbing objects in the history of art, and it was made by believers, as an act of devotion. They wanted it remembered that he went all the way.

And it did not work. That is his own verdict. By this racking practice of austerities, he says, I have not attained any superhuman distinction in knowledge and vision. Could there be another path to awakening? And here is the hinge, and I want you to hear the strangeness of it. What saved him was not a new doctrine, or a vision, or a teacher. It was a memory from childhood. He remembered, he says, a day when his father was at work — the tradition says it was the ploughing festival, the chieftain ceremonially cutting the first furrow — and the small boy had been left in the shade of a rose-apple tree; and sitting there, quite spontaneously, with nothing wanted and nothing feared, he had slipped into a state of deep, calm, concentrated happiness. The texts have a technical name for it, which we shall meet properly in Episode Five: the first jhana, the first of the absorptions. He had not sought it; he was a child; it had simply come, the way it comes to children in the sun. And now, a starving man under another tree, he asked himself: could that be the path?

And then a second thought, which is the one I find most human of all: I am afraid of that happiness. He had spent six years teaching himself that pleasure was the enemy. And then a third thought, which is the beginning of Buddhism: Why am I afraid of a happiness that has nothing to do with sensual desire, nothing to do with anything unwholesome? And he answered himself: I am not afraid of it. But one cannot reach such a state, he saw, in a body reduced to a string of beads. So he ate. He took solid food — rice and gruel; the tradition says a village girl named Sujata brought him milk-rice in a golden bowl, and though her name is not in the oldest account I see no reason to deny her the credit.

Two consequences, one immediate and one enormous. The immediate one: the five ascetics who had been practising beside him, and who had rather admired his ferocity, saw him eat, and were disgusted. The ascetic Gotama has become luxurious, they said; he has given up the struggle and reverted to luxury. And they left him. Remember them, sir: they will be needed at the end of the hour, and they will be needed with exactly this resentment on them. The enormous consequence is the idea the tradition calls the middle way. He had lived the extreme of indulgence, in the comfortable house at Kapilavastu, and it had not answered. He had lived the extreme of self-torture, to the edge of death, and it had not answered. What answered was in neither place; it was a third thing, a road between the two, and the road ran — this is the part nobody expected — through a kind of happiness: not the happiness of the senses, and not its punishment, but a calm, clear, collected state of mind that a child can fall into under a tree and a grown man can learn to cultivate. That the path to the end of suffering should run through a species of joy is, when you think about it, the least obvious idea in the entire story, and it is the one that every other idea in this course stands on. I shall say it again in a moment, in case the young master intervened: not indulgence, not mortification; a middle way, and the middle way is calm.

Part Three in a sentence: six years of pain, honestly reported, failed; a memory of a boy's untroubled calm under a rose-apple tree showed a third road; and the first thing he did on it was eat, and lose his only friends.

Part Four — The Night

This part is about what the tradition calls the awakening, and what a careful listener is entitled to say about it.

He walked, restored, to a place then called Uruvela, on the bank of a river, near the modern town of Bodh Gaya in the Indian state of Bihar, and sat down under a pipal tree — a fig, with heart-shaped leaves that tremble in still air — and resolved not to get up until the thing was done. That tree, or rather its lineage, is still there. A cutting from it was carried to Sri Lanka in the third century before the common era and planted at Anuradhapura, where it is, by common consent, the oldest tree on earth with a recorded planting date; and a cutting from that was brought back to Bodh Gaya when the original died. Buddhists have been sitting under the same family of fig for two thousand three hundred years. The continuity would please an archivist, sir; I merely note it.

The legend gives him an adversary for the night, and I want to introduce him properly because he will be useful to us as long as the course lasts. His name is Mara, which means something like death or the killer, and he is the tempter. In the pictures he comes with an army — elephants, demons, arrows that turn into flowers as they reach the man under the tree — and with three daughters, whose names are, and this is the giveaway, Craving, Discontent, and Lust. In one of the oldest poems in the canon, the Buddha himself lists Mara's army, and the list is not elephants: it is sensual desire, discontent, hunger and thirst, craving, sloth, fear, doubt, hypocrisy and stubbornness, gain and fame and honour, and the praising of oneself and the disparaging of others. That is to say: Mara is not a devil, or not only. Mara is the inside of the man under the tree, arguing back. Every time you meet him in this course — and he comes back, he is a recurring character, he turns up when the Buddha is old and tired and suggests it might be time to die — read him as a personification of exactly what the story says he is: the pull of the mind toward comfort, distraction, and despair. The tradition knew this perfectly well. It just found him more memorable with elephants.

Now, what happened? We have only his own later account, and it is structured, as such accounts usually are, in threes. Through the three watches of the night, he says, three knowledges came to him. In the first watch, he remembered his own past lives — one, two, a hundred, a hundred thousand, through many ages of the world's contracting and expanding: there I was so named, of such a clan, so I ate, so I died, and passing away there I reappeared here. In the second watch, with what the texts call the divine eye, he saw other beings passing away and reappearing, and saw that they went according to their deeds: the ill-conducted to a bad destination, the well-conducted to a good one. And in the third watch — and this is the one the tradition treats as the awakening proper — he saw this is suffering; this is its origin; this is its ceasing; this is the way to its ceasing, and the same for what the texts call the taints, the deep pollutants of the mind; and seeing this, he says, his mind was released. Birth is destroyed. The holy life has been lived. What had to be done has been done. There is no more of this to come.

I want to be careful here, sir, in both directions at once. On the one hand: the first two knowledges are about rebirth, and you may not believe in rebirth, and we shall have an honest conversation about that in Episode Four. On the other hand: the third knowledge, the one the tradition itself puts at the centre, is not a vision of heaven or a message from anyone. It is a diagnosis. It is a claim to have seen, with complete clarity, what suffering is, where it comes from, that it can stop, and how. It is the physician's four questions — what is wrong, what causes it, can it be cured, what is the treatment — and next week's whole evening is that diagnosis, so I shall not spoil it now. What I want you to take from tonight is the kind of thing an awakening was claimed to be. Not a revelation. Not a rapture. The word is bodhi, waking, and the image is exactly that of a man who has been dreaming and now is not. He does not say he has been told something. He says he has seen something that was there the whole time, and that the seeing changed him.

There is one more honest thing to say. We do not know what happened under that tree. We have the report of the man, decades after, in a text written down centuries after that. What we know is that a man went under the tree half-dead from failure, and came out of it with the most durable idea of the next two and a half thousand years, and a serenity that people who met him remarked on for the rest of his life. Something happened. The tradition calls it waking up. I am content to call it that too, provided we both remember that we are taking his word for it, and that he would have been the first to say we should not.

Part Four in a sentence: under a fig tree in the fifth century, the tradition says, he fought his own mind — Mara — and in the third watch of the night saw suffering, its cause, its ending, and the way, and called that seeing waking up.

Part Five — The Hesitation, and the Road to the Deer Park

The last part of the evening is about the weeks after, and it is the part I like best, because it is the part where he nearly did not bother.

He sat, the texts say, for some time under the tree and others near it, savouring what had happened, and he thought about teaching it, and he thought: no. His words, in the Discourse on the Noble Search, are these: This dharma I have attained is deep, hard to see, hard to understand, peaceful, sublime, beyond the reach of reasoning, subtle, to be known by the wise. But this generation delights in attachment, takes pleasure in attachment, rejoices in attachment. For such a generation this is hard to see. If I were to teach it and others did not understand me, that would be wearisome to me; that would be troublesome. And he inclined, the text says, to inaction.

I find that one of the most credible sentences in the scriptures, sir. A man who has just seen something rare looks at the world and calculates, correctly, that it does not want to hear it; and he is tired; and the thought it would be troublesome is exactly the thought one has. The tradition, which cannot leave a man alone with a decision, sends a god to argue with him: Brahma Sahampati, a very great deity, appears and kneels and says the world will be lost if the Buddha does not teach, for there are beings with little dust in their eyes who will understand. Read the god as you read Mara — as the other voice in the same head — and the scene becomes one of the great decisions of history made in the ordinary way, by a man in two minds, under a tree.

What tips it is an image, and I want you to keep it, because it is the tradition's own theory of who this teaching is for. He looks, the text says, at the world as a man standing on the bank of a lotus pond. Some lotuses are born in the water and grow in the water and never rise above it, and stay under the surface, drowned. Some rise to the surface and rest on it. And some grow up out of the water and stand clear of it, and the water does not wet them. So, he thinks, are people: some with much dust in their eyes, some with little, some sharp and some dull, some easy to teach and some hard. And for the sake of those with little dust in their eyes — not everyone, notice; he is not a universalist; he is a physician looking for the patients who can be helped — he decides to teach. Open to them are the doors of the deathless, he says. Let those with ears to hear declare their faith.

Whom to teach? His first thought is his two old teachers, Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta, the men of the deep stillness: they would understand quickly. And he learns — the text does not say how; the tradition says a god told him, and I say he asked around — that both are dead. Alara Kalama seven days ago; Uddaka Ramaputta last night. There is a small grief in that which the text passes over in a line. And so his second thought is the five ascetics, the ones who had walked out on him for eating rice. They had been very helpful to me, he says, without irony, and they were at a place called the Deer Park at Isipatana, near Benares, a good hundred and fifty miles away by the roads of the time. So he walked.

On the road, there is a scene I would not have you miss. A wandering ascetic of another sect, a man named Upaka, meets him coming the other way, and is struck by him — your faculties are clear, friend; your complexion is pure and bright — and asks the natural question: who is your teacher? whose doctrine do you profess? And the Buddha replies, in verse, that he has no teacher; that there is no one like him in the world; that he alone is fully awake, cooled and quenched, and is going to Benares to set the wheel of the teaching in motion in a world gone blind. It is, taken cold, an outrageous speech, and Upaka takes it cold. May it be so, friend, he says — the tone is unmistakable, sir; it is good luck with that — and he shakes his head, and goes off down a side road. The first person the Buddha ever tried to teach walked away from him with a polite noise. The scriptures kept this. I think it does them credit; and I think it will do you good, next week, when you weigh the doctrine, to remember that the tradition's own first listener declined.

And then the Deer Park. The five saw him coming, from a distance, and the text gives us their conference, and it is the conference of every group of ex-colleagues who ever saw the man who left the firm walking back in. Here comes the ascetic Gotama, they said, who lives luxuriously, who gave up the struggle. We will not greet him; we will not stand up for him; we will not take his bowl and robe. But a seat may be put out, if he wants to sit. And then, the text says, as he came nearer, they found themselves unable to keep their pact. One went to meet him and took his bowl. One prepared a seat. One brought water for his feet. They addressed him, as they always had, by name, as friend — and he stopped them. Do not address the Tathagata by name and as friend, he said. The Tathagata is awake. Listen: the deathless has been found. I shall instruct you. And they said, in effect: you? who could not manage it starving? — and he said: have I ever spoken to you like this before? — and they had to admit that he had not. And they sat down, all five, and he began.

What he said to them is next week's evening, sir, and I shall not start it tonight. I shall only tell you its shape, so you can look forward to it. It was short. It was organised like a physician's case notes: here is what is wrong with you, here is what is causing it, here is the good news, here is the prescription. And by the end of it one of the five, a man named Kondanna, understood; and the Buddha, who had walked a hundred and fifty miles in the hope of exactly this, said, aloud and, I think, with something like joy: Kondanna knows! Kondanna knows! One man. That was the whole of the Buddhist religion, on its first day: a teacher, five sceptics, and one of them nodding.

The Raft, So Far

Every evening of this course will end here, sir, in a section under this name, and I owe you the name. It comes from a story the Buddha told, and I want to tell it in full, once, tonight, because it is the frame I intend to hang the whole course on.

A man, he said, is travelling, and comes to a great stretch of water. The near bank is dangerous and frightening; the far bank is safe. There is no ferry and no bridge. So the man gathers grass and sticks and branches and leaves, and binds them together into a raft, and, working with his hands and his feet, he crosses to the far bank. And there, on the far bank, it occurs to him: this raft has been very useful to me. Suppose I lift it onto my head, or onto my shoulder, and carry it wherever I go? And the Buddha asks his monks: would that man be doing what should be done with the raft? No, they say. What should he do? He should beach it on the dry ground, or set it adrift, and walk on unburdened. So too, says the Buddha, I have taught you a teaching that is like a raft: for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding on to. And then the line that I find, of everything he said, the most unexpected from the founder of a religion: when you understand the teaching to be like a raft, you should let go even of the teachings — how much more of what is not the teachings.

That is the deal, sir, as I understand it, and it is a rare deal. The thing on offer is not a set of beliefs to be held for life; it is a vessel for a crossing, to be used and then left on the bank. Each evening of this course will lash on one more plank, and at the end of the last evening we shall stand on the far bank and consider, honestly, which planks you need and which you may leave — and I promise you the man himself would have approved of the leaving. The section is called The Raft, So Far because that is what it is: the planks lashed on tonight, said once more, plainly, for a listener who may have been elsewhere for part of the hour.

Tonight's planks, then. First: there was a man. Not a god, not a myth, though myths have grown on him like ivy: a member of a small hill clan in the fifth century before the common era, called Gotama, whose birthplace a real emperor marked with a real pillar that can be touched today. Second: there is a legend of him and there is his own account, and where they differ, this course will tell you which is which, and will prefer the account. The legend has four sights and a charioteer; the account has a young man who noticed ageing and death and could not stop noticing, and walked out on his weeping parents and his newborn son to find whether there was anything that did not age and die. Third, and this is the plank that bears the weight: he tried the two great methods of his age — stillness, from the best teachers alive, and pain, to the edge of death — and both failed; and what did not fail was a memory of a child's untroubled happiness under a rose-apple tree, which showed him a middle way between indulgence and mortification, a road that ran, to everyone's surprise including his own, through a species of calm. Fourth: under a fig tree he says he saw what suffering is, where it comes from, that it ends, and how — a physician's diagnosis, which he called waking up — and then very nearly decided not to tell anyone, and told five sceptics who had sworn not to stand for him, and one of them understood.

I promised you the title. Late in his life the Buddha was walking in a grove of simsapa trees — a kind of rosewood — and picked up a few leaves in his hand, and asked the monks which were more: the leaves in his hand, or the leaves in the forest overhead. The forest, obviously. Just so, he said: what I have known and not taught you is far more than what I have taught you. And why have I not taught it? Because it is not useful; it does not lead to the end of suffering. And what have I taught you? This is suffering; this is its origin; this is its ceasing; this is the way. A handful of leaves, sir. That is what he said his own teaching was, next to what could be known: a handful, chosen for use. An introduction to it, by the same logic, is a handful of the handful, and I would rather call it so than pretend to hand you the forest.

One book, if you want the story of the man told at length and with grace, and you want it this month rather than this year: Karen Armstrong's short biography, simply called Buddha, which keeps the legend and the history in view at once and can be read in two evenings. And standing behind the whole course, for whenever you want the scholar's view of any of it, Rupert Gethin's The Foundations of Buddhism, which I shall not recommend again because I am recommending it now, permanently.

Two accounts remain open on the page tonight, and I shall carry them forward. The boy named Fetter has not seen the last of his father: the man did go home to Kapilavastu, years later, and what he taught his son, who was seven by then, with a dipper of water and a mirror, is where Episode Five begins. And the line with which I am about to close, and shall close every evening, is not mine. It is borrowed. Whose it is, and when he said it, I shall tell you on the evening we reach his last day.

Next week, sir: the Deer Park, and what was actually said there. The four truths, taken one at a time and then all together; the word that gets translated suffering and what it really means; why the man ate breakfast and called it a doctrine; and what one of the five understood. Bring the young master. It concerns him.

Strive on with diligence, sir.

— End of Episode One.
