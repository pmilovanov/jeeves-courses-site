Episode Four — The Wheel

A Handful of Leaves — An Introduction to Buddhism

Do Not Say So, Ananda

Good day, sir.

There is a scene in the scriptures that I have always found reassuring, because it is the sound of a very intelligent man being told that he has understood too quickly. The man is Ananda — the Buddha's cousin, his attendant for the last twenty-five years of his life, the man with the famous memory who would one day recite the whole of the teaching from the beginning — and he comes to the Buddha in a good mood. It is wonderful, venerable sir, he says, it is marvellous, how deep this dependent origination is, and how deep it appears. And yet to me it seems as clear as clear can be. And the Buddha says: Do not say so, Ananda. Do not say so. This dependent origination is deep, and it appears deep. It is through not understanding this teaching, through not penetrating it, that this generation has become like a tangled skein, like a knotted ball of thread, like matted reeds and rushes, and cannot pass beyond the wheel of birth and death.

I offer that, sir, as tonight's warning label. We are about to do the thing Ananda thought was clear, and I shall try to make it clear, and I want you to hold in the back of your mind that the man who taught it said it was not. It is the most systematic thing in the teaching — the evening, of the ten, that a man in your trade will find most familiar in shape, because it is about causation, and chains of dependency, and where in a chain one may insert a hand — and its very systematicness is the trap. One can learn the twelve links the way one learns a bus route and understand nothing. So I shall walk them, twice, and then I shall show you what the walk is for.

Let me set the furniture, since you may be arriving with the young master and without last week. Last week took a person to pieces: five heaps — the body, the feeling-tone of each moment, the recognising, the compiled habits, the bare awareness — and found no owner among them; and when the Greek king asked what, then, continues, the monk gave him a lamp burning through the night, each moment's flame lighting the next, neither the same nor another. That is the picture: not a soul that persists, not nothing, but a process in which each state causes the next. The Buddha gave the process a name, dependent origination, and tonight is that name, and its two great consequences, which are the two words everyone associates with the religion whether they know anything else about it or not: karma, and rebirth. About the second I promised you an honest conversation, and I have kept a place for it in Part Four.

One image before we begin, because the tradition drew one, and it is the best syllabus for tonight I could devise. In every Tibetan monastery, painted by the door, there is a wheel. It is held in the claws and teeth of a monster — Yama, the lord of death — and it has three rings. At the hub, three animals chase each other in a circle, each biting the tail of the next: a cock, a snake, and a pig. They are greed, hatred, and delusion — the three fires of the Fire Sermon, you will remember, the tradition's short diagnosis of everything that goes wrong in a mind. Around the hub, the wheel is divided into six segments, like a pie: six realms into which a being may be born, from the palaces of the gods at the top to the hells at the bottom. And around the rim, twelve small pictures: a blind man with a stick; a potter at his wheel; a monkey; a boat; an empty house with six windows; a couple embracing; a man with an arrow in his eye; a drinker; a monkey picking fruit; a pregnant woman; a birth; a corpse being carried away. Twelve pictures, twelve links. And outside the wheel, in the corner, standing free of the whole apparatus and pointing at the moon, the Buddha. That wheel is tonight, sir. The hub you know. The rim is Part Two. The pie is Part Four. And the point of the exercise is the man in the corner.

Part One — This Being, That Is

This part is about the principle before the list, and the principle is four lines long.

The Buddha stated the general law of dependent origination in a formula that appears dozens of times in the scriptures, and I shall give it to you exactly, because it is short, and then explain it, because it is not simple. When this is, that is. From the arising of this, that arises. When this is not, that is not. From the ceasing of this, that ceases. Four lines. Say them to yourself once, sir, if the young master permits: when this is, that is; when this is not, that is not.

What does it claim? It claims that things arise in dependence on conditions — that nothing in experience is self-standing, self-caused, or permanent, and that everything you can point to is there because something else is there, and will go when that goes. It is a claim about the texture of the world: not a set of things but a web of conditions, in which every node is held up by its neighbours and holds them up in turn. And it has two edges, and both cut.

The first edge cuts against permanence. If everything is conditioned, nothing is eternal: not the gods, not the soul, not the mountain, not the self you found missing last week. Whatever arose from conditions will cease when the conditions change; that is Kondanna's sentence from the Deer Park — whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing — and it is the same law seen from the far end.

The second edge cuts against nothingness, and this is the edge people forget. If everything arises from conditions, then things do arise: there is a world, there are consequences, actions have results, the present is caused by the past and causes the future. Dependent origination is not the doctrine that nothing is real; it is the doctrine that everything real is contingent. The Buddha put it, to a monk named Kaccana, as a middle way between two errors: "Everything exists" is one extreme; "nothing exists" is the other extreme. Avoiding both, the Tathagata teaches by the middle: when this is, that is. You will recognise the two ditches from last week, the eternal self on one side and the annihilated self on the other. This is the road between them, and it is not a compromise; it is a different kind of answer. Not what is there? but how does what is there come to be there, and how does it go?

I should say, since you are an engineer and will wonder, what this principle is not. It is not a cosmology. The Buddha declined, as we saw with the poisoned arrow, to say how the world began; and dependent origination has no first link, no uncaused cause, no creator setting the wheel turning. When asked whether suffering was made by oneself, or by another, or by both, or by neither and by chance, he refused all four and said: it arises dependently. The chain does not start; it circles. That is why the picture is a wheel and not a ladder. And it is why the tradition's word for the whole business of birth and death is samsara, which means wandering on, going round — the same twelve links revolving, without beginning, until something breaks the circuit.

And the scriptures record a remark, attributed to the Buddha by his chief disciple Sariputta, which I offer as the measure of how central this is: One who sees dependent origination sees the teaching; and one who sees the teaching sees dependent origination. The four truths, the five heaps, the three marks — all of them, on this view, are the one principle worn in different clothes. Dukkha arises from craving: that is dependent origination. The five heaps are impermanent and not-self: that is dependent origination. The physician's whole case rests on it, because a disease that arises from conditions is a disease that can be stopped by removing them, and that is the third truth.

If you lost the thread, sir, Part One in a sentence: everything arises from conditions and ceases with them — when this is, that is; when this is not, that is not — which rules out both the eternal and the nothing, and has no first cause and no end, which is why it is drawn as a wheel.

Part Two — The Twelve Links, Walked

This part is the rim of the wheel, and it is the densest ten minutes of the course. I shall announce the cast before anything moves, walk the chain in four stages of three, walk it back, and then say what the walk was for.

The cast, briskly, with one word each, so that the names are in the room before we use them. One, ignorance. Two, formations. Three, consciousness. Four, name-and-form. Five, the six sense bases. Six, contact. Seven, feeling. Eight, craving. Nine, clinging. Ten, becoming. Eleven, birth. Twelve, ageing-and-death. Twelve links, and the formula joins each to the next: with ignorance as condition, formations; with formations as condition, consciousness; and so on to the end, where the text says: thus is the origin of this whole mass of dukkha.

Now slowly, three at a time, and I shall tell you at each stage what the pictures on the wheel show, because the painters were teachers too.

The first three: ignorance, formations, consciousness. The chain begins with ignorance — avijja, not-seeing — and the picture is a blind man walking with a stick. Ignorance here is not stupidity; it is a specific not-seeing: not seeing the four truths, not seeing the three marks, taking the impermanent for lasting and the not-self for a self. It is the pig at the hub, delusion, given its place at the head of the chain. With ignorance as condition arise formations — sankhara again, the compiled tendencies of the fourth heap — and the picture is a potter at his wheel, shaping pots: for the formations are the shaping activity, the intentions and habits that, acting in the dark, make the next thing. And with formations as condition arises consciousness — the bare knowing, the fifth heap — and the picture is a monkey leaping from branch to branch, never still, never holding one thing. Ignorance shapes habit; habit shapes what the mind lights on. That is the first stage: the dark stage, where the machinery runs before anything has happened yet.

The second three: name-and-form, the six sense bases, contact. With consciousness as condition arises name-and-form — nama-rupa, mind-and-body, the whole psychophysical organism; the picture is a boat with passengers, the body carrying the mind down the river. With name-and-form as condition arise the six sense bases — eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; the picture is an empty house with six windows, and I think it is the best of the twelve: a dwelling with six openings through which the world comes in. And with the six bases as condition arises contact — phassa, the meeting of a sense, its object, and the consciousness of it; the picture is a couple embracing. Sense meets world: that is the second stage. The equipment stage: a being with a body and six doors, and the world arriving at the doors.

The third three: feeling, craving, clinging. With contact as condition arises feeling — vedana, the second heap, the tone of pleasant, unpleasant, or neither; and the picture is a man with an arrow in his eye, because feeling is immediate, it arrives before thought, it is the one thing in the chain you cannot prevent. With feeling as condition arises craving — tanha, the thirst, the second noble truth in its exact place; the picture is a man drinking, and you will recall from the Diagnosis that the drinking does not slake it. And with craving as condition arises clinging — upadana, the word means the fuel a fire takes hold of; craving tightened into a grip; the picture is a monkey gathering fruit and holding it. Feeling, thirst, grip: that is the third stage. The response stage. And I want you to notice, and I shall say it again in Part Five, that this is the only stage where anything is up to you.

The last three: becoming, birth, ageing-and-death. With clinging as condition arises becoming — bhava, the momentum toward a new state of existence, the thirst-to-be of the Diagnosis gathering itself into a next life or a next moment; the picture is a pregnant woman. With becoming as condition arises birth — and the picture is a birth. And with birth as condition arise ageing and death, jara-marana, and with them, the text says, sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief, and despair; the picture is a corpse being carried to the burning-ground. Thus is the origin of this whole mass of dukkha. And the wheel turns, and the corpse's last moment of clinging becomes the next round's ignorance, and the blind man sets out again with his stick.

Let me say the twelve once more, in threes, without the pictures, so that the shape is in your ear. Ignorance, formations, consciousness — the machinery in the dark. Name-and-form, the six bases, contact — the equipment, and the world arriving. Feeling, craving, clinging — the response. Becoming, birth, ageing-and-death — the consequence. Four stages: the dark, the equipment, the response, the consequence.

Now the walk back, because the formula has a second half, and the second half is the physician's. With the remainderless fading away and ceasing of ignorance, formations cease; with the ceasing of formations, consciousness ceases; and so on down the whole chain, to with the ceasing of birth, ageing and death cease, and sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief, and despair. Thus is the ceasing of this whole mass of dukkha. Every link is a condition for the next; therefore every link is a place where the next can be stopped. The chain is not a sentence; it is a circuit, and a circuit can be broken. That is the third truth again, worn as a diagram.

And now I owe you the honest complication, because the tradition has argued for fifteen hundred years about what the twelve links are a diagram of, and I promised to tell you when things are contested. The classical reading — the one the great commentator Buddhaghosa set down in the fifth century, and the one the Tibetan wheel paints — is that the chain spans three lives. Ignorance and formations belong to a past life: the not-seeing and the habits that caused you to be born. Consciousness through becoming, links three to ten, are this life: the body you got, the senses, the contacts, the feelings, and the craving and clinging by which you are even now building the next. And birth and ageing-and-death, links eleven and twelve, are the next life, which your present clinging is arranging. On this reading the chain is a diagram of rebirth, and the Tibetan monastery door is a warning.

The other reading — and it has serious defenders, among them the great Thai teacher Buddhadasa in the last century, and an English monk in Ceylon named Nanavira who wrote a famous set of notes on the question in the nineteen-sixties — is that the chain describes what happens in one life, or indeed in one moment: that birth here means the birth of a self — the arising, in the wake of clinging, of the sense I am this, I am the one who wants this — and ageing-and-death is the inevitable collapse of that momentary self when its object changes; so that the wheel turns not once per lifetime but many times a minute, every time a feeling becomes a craving becomes a grip becomes a me. On this reading the chain is a diagram of how the self is manufactured, and you can watch it turn in real time.

I shall not adjudicate, sir; the tradition has not, and the two readings are not, in practice, enemies: the three-lives reading says the wheel turns across lifetimes, the one-life reading says it also turns across breakfasts, and both agree entirely on the place to cut it. Which is Part Five. First, the two consequences.

Part Two in a sentence: twelve links in four stages — the dark, the equipment, the response, the consequence — each conditioning the next, so that the chain circles; and read either as three lifetimes or as one moment, it has the same one place where it can be broken.

Part Three — Karma, Which Is Intention

This part is about the word that the world has borrowed from Buddhism and got most wrong, and the sentence in which the Buddha redefined it.

The word is karma — kamma in the older language — and it means, simply, action. In the India of the fifth century before the common era it already meant more than that: it meant action with consequences, the doctrine that what you do returns to you, in this life or another, as a kind of moral bookkeeping. The Jains, whose founder Mahavira was the Buddha's contemporary, held the most literal version of it: karma for them was a fine substance, a dust, that adhered to the soul with every act — every act, the intended and the unintended alike, the stepping on an insect as much as the striking of a man — and weighed it down into the cycle of birth, and could be removed only by burning it off through austerity. I mention this, sir, because it explains the six years. The starving man of Episode One, with his spine like a string of beads, was following a theory: that the dust of past deeds could be scorched off a soul by suffering. And when he abandoned the theory, he had to say what karma was instead.

He said it in one sentence, which I regard as among the most consequential he spoke. It is intention, monks, that I call karma. Having intended, one acts by body, by speech, or by mind. Intention — cetana — the choosing. Not the deed's physical shape, not its consequences in the world, not the dust: the movement of the mind that produced it. A modern scholar, Gombrich, calls this the ethicising of karma, and the word is right: the Buddha took a doctrine about ritual and substance and made it a doctrine about the mind. The moral weight of an act lives in the state of mind that did it. Step on an ant unseeing, and there is no karma; crush it in a temper, and there is, and the karma is the temper, and it is you who are shaped by it, not the ant.

Notice what that does to the fourth heap. Each intention, on this account, is not only an act but a deposit: it lays down a formation, a groove, a tendency to intend the same way next time. That is the mechanism by which karma works, in the Buddha's own psychology, and it is not mystical in the least. Act in anger, and you become slightly more a person who acts in anger; the groove deepens; the next provocation runs down it faster. Act with patience, and the same, the other way. The tradition's word for the result of an act is its fruit, and the fruit of an intention is, first and mostly, the person you become by having it. You will know the sentence that is always attributed to Aristotle — we are what we repeatedly do; excellence, then, is not an act but a habit — and you will perhaps not know that Aristotle never wrote it; it was the historian Will Durant, in the nineteen-twenties, summarising him. But the sentence is true, and the Buddha said it first, with a mechanism attached. Karma, at its plainest, is compound interest on intention.

Now the three things karma is not, because the Buddha said all three, and the tradition later forgot at least one of them.

Karma is not fate. There were teachers on the road who taught that everything a person experiences is the result of past deeds — the Jains, in effect — and the Buddha rejected it flatly. A man named Sivaka put the view to him, and he answered with a list I want you to hear, because it is so wonderfully medical: some feelings, he said, arise from bile; some from phlegm; some from wind; some from a combination of the three; some from a change in the weather; some from careless behaviour; some from assault; and some, some, from the ripening of karma. Eight causes, and karma is one of the eight. Those who say that everything a person feels is caused by what was done in the past, he said, go beyond what they themselves know, and beyond what is agreed to be true in the world. Bile, phlegm, wind, weather, carelessness, assault — and karma. I would have you remember that list, sir, the next time anyone, Buddhist or otherwise, tells a sick person they must have deserved it. The founder of the religion called that view going beyond what you know.

Karma is not no cause, either. The opposite error had a teacher too — Makkhali Gosala, one of the six rival teachers of the day, who held that there was no cause and no condition for anything, that beings were purified or defiled by fate alone, and that effort was pointless, since everyone would arrive at the end of their wanderings in due course like a ball of thread unrolling. The Buddha, who was courteous to almost everyone, was not courteous to this. He said that as the hair blanket is the meanest of all woven garments, so Makkhali's doctrine was the meanest of all doctrines; and he said, in a passage I find almost shocking in its heat, that he knew of no other person whose teaching had done so much harm to so many. Why the heat? Because fatalism removes the one thing the whole physician's case depends on: that the grip is yours to loosen. A doctrine that says nothing you do matters is, to a physician, the one doctrine that kills the patient for certain.

And karma is not mechanical. The Buddha told a parable, to a group of monks, which I want to give you in full, because it corrects the most common Western picture of karma — the account book, the cosmic accountant totting up debits. Suppose, he said, a man puts a lump of salt into a small cup of water. Would the water be undrinkable? Yes. And suppose he puts the same lump of salt into the river Ganges. Would the river be undrinkable? No; the great mass of water would take it up. Just so: a trifling bad deed done by one person leads him to a hell; and the same trifling deed, done by another person, is experienced here and now, and barely at all. And who is the second person? One who has developed the body, developed virtue, developed the mind, developed wisdom — one who is not confined, whose heart is vast, who dwells without measure. The same deed; the cup or the river. Karma, on the Buddha's own account, is not a fixed price paid for a fixed act. It is a dose, and the dose depends on the size of the vessel. Which means — and this is the whole practical consequence — that the response to having done wrong is not to pay, but to grow.

I said the tradition forgot one of these, and it was the first. In the Buddhist countries, over the centuries, karma slid back toward what it had been before the Buddha ethicised it: a cosmic explanation for everything, the reason the poor are poor and the sick are sick and the neighbour's house burned down. That is the folk religion, and it is real, and it is not what the man said. What he said is that intention is karma; that its fruit is chiefly the person you become; that it is one cause among eight; that fatalism is the meanest of doctrines; and that the same salt is a poison in a cup and nothing in a river.

Part Three in a sentence: karma is intention, not deed and not dust; it works by laying down habit, compounding; it is one cause among many, never fate; and its weight depends on the size of the vessel it falls into, which is the one thing you can enlarge.

Part Four — Rebirth, Honestly

This part is the conversation I promised, and I shall have it in three movements: what the tradition believes, what it disagreed with itself about, and what a reasonable man in Toronto may do with it.

What the tradition believes, first, and I shall state it without hedging, because you should hear it as its own people hear it. Beings are born and die and are born again, without a beginning that can be found — inconceivable, monks, is the beginning of this wandering-on. The Buddha had a way of making the scale felt: the tears you have shed, he told his monks, over the death of mothers and fathers and children in your lives past, are more than the water in the four great oceans; the bones you have left behind, could they be gathered, would stand as a mountain. This wandering is samsara, and there are six realms in which one may land — five in the oldest lists; the sixth was added later — and I shall name them from the top of the wheel down: the gods, in their long, pleasant, and impermanent heavens; the titans, the jealous gods, forever at war with the gods above them; the humans; the animals; the hungry ghosts, with vast bellies and needle throats, who can never eat enough; and the hells, of which the tradition has an appalling catalogue, and which, I would have you note, are not eternal: a being in hell is there for a very long time and then is not. No one is damned. Everyone, including the gods, moves on. And the human realm, in the middle, is the best place to be born — not the pleasantest; the best — because there is enough suffering in it to notice the problem and not so much as to be crushed by it. The gods are too comfortable to practise; the animals too frightened; the hungry ghosts too hungry. A human birth, the tradition says, is as rare as a blind sea-turtle surfacing once a century and putting its head by chance through a single yoke floating on the whole ocean; and one should not waste it.

Now, what carries over? Here is where last week pays out. Not a soul; the Buddha was explicit, and rebuked a monk sharply for teaching that consciousness was the thing that travelled. What continues is the process: the flame lighting the next flame; the last clinging of one life conditioning the first consciousness of the next, as link ten conditions link eleven on the wheel. Neither the same nor another. Rebirth, in the Buddhist account, is not reincarnation — there is no carnate thing, no passenger, to re-enter a body. It is continuation of consequence. And it is here that the schools disagreed, and I owe you the disagreement. The tradition of the elders, Theravada, holds that it is immediate: the last moment of one life conditions the first of the next with no gap, as one flame lights another the instant they touch. The northern schools held that there is an interval — an intermediate state between death and the next birth, lasting up to forty-nine days, in which the process, unmoored from a body, wanders and is drawn by its habits toward its next form. The Tibetans built an entire literature on that interval, and called it the bardo, and wrote a famous guidebook for the traveller in it, and that book and that interval are Episode Nine's business; I issue the debt now.

And the founder's own claim? You heard it in Episode One: in the first watch of the night under the fig tree, he says, he remembered his own past lives — one, two, a hundred, a hundred thousand — and in the second watch he saw others passing away and reappearing according to their deeds. He did not present rebirth as a philosophy he had adopted; he presented it as something he remembered. The tradition takes him at his word. And a psychiatrist at the University of Virginia named Ian Stevenson spent forty years, from the nineteen-sixties, collecting cases of small children who claimed to remember previous lives, and published thousands of them; the tradition cites him gratefully; and the sceptics reply that the cases cluster in cultures that expect them, and pass through informants who want them, and that a claim this large wants evidence of a different kind. I record both and press neither.

So: the third movement. What do you do with it, sir, a reasonable man in the former colonies who has just been told that the young master is considerably older than he looks?

I see three honest positions, and I shall give you each with its best advocate. The first is to believe it, as the tradition does, and the best advocate is the scholar-monk Bhikkhu Bodhi, who argues — and he is right about the structure — that a Buddhism without rebirth is not the same teaching with a part removed but a different teaching: the goal changes from escaping the wheel to being calmer on it; the point of karma changes; the arahant, the one who has finished, becomes merely a very well-adjusted person. Take the rebirth out, he says, and the building stands, but it is a different building. The second position is to bracket it: to say, honestly, I do not know, and to practise the parts of the treatment that do not depend on the answer, which is nearly all of them. Its best advocate is the former monk Stephen Batchelor, who has written a whole shelf on what he calls a Buddhism without beliefs, and whose reply to Bodhi is, roughly, that the Buddha himself declined to pull the arrow out until the metaphysics was settled, and neither will he. The third is to read it inward: to take the one-life reading of the wheel from Part Two seriously, and to say that rebirth is what happens every time a craving manufactures a self and the self collapses — that you have been reborn some thousands of times since breakfast, and that ending that rebirth is a project one can begin this afternoon. Its advocate is Buddhadasa, and a good many teachers since.

My own view, since the persona under which I serve you requires me to have one: I incline to the second, and use the third, and keep the first with respect and without mockery, because the people who hold it built everything I am describing and I am a guest in their house. And I have, for my leaning, the Buddha's own leave, which is the last thing in this part and the loveliest.

There was a village called Kesaputta, of a people called the Kalamas, and the Kalamas were perplexed. Teacher after teacher had come through, each expounding his own doctrine and rubbishing the others', and the villagers went to the Buddha and said, in effect: they cannot all be right; how are we to know? And the Buddha's answer has become the most quoted passage in the scriptures in the modern West, so I shall give it carefully, in both its halves. The first half: Do not go by oral tradition, nor by lineage, nor by hearsay, nor by a collection of scriptures, nor by logic, nor by inference, nor by reasoned reflection, nor by the acceptance of a view, nor by a speaker's apparent competence, nor because you think "the ascetic is our teacher." When you know for yourselves: these things are unwholesome, these things lead to harm and suffering — then abandon them. And when you know for yourselves: these things are wholesome, these lead to welfare and happiness — then live by them. That is the charter of the perplexed, sir, and it is genuinely there, and the founder of a religion said it, and I do not know of another who did.

And then the second half, which the modern West leaves out, and which is the part I want. Having taught the Kalamas that greed, hatred, and delusion lead to harm, and their opposites to welfare, he tells them what a person who has set the three fires down may say to himself, and he calls them the four assurances. If there is another world, and if good and bad deeds bear fruit, then after death I shall arise in a good place: that is the first assurance. If there is no other world, and deeds bear no fruit, then still, in this very life, I keep myself happy, free of hatred and ill will: that is the second. If evil befalls the evil-doer, then I, who intend no evil, will not be touched by it: the third. And if no evil befalls the evil-doer, then I see myself pure either way: the fourth. Whether or not there is a next world; whether or not an account is kept. The Buddha's own wager, made to a village of sceptics, is that a mind without the three fires is well off in every case — and so the question of rebirth, though he believed in it and remembered it, was not the question he asked the Kalamas to settle before they began. I should add, because the scholars ask me to: the Kalamas were not being told to believe nothing; they were being told how to weigh teachers, and the discourse ends with them taking the Buddha as theirs. It is a charter for checking, not a charter for shrugging. But it is a charter for checking, and it means that a man who brackets the sixth realm and practises the second assurance is not a bad Buddhist. He is, on the Buddha's own arithmetic, well off in every case.

Part Four in a sentence: the tradition believes in a beginningless wandering through six impermanent realms, with a process and not a soul continuing; the schools disagreed about the interval between lives; a modern listener may believe it, bracket it, or read it inward; and the Buddha told a village of sceptics that a mind without the three fires is well off whether there is a next world or not.

Part Five — Where the Knife Goes

The last part is short, and it is the payoff of the walk, and I want you to carry it off the water tonight above everything else.

Look at the twelve links once more, and ask the engineer's question: where is the intervention point? Not the first link — ignorance is the whole problem, and one cannot simply decide to see. Not the equipment — you have a body and six doors and the world arrives at them whether you like it or not. Not feeling, link seven — the arrow is in the eye before you can act; feeling is immediate, and the Buddha never asked anyone to stop feeling. Not birth or death. There is exactly one place in the chain where the next link is not automatic, and it is the gap between link seven and link eight: between feeling and craving. The pleasant tone arrives, and then the thirst — and the tradition's entire claim is that the and then is not a law. A feeling need not become a craving. There is a moment, ordinarily unnoticed, ordinarily a fraction of a second wide, between the tone and the grip, and everything the treatment does — everything Episode Five will describe — is a way of widening that moment until one can act inside it.

Let me put it in the nursery, since we are there. The young master, at eleven weeks, runs the chain from contact to feeling to craving to clinging with no gap at all: the wet cloth is unpleasant and he is already at full voice; the milk is pleasant and he is already gripping. That is not a fault; it is what a new mind is. And the whole of what you and Mrs Milovanov will do for him over the next twenty years — the whole of what is meant by growing up — is, on this account, the gradual widening of the gap between the feeling and the grip: the two seconds in which a three-year-old decides not to hit her brother; the ten seconds in which a man decides not to send the email. The Buddha's treatment is a further widening of the same gap, by deliberate training, past the point where ordinary maturity stops. That is all mindfulness is, when it is finally defined: the faculty that notices the feeling before the craving has fired. Next week, sir.

A professional note, since the observation is available. A gentleman's gentleman stands beside a good many second helpings. One learns to see the exact instant — it is visible, if one knows where to look; something in the hand — at which the pleasant tone of the first helping becomes the reaching for the second; and one learns, on the better evenings, to be already moving the dish. The whole of the Buddhist path is a method for becoming one's own valet in that instant.

The Raft, So Far

The planks lashed on tonight, sir, said once more.

First: the principle — when this is, that is; when this is not, that is not — everything arising from conditions and ceasing with them, which rules out both the eternal and the nothing, and has no first cause, which is why it is a wheel and not a ladder. Second: the twelve links in four stages — the dark, the equipment, the response, the consequence — each conditioning the next, so the chain circles; readable as three lifetimes or as one moment, and either way breakable. Third: karma is intention, not deed and not dust; it works by laying down habit and compounds; it is one cause among eight and never fate; and its weight depends on the vessel it falls into, which is the one thing you can enlarge. Fourth: rebirth is the tradition's belief, held as a process and not a soul, disputed between the schools as to the interval, and left to you as a thing to believe, to bracket, or to read inward — with the Buddha's own assurance, to a village of sceptics, that a mind without the three fires is well off in every case. Fifth, and above the rest: the one place in the chain where the next link is not automatic is between feeling and craving, and the whole treatment is the widening of that gap.

The one book tonight, for a listener who wants the Buddha's own words on all of this arranged by a scholar who knows where every sentence lives: Bhikkhu Bodhi's anthology In the Buddha's Words, which sets the discourses on dependent origination, karma, and rebirth in their own chapter with his introductions, and which will let you check tonight against the source, which is the deal.

Two accounts I carry forward. The interval between lives, the bardo, is payable when we climb to Tibet in Episode Nine. And the widening of the gap — the treatment itself — is next week entire.

Next week, then, sir: a father comes home to Kapilavastu after seven years, and his son, whom he named Fetter on the night he left, comes to him with a request from the boy's mother; and what the father teaches the boy, with a dipper of water and a mirror, is the beginning of the eightfold path, which we shall walk whole — ethics first and mostly; then, once and concretely, the practice itself, which you may try at the cradle and had better not try on the road.

Strive on with diligence, sir.

— End of Episode Four.
