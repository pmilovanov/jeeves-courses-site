# Episode Nine -- The Roof of the World

*A Handful of Leaves -- An Introduction to Buddhism*

## A Temple on the Neva

Good day, sir.

In the year nineteen hundred and nine, on the northern edge of Saint Petersburg, where the city ran out into the islands and the summer villas, a Buryat lama named Agvan Dorzhiev obtained the Tsar's permission to build a Tibetan Buddhist temple, and began to build it. I want to spend a few minutes on that man, because he is the hinge between tonight's two halves -- the roof of the world, and the country north of it that sir knows from the inside -- and because nobody in this course has had a stranger career.

Dorzhiev was born in the eighteen-fifties in a Buryat village east of Lake Baikal, a subject of the Russian empire and a Buddhist of the Tibetan school; went to Lhasa as a young man; and rose, by sheer scholarship, through the great monastic university of Drepung to become one of the tutors, and then the closest confidant, of the thirteenth Dalai Lama. He was, that is, a Russian subject at the elbow of the ruler of Tibet, at the height of the Great Game -- the century-long shadow-war between Britain and Russia for the approaches to India -- and when, at the turn of the century, he made two journeys from Lhasa to the Tsar's court, carrying letters, the British viceroy in Calcutta concluded that Russia was about to take Tibet. So Britain took it first. In nineteen hundred and three an expedition under Colonel Younghusband marched over the passes with machine guns, killed some hundreds of Tibetan militiamen armed with matchlocks at a place called Guru, and occupied Lhasa; the Dalai Lama fled north to Mongolia, with Dorzhiev; and the treaty the British extracted was, in substance, a promise that Tibet would have nothing to do with Russia. That is what a Buryat monk's diplomacy cost. And six years later, in the Russian capital, the same monk was laying the foundation of a temple.

It went up between nineteen hundred and nine and nineteen fifteen, in granite, in the Tibetan style, with a building committee that included the greatest Buddhist scholars of the Russian academy -- of whom more in Part Five -- and stained-glass windows designed by the painter Nicholas Roerich, and a running commentary from the Orthodox press about a pagan shrine in the capital of Holy Russia, and the attentions of the police. The first service was held in February nineteen thirteen, for the three hundredth anniversary of the Romanov dynasty, with the Tsar's blessing; the consecration in August nineteen fifteen. It is the northernmost Buddhist temple in the world, and it is still there, at the top of Primorsky Prospekt, and it has had, since, the history that everything in that city has had: looted in the Revolution; the lamas driven out; Dorzhiev himself arrested in the purges of nineteen thirty-seven, at eighty-four, and dead in a prison hospital in Ulan-Ude within the year; the building used as a radio station, and then, for decades, as a laboratory of the zoological institute; and returned, in nineteen ninety, to the Buddhists, who hold services in it now. I begin there, sir, rather than in Lhasa, because it is the end of tonight's road, and because a course on Buddhism that never mentioned that there are Buddhists in Europe -- old ones, native ones, with their own martyrs -- would be a course written by someone who had not looked at the map.

Tonight is the fourth border, and the highest. The teaching crossed the Himalaya into Tibet in the seventh and eighth centuries, and what Tibet did with it is the most *thorough* thing any people ever did with it: they translated the entire library, systematically, and then they built on top of it the most elaborate religious civilisation in Asia -- a theocracy ruled by a monk who was held to be a god's incarnation, discovered as a small child by tests -- and then, when the roof fell in, they carried it out again, west, to us. One idea tonight, and the tradition's own name for it: the **diamond vehicle**. Five parts: the conversion and the debate that chose India over China; the diamond vehicle and what it claims; the four schools and the reborn lama; the book of the dead, which pays a debt from the fourth evening; and the road north, to Mongolia, to the Buryats and Kalmyks and Tuvans, to Petersburg, and to the year nineteen fifty-nine.

## Part One -- The Conversion, and the Debate

This part is about how Tibet became Buddhist, and the argument, at a monastery called Samye, that decided what kind.

Tibet in the seventh century was an empire -- a warlike one, that took Chinese cities and frightened the Tang -- ruled by a king named Songtsen Gampo, who is remembered as the first Buddhist king, and whose two foreign wives, a Chinese princess and a Nepalese one, are remembered as having each brought a Buddha image to Lhasa, for which he built the temple that is still the holiest in the country. The historians think the wives are mostly legend and the king's Buddhism mostly diplomacy; what is certain is that he had a *script* made for Tibetan, on an Indian model, and that without the script nothing that follows could have happened.

The real conversion came a century later, under a king named Trisong Detsen, and the tradition tells it as a contest with demons. The king invited the greatest scholar of Nalanda, Shantarakshita, to found a monastery; and the local gods -- the spirits of the mountains and lakes and passes, whom the Tibetans had worshipped for ever -- objected, and sent floods and lightning, and the walls fell down as fast as they were built. So the scholar advised the king to send for a different kind of man: a tantric master from the northwest of India named **Padmasambhava** -- *the Lotus-Born*; the Tibetans call him simply *Guru Rinpoche*, the precious teacher -- who came over the passes and *subdued* the gods one by one, not by destroying them but by binding them with oaths to *protect* the teaching. And the walls stood, and the monastery of Samye was finished around the year seven hundred and seventy-nine, and the first seven Tibetans were ordained. I want you to notice the pattern, sir, because it is the Tibetan pattern from first to last: *nothing is destroyed; everything is bound and put to work.* The old gods became the guardians of the monastery gates, and they are there yet, painted in the porches, glaring.

And then the debate. Samye had two parties of foreign teachers: Indians, in the line of Shantarakshita, teaching the Great Vehicle as Nalanda taught it -- the perfections, the stages, the long road; and Chinese, from Dunhuang, of the school you met last week, Chan, teaching that the road was an illusion and awakening was sudden. The king, who wanted one religion and not two, ordered a debate, which is said to have run from seven hundred and ninety-two to seven hundred and ninety-four. For India, Shantarakshita's student Kamalashila, a scholar; for China, a master named Moheyan, whose position was that all thought, good or bad, obscures the mind as clouds, white or black, obscure the sun, and that the whole practice was to *stop thinking*. Kamalashila answered that a mind that has merely stopped is a mind asleep, and that awakening requires wisdom, which requires analysis, which requires the long road. And the king ruled for India, and the Chinese were sent home, and the tradition says Moheyan's partisans were so ashamed that some killed themselves. Now the honest footnote, which you will by now expect: a Chinese account of the same debate, found -- as everything is found -- in the Library Cave at Dunhuang, says that Moheyan *won*; and some historians doubt the debate as described took place at all. But whoever won the argument, India won Tibet. From that day Tibetan Buddhism has been *Indian*: scholastic, gradual, philosophical, built on Nagarjuna and the perfections and the monastic universities, with *Chan* a term of abuse in its literature -- so that the two great Buddhisms north and east of India, Tibet's and China's, are the two sides of the Samye debate, still not speaking.

Then the roof fell in for the first time. A king named Langdarma, in the eight-forties, persecuted the monasteries -- the tradition makes him a demon, and says a monk in a black hat and a black cloak rode into Lhasa, danced before the king, drew a bow from his sleeve, shot him dead, and rode away on a horse he had blackened with soot, which he washed white in a river as he fled; the black-hat dance is still performed -- and the empire collapsed, and Tibet fell into a century and a half the tradition calls the dark age, in which the monasteries were empty and the teaching survived only in the hands of laymen and hermits. The recovery -- the *second diffusion*, the Tibetans call it -- began around the year one thousand, in the far west, with a translator named Rinchen Zangpo who went to Kashmir for texts, and it culminated in the arrival, in ten forty-two, of the greatest Indian scholar of his age, **Atisha**, from the university of Vikramashila, whom a western Tibetan king had ransomed with gold. Atisha stayed twelve years and died in Tibet, and his little book, *A Lamp for the Path to Awakening*, laid out the whole teaching as a graded sequence of stages, which every Tibetan school has followed since.

And here is the thing the Tibetans did that no one else did, and I say it as an engineer would. They *translated everything*. Not a selection, as the Chinese had, and not the Pali of one school, as Sri Lanka had, but the whole of late Indian Buddhism -- the sutras, the tantras, Nagarjuna, the mind-only masters, the logicians, the commentaries -- into Tibetan, over some four centuries, with a rigour the Chinese never attempted: the king's translators compiled, in the early ninth century, an official dictionary fixing a single Tibetan equivalent for every Sanskrit technical term, and forbade deviation from it, so that a Tibetan translation of any Indian text can be turned back into its Sanskrit almost word for word. The Chinese, you will recall, had translated emptiness as the Daoist *nothing* and lived with the consequences; the Tibetans wrote a specification first. The result is a canon in two parts -- the **Kangyur**, the *translated word*, the Buddha's own discourses and tantras, in something over a hundred volumes; and the **Tengyur**, the *translated treatises*, the Indian masters, in something over two hundred -- and when the Muslim conquests and the Hindu revival wiped Buddhism from India, a great part of its literature survived *only* in this Tibetan copy. That is the debt from Episode Seven, paid: when the house fell, the neighbours had the books, and the neighbours had checked every term.

Part One in a sentence: Tibet was converted by a scholar and a sorcerer, who bound the old gods rather than breaking them; chose India over China in a debate whose loser's descendants say he won; survived a dark age; and then translated the whole Indian library against a fixed dictionary, so that what India lost, Tibet kept.

## Part Two -- The Diamond Vehicle

This part is about the kind of Buddhism Tibet took, which is the third and last of the vehicles, and the one that will sound least like the Deer Park.

Its name is **Vajrayana**. A *vajra* is a thunderbolt, or a diamond -- the Sanskrit means both -- the weapon of the god Indra and the hardest thing there is; and the *vajra vehicle* is the tantric Buddhism of late India, which grew up in the monasteries and the charnel grounds from about the seventh century, reached Japan with Kukai, as I told you last week, and Tibet with Padmasambhava, and died in India with everything else. It is not, as the two earlier vehicles were, a new philosophy; the Vajrayana's philosophy is the Great Vehicle's -- emptiness, Buddha-nature, the bodhisattva's vow. It is a new *method*, and the method's claim is about *speed*. The bodhisattva's road, as the sutras lay it out, takes three incalculable aeons of rebirths to walk. The tantras claim that with their methods a person may attain Buddhahood *in this very life*, or at most in a handful. The diamond vehicle is the Great Vehicle *accelerated*, and everything strange about it follows from what acceleration requires.

Let me give you the instruments, five of them, briskly and then slowly. Briskly: the guru; the empowerment; the mantra; the mandala; and the deity. Slowly, then.

The **guru** first, because it is the foundation and the danger. In the tantric path the teacher -- the *lama*, in Tibetan -- is not a guide but a *Buddha*: the practitioner is instructed to regard the lama as the Buddha in person, and the formula every Tibetan child learns runs *the lama is the Buddha, the lama is the dharma, the lama is the sangha.* This is how the fast road is made possible: one cannot accelerate without total trust in the driver. It is also, obviously, how the fast road is abused, and the last evening will have two paragraphs on that. The **empowerment** -- the initiation, the ritual by which a lama admits a student to a particular practice -- comes next, and it is why tantra is *secret*: the texts are not to be read, and the practices not to be attempted, without it; a Tibetan who has not received the empowerment for a deity will decline to discuss that deity's practice with you, and will not be being coy. The **mantra** -- the formula, the sound: *Om mani padme hum*, the six syllables of the bodhisattva of compassion, which are carved on every rock and turned in every prayer wheel in Tibet, and which the Tibetans say, aloud and under the breath, more often than any other words -- is the tantric instrument you have seen. The **mandala** is the palace of the deity: a diagram of the cosmos as a square palace with four gates inside a circle, drawn in coloured sand over days by monks with funnels, consecrated, and then -- this is the point -- *swept up and poured into a river*: the most laborious art in the world, made to be destroyed, as an object lesson in the first mark. And the **deity** -- here is the heart of the thing. In *deity yoga* the practitioner does not pray *to* the Buddha he is visualising. He visualises himself *as* the Buddha: generates, in imagination, the deity's body, with its colours and arms and ornaments, at the centre of its mandala; recites its mantra; takes on its speech and its mind; and then dissolves the whole vision into emptiness and rises from it as the deity again. The tradition's phrase for this is *taking the result as the path*: rather than practising for aeons to become a Buddha, one practises *being* one, now, in imagination, until the imagination and the fact converge. You will notice, sir, that this is a very sophisticated theory of what a mind can do to itself, resting on the mind-only school of Episode Seven; and that it is a long way from a man under a fig tree watching his breath.

And then the edge, which I shall not hide. The highest class of tantras -- there are four classes, graded, and the empowerments for the highest are given only after years -- works with the *body*: a subtle anatomy of channels and winds and drops, manipulated by breath and posture to produce, at the centre of the chest, the *clear light* that the tradition says appears naturally at death and can be induced in life. And its symbolism is drawn from the charnel ground and the bedchamber. The deities of the highest tantras are *wrathful* -- fanged, flaming, wearing garlands of skulls, trampling corpses -- and they are shown in *union*, the male deity embracing a female consort; the Tibetans call the image *father-mother*. The tantric texts speak of practices with a consort, and of substances no monk may touch, and of a *transgression* that is itself the method: the passions, which the Deer Park taught one to extinguish, are here *used* -- desire ridden, as one rides a horse, to the far side of desire; the poison taken as medicine. The great majority of Tibetan practitioners, I should say at once, take all of this *symbolically* -- the consort is visualised, the skull cup is a symbol, the wrath is compassion in its fierce form -- and the monastic schools required years of sutra study before a monk was let anywhere near it; but a minority in every century has taken it literally, and the tradition's literature of its own *mad saints* is frank about that. The eighty-four *mahasiddhas* -- the great adepts of tantric India: Tilopa who pounded sesame, Naropa who was set twelve trials by him, Saraha the arrow-maker who lived with a low-caste woman and sang -- are, in the tradition's own telling, men who broke every rule and got there first. I give you the edge honestly because the tradition does: the diamond vehicle is the part of Buddhism that most resembles the things the Buddha walked *out* of, and it knows it, and its answer is that a fast road through a mountain range is not a road for children.

Let me give you its saint, who is the best-loved man in Tibet, and then Part Two is done. **Milarepa** was born in the eleventh century, and his father died, and his uncle stole the inheritance and reduced his mother and sister to servants; and his mother sent him to learn black magic, and he learned it, and brought down the roof of the uncle's house during a wedding feast and killed thirty-five people, and then sent hail on the village's barley for good measure. And then he was afraid of what he had done, and went to find a teacher, and found **Marpa** -- a farmer, a translator, a man who had walked to India three times and come back with the tantras of Naropa -- and Marpa, seeing what he was, refused to teach him and set him to work. *Build me a tower.* Milarepa built it. *Wrong place; tear it down; build it there.* He built it. Nine storeys; torn down; rebuilt; torn down; the skin of his back worn off by the stones; and only when the sorcerer's pride was entirely gone did Marpa give him the teachings and send him to the caves. He lived in them for years, on nettles, until his skin turned green, in a cotton robe in the Himalayan winter -- his name means *the cotton-clad* -- and he *sang*: his hundred thousand songs are the poetry of the country, and the one every Tibetan can quote goes, roughly, *my temple is the mountain; my companions are the deer; my book is the sky.* That is what the diamond vehicle looks like when it works, sir: a murderer who became, by the only road that could have taken him, the country's saint. And the lineage that descends from Marpa and Milarepa is the second of tonight's four schools.

Part Two in a sentence: the diamond vehicle is the Great Vehicle accelerated -- Buddhahood in one life -- by the guru, the empowerment, the mantra, the mandala, and the practice of being the deity; its highest tantras use the body and the passions as the road, which the tradition takes mostly as symbol and admits is not for children; and its saint is a repentant sorcerer who sang in a cave.

## Part Three -- Four Schools, and a Reborn Lama

This part is the institutions, and the cast is four schools and one child, so hear the names once before they move: the Nyingma, the Kagyu, the Sakya, and the Gelug; and the tulku.

The **Nyingma** -- the *ancient ones* -- are the school of Padmasambhava, and their distinction is the *treasure text*: the claim that Guru Rinpoche, foreseeing the dark age, hid teachings in rocks and lakes and in the minds of his disciples, to be discovered, centuries later, by the right person at the right time. A great deal of the Nyingma canon is such discovered treasure, and the book of the dead in Part Four is one of them. Their highest teaching, the *Great Perfection*, holds that the mind is already, in its nature, awake, and that the whole path is recognising this -- which will remind you of the woodcutter's verse, and did remind the Gelug, who sniffed at it. The **Kagyu** -- the *oral lineage* -- descend from Marpa and Milarepa, and they are the school of the yogi and the cave; and they gave Tibet the institution that made it unlike any country on earth, of which in a moment. The **Sakya**, named for their monastery of the grey earth, are the school of one aristocratic family, and they are the school that first made a Tibetan monk the ruler of Tibet -- by way of the Mongols. In twelve forty-seven the head of the Sakya went to the court of a Mongol prince, Godan, a grandson of Genghis Khan, to negotiate Tibet's submission, and did it so well that the Mongols made the Sakya lamas their viceroys over the country; and his nephew, Phagpa, became the imperial preceptor of Kublai Khan himself, and designed a script for the Mongol empire, and established the arrangement that governed Tibet's foreign relations for seven centuries: the *priest and patron* -- the lama supplies the emperor with religion, the emperor supplies the lama with an army. You will recall that Huiyuan lost the argument about bowing to kings in China; in Tibet the lama did not bow. He *anointed*. And the **Gelug** -- the *virtuous ones*, the yellow hats -- are the reform. Their founder, **Tsongkhapa**, born in thirteen fifty-seven, was a scholar of prodigious range who thought the country had grown lax -- too much tantra too soon, not enough discipline, not enough logic -- and who founded, in fourteen hundred and nine, the monastery of Ganden, and a school built on strict monastic celibacy, on years of sutra study before any empowerment, and on *debate*: the Gelug monk earns his degree over a decade or more of formal disputation in the courtyard, in which a standing challenger hurls questions at a seated defender and claps his hands together on each one, and the sound of a Gelug monastery at study is the sound of a thousand men slapping their palms. The three great Gelug houses near Lhasa -- Ganden, Drepung, Sera -- held, before nineteen fifty-nine, some twenty thousand monks between them, and were the largest monasteries in the history of the world.

Now the child. Tibet did one thing that no other Buddhist country did, and it grew out of the not-self teaching of the third evening in a way that would have made the Greek king's monk blink. If a person is a process and not a soul, and if a bodhisattva has vowed to return until all beings are freed, then a great lama who dies may be expected to *return*, deliberately, in a new body -- and the new body can be *found*. The first lineage to do this was the head of the Kagyu, the Karmapa, in the thirteenth century; and from there the institution of the **tulku**, the *emanation body*, the recognised reincarnation, spread to every school, until Tibet had several thousand such lineages. The method is the part sir will enjoy. When a great lama dies, his attendants look for signs -- his last words, the direction his body faced, visions in a sacred lake -- and search the region for a child born at the right time; and the child is *tested*: the dead lama's possessions are set out among decoys -- his rosary among other rosaries, his drum, his cup -- and a child of two or three who reaches, without hesitation, for the right ones, and calls the searchers by name, and says *that is mine*, is the lama returned, and is taken to the monastery, and raised as its head. Master Ian, sir, will within the year be reaching for your keys among the decoys with exactly that unhesitating certainty, and I mention it only to say that the tradition built a theocracy on a gesture every parent has seen.

The greatest of the lineages is the one the world knows. In fifteen seventy-eight a Gelug abbot named Sonam Gyatso travelled to Mongolia to convert the Mongol ruler Altan Khan, and did, and the Khan gave him a title: *Dalai*, which is the Mongolian word for *ocean*, a translation of *Gyatso*, which is the Tibetan word for the same. So the *Dalai Lama* is *the Ocean Teacher*, and he is a Mongol title on a Tibetan monk, and Sonam Gyatso, being the third in his line of recognised rebirths, became the third Dalai Lama, and his two predecessors were promoted posthumously. The fourth was, not by coincidence, a great-grandson of Altan Khan: the only Mongol Dalai Lama, and the seal on an alliance. And the fifth -- the *Great Fifth*, born in sixteen seventeen -- made the office what it is. He called in a Mongol army under a prince named Gushri Khan, who conquered Tibet for him in sixteen forty-two and handed it over; and from that year until nineteen fifty-nine the Dalai Lama was the ruler of Tibet, priest and king in one -- the one Buddhist country ever governed by its own religion's head -- and the Great Fifth built, on a hill above Lhasa, the Potala, the thousand-roomed palace that is the most famous building in the country, and died in sixteen eighty-two, and his regent *concealed the death for fifteen years* to finish it, governing in the name of a dead man. The sixth, when he was finally produced, turned out to be a poet who preferred the taverns and the girls of Lhasa to the palace, and was deposed and died young on the road to China, and his love-songs are the second-best-loved verse in Tibet after Milarepa's. Of the thirteenth you heard at the start of the evening, with his Buryat tutor. The fourteenth was found in nineteen thirty-seven, a farmer's son of two, in a village in the far northeast, by a search party guided by a vision in a lake; he chose the rosary and the drum, and called the disguised abbot by his name, and was carried to Lhasa, and enthroned at four, and is, as I record this, ninety-one years old, in exile in India, of which more before the evening ends.

Part Three in a sentence: four schools -- the ancient ones with their hidden treasures, the oral lineage of the cave, the family school that anointed the Mongols, and the reforming scholars of the debate courtyard -- and one institution, the reincarnate lama found as a child by tests, which put a monk on the throne of Tibet for three centuries under a Mongol title meaning *ocean*.

## Part Four -- The Book of the Dead

This part pays the debt from the fourth evening -- the interval between lives -- and tells you how a Tibetan funeral manual became the most famous Buddhist book in the West.

You will recall that the schools disagreed about what happens between death and the next birth: the elders said nothing, no gap, one flame lighting the next; the northern schools said an *intermediate state*, the *bardo*, of up to forty-nine days, in which the process, unmoored from a body, wanders and is drawn by its habits toward a womb. Tibet took the northern view and made of it a literature, and the most famous item in that literature is a treasure text -- one of the Nyingma's hidden teachings -- discovered in the fourteenth century by a man named Karma Lingpa, and titled, properly, *Liberation through Hearing in the Intermediate State*. It is a book to be *read aloud to the dead*.

Here is what it says, and I shall walk it as the tradition walks it, because it is a procedure. At the moment of death, the winds of the body dissolve inward, and there dawns -- for everyone, saint or sinner -- the *clear light*: the mind in its naked nature, empty and luminous, the Buddha-nature of the seventh evening with nothing in front of it. If the dying person recognises it as *his own mind*, he is free, then and there; most do not, and faint. Then, over the following days, the reader at the corpse's side -- for the consciousness is held to linger, and to hear -- describes what the dead person is now seeing: first, for seven days, the *peaceful deities*, radiant Buddhas in brilliant light, terrifying in their brightness, beside which appear soft, dim, comfortable lights of the six realms; and the instruction, repeated, is *do not flee the brightness; do not go toward the comfortable dim light; recognise the deities as your own mind.* Then, for the next seven, the *wrathful deities*, the same Buddhas in their fierce forms, blood-drinking, flame-wreathed, filling the sky; and the instruction is the same: *these are your own projections; do not be afraid; recognise them.* And then, for those who have recognised nothing, the *bardo of becoming*: the winds of karma blowing the consciousness about like a feather, the visions of the six realms, the sight of one's own funeral, the terrible longing for a body, and at last the couples seen in union and the drawing toward a womb; and the book gives instructions even here, for choosing a good birth, and shutting the door of the womb, and being born, if one must, as a human near the teaching. Forty-nine days, and at every step the same sentence: *this is your own mind; recognise it and you are free.* The whole of Tibetan Buddhism is in that sentence, sir, and so, if you have been listening, is the whole of this course: the terrors are the three fires projected onto the sky, and the cure is to see that they are projections.

Now the West, because this book had a second life. In nineteen twenty-seven an American Theosophist named Evans-Wentz, who had bought a manuscript of it in Darjeeling and had it translated by a Sikkimese schoolmaster -- Evans-Wentz himself read no Tibetan -- published it in Oxford under a title he invented, borrowed from the Egyptian text of that name: *The Tibetan Book of the Dead*. Carl Jung wrote a psychological commentary for it and said he had carried it with him for years. In nineteen sixty-four three Harvard psychologists named Leary, Metzner, and Alpert rewrote it as a manual for guiding an LSD trip, on the theory that the bardo visions and the acid visions were the same thing, and the Beatles set a passage from that rewrite to music. And so a funeral manual of one school, one of hundreds of such manuals in a country that had a great many, became, in the West, *the* Tibetan book, and the whole of Tibet became, in the Western mind, the land of the *Book of the Dead*, of lost wisdom, of the secret in the mountains. A scholar named Lopez wrote a book about this in the nineteen-nineties called *Prisoners of Shangri-La*, and its argument is one I commend to you as the last evening approaches: that the Tibet the West loves is, in large part, a Tibet the West *made*, out of its own needs, and that the making has not always served the Tibetans. Which is not to say the book is not what I have told you it is. It is. It is simply not the only thing they have.

Part Four in a sentence: the interval between lives, which the elders denied, became in Tibet a forty-nine-day procedure read aloud to the dead -- *these visions are your own mind; recognise them* -- and an Oxford Theosophist's retitling of one such manual made it the West's Tibet, for better and for worse.

## Part Five -- North, and Nineteen Fifty-Nine

The last part is the road out: north over the steppe, to the empire that built the temple on the Neva; and then the year the roof came down.

Tibet's Buddhism went north with the Mongols. Altan Khan's conversion in fifteen seventy-eight was followed by the whole Mongol nation's, and within a century Mongolia was a country of monasteries, with its own line of reincarnate lamas at its head, and the Gelug's reliable army. And from the Mongols it went to the Mongol peoples inside Russia -- for the Russian empire, sir, expanding east across Siberia in the seventeenth century, acquired three Buddhist nations, and I should like to name them, because they are the Buddhists of Europe and nobody in the West knows they exist. The **Buryats**, east of Lake Baikal, Dorzhiev's people, who took the Gelug from Mongolia in the seventeen-hundreds and built, along the Selenga, the great *datsans* -- the Buryat word for a monastery -- that were the northernmost Buddhist universities on earth; the empress Elizabeth recognised their religion by decree in seventeen forty-one, and their chief lama, the *Pandito Khambo Lama*, was an officer of the Russian state. The **Tuvans**, in the mountains south of Siberia, who took it from Mongolia in the eighteenth century and were the last to join the Soviet Union, in nineteen forty-four. And the **Kalmyks**, who are the strangest case. They were a western Mongol people who, between sixteen hundred and nine and sixteen thirty-two, migrated three thousand miles west across the steppe and settled on the lower Volga, north of the Caspian -- in *Europe*, by every definition of the continent -- with their Tibetan Buddhism in their saddlebags, and have been there since: the only Buddhist people native to Europe, with their own monasteries on the Volga a century before Peter built Petersburg. Their capital, Elista, has today the largest Buddhist temple on the continent, opened in two thousand and five on a site the Dalai Lama chose.

And the empire produced scholars to match. Petersburg in the decades before the Revolution had the greatest school of Buddhist studies in the world, and its master was a man named Stcherbatsky -- *Shcherbatskoy*, properly -- who read Sanskrit, Tibetan, and Mongolian, worked with the Buryat lamas, sat on the building committee of Dorzhiev's temple, and wrote, between the wars, the two books that first made the Buddhist philosophers respectable in the West: *The Central Conception of Buddhism*, on the Abhidharma, and the two volumes of *Buddhist Logic*, on the Nalanda logicians, which argued, to the scandal of the professors of his day, that Dignaga and Dharmakirti were the equals of Kant. He died in nineteen forty-two, in evacuation, in Kazakhstan. The Petersburg school is a Russian gift to the field that the field has been slow to acknowledge, and I acknowledge it here.

Then the purges. What Stalin did to the Buddhists of the Soviet Union was, proportionally, the most thorough destruction of the religion since the Muslim conquest of India, and it is scarcely remembered. In Buryatia, every one of the forty-seven datsans was closed or razed by nineteen forty, and the lamas shot or sent to the camps. In Mongolia, a Soviet client, the seven hundred monasteries were destroyed in two years, nineteen thirty-seven to thirty-nine, and something like eighteen thousand lamas were killed. In Kalmykia the monasteries went the same way, and then, on the twenty-eighth of December nineteen forty-three, the *entire Kalmyk nation* -- more than ninety thousand people -- was loaded into cattle-trucks in a single operation and deported to Siberia, on a charge of collaboration with the Germans, and something like a third of them died on the journey or in the first winter; the survivors were allowed home in nineteen fifty-six. And Dorzhiev, who had built the temple and served the Tsar and the Dalai Lama, died in a prison hospital in nineteen thirty-eight at eighty-four. The tradition in Russia went underground, and survived, as it survives everything, in the hands of old women and hidden lamas, and one strange story, which I give you because sir will want it. Stalin, in nineteen forty-five, in a gesture to the Buryat soldiers who had fought for him, permitted one new datsan to be built, at a place called Ivolginsk, near Ulan-Ude; and it is there, in a glass case, that the Buryats keep the body of a lama named Itigilov, the head of their church, who in nineteen twenty-seven, at seventy-five, told his monks he was going to die, sat down in meditation, asked them to bury him sitting and to dig him up in thirty years, and died. They dug him up in nineteen fifty-five, and found him, they said, undecayed, and buried him again in salt; and in nineteen seventy-two, and again; and in September two thousand and two, with the leaders of the Russian sangha present, they exhumed him a third time, and the pathologists who examined the body reported it, in their words, *in the condition of someone who had died thirty-six hours ago.* I offer no explanation, sir. The Buryats offer one, and it is the diamond vehicle's: that he is not dead but in a meditation from which he has not yet chosen to return. Pilgrims come to Ivolginsk from all over Russia to see him. He sits in his robes with his head slightly bowed, and I am told that one does not laugh.

And Tibet. In nineteen fifty the army of the new People's Republic of China crossed the eastern border, and Tibet, which had never had an army worth the name and had trusted for seven centuries to the priest-and-patron arrangement, submitted. For nine years the arrangement held after a fashion. In March nineteen fifty-nine the people of Lhasa rose, fearing the Dalai Lama was to be seized, and the rising was put down with artillery, and the Dalai Lama, twenty-three years old, disguised as a soldier, rode out of the Potala by night and over the passes into India, where the Indian government gave him a hill town called Dharamsala, and where he has lived since. Some hundred thousand Tibetans followed him. And then, in the Cultural Revolution, the roof of the world was pulled down: of some six thousand monasteries in Tibet, all but a handful were destroyed, and the books burned, and the monks laicised, in a persecution beside which Wuzong's of eight hundred and forty-five looks like a tax audit. The library, once more, had to be carried out. And it was: by the refugees, in their packs, to India and Nepal; by the lamas who went west, of whom the last evening will speak; and by the Dalai Lama himself, who became, in exile, the most famous Buddhist in the world, and took the diamond vehicle, which had lived for a thousand years behind the highest mountains on earth and behind its own secrecy, and set it down in Paris and New York and, in two thousand and four, on the Volga. That is the fourth border, sir, seen whole: Tibet took the *entire* library and checked every term; it added the guru, the diamond road, the reborn lama, and the monk on the throne; and when the roof fell in, the neighbours -- this time, us -- had the copies.

## The Raft, So Far

The planks lashed on tonight, sir, said once more.

First: a Buryat monk who was tutor to the Dalai Lama and back channel to the Tsar caused a British invasion of Tibet and built a temple on the Neva, which stands, with its martyrs, at the northern end of tonight's road. Second: Tibet was converted by a scholar and a sorcerer who bound the old gods rather than breaking them, chose India over China in a debate at Samye, and after a dark age translated the whole Indian library against a fixed dictionary -- so that when India lost the books, Tibet had them. Third: the diamond vehicle is the Great Vehicle accelerated to one lifetime, by the guru, the empowerment, the mantra, the mandala, and the practice of being the deity; its highest tantras use the body and the passions as the road, mostly in symbol, and its saint is a murderer who sang in a cave. Fourth: four schools and one institution -- the reincarnate lama, found as a child by tests any parent would recognise -- put a monk on the throne of Tibet for three centuries under a Mongol title meaning *ocean*; and the interval between lives, denied by the elders, became a forty-nine-day procedure read aloud to the dead, whose one sentence is *these visions are your own mind*. Fifth: the teaching went north to the Mongols and to the three Buddhist nations of the Russian empire -- the only Buddhists native to Europe -- produced the Petersburg school, and was all but destroyed by Stalin and by the Cultural Revolution; and the library was carried out again, west, to us.

The one book tonight, and two roads, as usual. Sam van Schaik's *Tibet: A History*, which tells the whole story from the empire to the exile in four hundred honest pages and is the book to read before believing anything anyone tells you about Tibet. And for the Petersburg half of the evening, John Snelling's *Buddhism in Russia: The Story of Agvan Dorzhiev, Lhasa's Emissary to the Tsar*, which is the only book in English on the man, and which sir will find, I think, a chapter of his own country's history he was never told.

One account paid tonight -- the bardo, from the fourth evening -- and one carried to the last: the library carried west, and what the West has done with it.

Next week, then, sir, the last evening: Calcutta, eighteen thirty-seven, and an assayer at the mint reads, on pillar after pillar, the words of a king who calls himself Beloved of the Gods, and nobody knows who that is. The rediscovery of the Buddha by the people who had never heard of him; the half-million who came back to him on one afternoon; the mindfulness industry and what the evidence says; the scandals; the dark side; and then the far bank: the raft set down, the planks counted, and a verdict, which the persona under which I serve you obliges me to give.

Strive on with diligence, sir.

*-- End of Episode Nine.*
