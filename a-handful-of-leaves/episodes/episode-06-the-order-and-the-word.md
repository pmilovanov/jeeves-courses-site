# Episode Six -- The Order and the Word

*A Handful of Leaves -- An Introduction to Buddhism*

## The Last Meal

Good day, sir.

I have closed every evening of this course with a sentence that is not mine, and tonight I pay the debt. It is the last thing the man said, and to give it its weight I must give you the last year.

He was eighty. The scriptures preserve, in the long discourse that tells of his final journey, a remark he made to Ananda when a sickness had nearly killed him during the rains at Vesali and he had held it off by will: *I am old now, Ananda, worn out, full of years; my journey is done. I am like an old cart that is kept going only by being held together with straps.* He set out anyway, north and west, walking, with Ananda and a company of monks, through the villages of the Ganges country toward the hills where he had been born; the tradition reads the direction as a man going home, and I see no reason to argue. On the road there is a scene the scriptures tell against Ananda, gently: the Buddha remarks, three times, in three different ways, that one who has mastered the mind might, if asked, remain in the world for a very long time -- and Ananda, distracted, the text says, *by Mara*, does not take the hint and does not ask him to stay. And so the Buddha, alone, tells Mara -- who has turned up, as he does, to suggest that it is time -- that in three months he will go. Read Mara as you have read him all season: as the voice in the head that says *enough*. An old man, tired, with the strap-bound cart of his body failing, decided that it was time. That is the plain account, and the tradition's ornaments do not hide it.

At a village called Pava, a smith named Cunda invited him to a meal, and served a dish whose name has caused two thousand years of argument. The old word is *sukara-maddava*, and the first half of it means *pig*, and the second half means something like *soft* or *delicacy*: so, *pig's delight* -- either tender pork, which is what the oldest commentators thought and the plainest reading gives, or, as others have argued, something pigs delight in: a truffle, a mushroom, a root that pigs dig for. I cannot settle it, sir, and neither can anyone; I lean toward the pork and hold the truffle in reserve. What the text records is that the Buddha asked to be served the dish himself, and that the rest of it be buried in a pit, *for I see no one in the world who could digest it but the Tathagata* -- which is either a statement about the dish or a man who already knew what was coming and would not have others eat it. And he fell sick: the text is unsqueamish; it says *bloody*, and it says the pain was violent, and it says that he bore it, *mindful and clearly aware*, and got up, and told Ananda: *let us go to Kusinara.*

The walk is told in small mercies. He grew faint and asked for his robe to be folded so he could sit under a tree; he asked for water from a stream that had just been muddied by carts, and Ananda demurred, and the Buddha asked again, and Ananda went, and the stream, the text says, had cleared. A man of the Malla people named Pukkusa, passing, was so struck by his composure that he gave him two robes of cloth of gold, and Ananda, putting one on him, said that against the Buddha's skin the gold looked dull. And one more mercy, which I want you to hear because it is the man entire. He told Ananda that someone might later say to Cunda the smith: *it was your meal that killed him* -- and that Cunda's remorse must be removed. *Say to him: it is a gain for you, friend Cunda, that the Tathagata took his last meal from you. There are two offerings of food that bear equal fruit, greater than any other: the meal before the awakening, and the meal before the final passing.* A dying man, in pain, arranging that the cook should not blame himself. I find nothing in the biography more persuasive.

At Kusinara -- a small town; Ananda protested that a greater city would have been fitter, and was told to hush -- he lay down between two sal trees, on his right side, one foot on the other, and the trees, the text says, flowered out of season and dropped their blossoms on him. Ananda went into a hut and leaned against the door-post and wept: *I am still a learner, with work to do, and my teacher, who was so kind to me, is going.* And the Buddha sent for him and said the words I would have you keep beside the raft. *Enough, Ananda. Do not grieve. Have I not told you, from the beginning, that all that is dear to us must be parted from, must be left? How could it be, Ananda, that what is born, what has come to be, what is put together and must decay, should not decay? That cannot be. For a long time you have been in my presence, showing loving-kindness in body, speech, and mind, boundlessly and without stint. You have done well, Ananda. Make the effort, and you too will soon be free.* Then he sent for the Mallas of Kusinara, so that they might see him before the end; then he taught a last wanderer, named Subhadda, who came to the grove that night and was admitted, the last person the man himself received. Then he told the monks that when he was gone, *the teaching and the discipline that I have taught you will be your teacher*; and that they might, if they wished, abolish the minor rules -- and Ananda, distracted a second time, did not ask which rules were minor, and the order has been arguing about it ever since. And then he asked the monks, three times, whether any of them had a doubt or a question about the teaching, so that they should not afterwards say *we were face to face with the master and did not ask* -- and three times there was silence. And then he said the last thing.

*Vayadhamma sankhara. Appamadena sampadetha.* Two clauses. *All put-together things are of a nature to decay. Strive on with diligence.* And he passed, the text says, through the four absorptions and beyond them and back, and from the fourth absorption went out, like a lamp.

That is the sentence, sir. I have been saying it to you at the close of every evening since the first, and now you know whose it is and when he said it, and I hope it sounds different. The first clause is Kondanna's sentence from the Deer Park, spoken at the end as it was at the beginning -- *whatever arises, ceases* -- and the second is the whole of the treatment in two words. Tonight is about what the people around him did with those two words: how the order he left behind kept itself together without him, how it kept his *words* -- by reciting, for two centuries, before a line was written -- and how an emperor and an island carried both across the first border.

## Part One -- The Order

This part is about the sangha as an institution: what a monk actually was, and how the rules were made, and the two great troubles of the early order.

The word **sangha** means, simply, *assembly* -- the same word the Shakyas used for their council of elders -- and in its narrow sense it means the community of monks and nuns; in its wide sense, the four assemblies: monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen. It is the third refuge, and I want to say why. The Buddha did not found a church with a creed; he founded a *community with a discipline*, and the discipline is what kept the community recognisably itself for two and a half thousand years across a dozen cultures. A Buddhist monk in Bangkok and a Buddhist monk in Kyoto disagree about nearly everything; they both eat from a bowl, and both descend from the same code.

The code is called the **Vinaya** -- the *discipline* -- and its heart is a list of rules called the *Patimokkha*: two hundred and twenty-seven for monks, in the tradition of the elders, and somewhat more for nuns. Every fortnight, at the full and new moon, the monks of a district assemble and one of them recites the whole list aloud, and after each section he asks, three times, whether any monk present has broken a rule in it; and a monk who has confesses. That fortnightly recitation is the sangha's heartbeat, and it has not stopped since the fifth century before the common era. The rules are graded: at the top, four **defeats** -- sexual intercourse; theft of anything of value; killing a human being; and falsely claiming a spiritual attainment -- for which the penalty is expulsion, and the tradition's image is of a palm tree cut through: it cannot be rejoined. Below them, offences requiring a formal meeting, and below those, offences requiring confession or forfeiture -- of a robe wrongly acquired, say -- and at the bottom a great many rules of *training*: how to walk, how to eat, not to laugh loudly in a village, not to teach a person who is wearing shoes.

What I most want you to notice about the code, sir, is that it is *case law*. Every one of the rules has a story attached, in the Vinaya itself, of the incident that occasioned it: a monk did such-and-such; the laity complained, or another monk complained; the matter was brought to the Buddha; he questioned the monk; and he laid down the rule *for the future* -- and, characteristically, the monk who committed the first offence is not punished by it, because there was no rule yet. The very first rule -- the first defeat -- has the longest story, and I shall tell it because it is about a family. A monk named Sudinna, of a good house, had gone forth against his parents' wishes. In a famine he went home for alms, and his mother saw her chance: the family's wealth would go to the king, she said, if there was no heir; and she prevailed on him, monk though he was, to lie once with his former wife and produce one. He did, and the wife bore a son, and the tradition, with a pleasure I share, says the boy was nicknamed *Seed*. And Sudinna was wretched afterward and confessed, and the Buddha rebuked him in terms I shall soften only slightly -- it would have been better, he said, to have put the organ in question into the mouth of a poisonous snake -- and laid down the rule. Every rule in the code is a Sudinna: an actual person, an actual mess, a ruling. The order was not designed. It was *litigated into existence*, one case at a time, by a founder who preferred to wait for the problem.

Three more things make a monk, and I shall give them a sentence each. The **almsround**: every morning, before noon, a monk walks through the village with his bowl, and the laity put food in it, and he eats once, before midday, and not again until the next morning; he may not cook, may not store, may not choose. The **rains retreat**: for the three months of the monsoon the wandering stops -- the rule was made, the Vinaya says, because the laity complained that monks walking in the rains trampled the young crops and the small creatures -- and the monks settle in one place, and the rains-shelters became, over a few generations, the *monasteries*, the first fixed institutions of the religion. And **poverty**: a monk owns his robes, his bowl, a razor, a needle, a water-strainer, and a belt, and may not handle gold and silver -- which the tradition understood, correctly, to mean *money* -- a rule that, as we shall see, nearly split the order a century after the founder's death, and was argued over in Japan a thousand years later, and is argued over now.

Now the first of the two troubles, and it is the nuns. The Buddha's aunt and foster-mother, Mahapajapati -- the woman who had raised him after his mother died in his first week -- came to him after his father's death and asked to be allowed to go forth, and he refused; and refused again; and she cut her hair and put on the robe and walked, with five hundred Shakya women, barefoot, the long road to Vesali, and stood outside the gate in the dust, weeping. And it was Ananda -- Ananda again -- who went in and pleaded, and, being refused, asked the question that turned it: *Venerable sir, are women capable of attaining the fruits of the path -- of becoming stream-enterers, once-returners, non-returners, arahants?* And the Buddha said: they are. *Then, venerable sir, since they are capable* -- and the order of nuns was founded, on eight *heavy rules*, the first of which is that a nun of a hundred years' standing must rise and bow to a monk ordained that day; and the text adds a prophecy, in the Buddha's mouth, that because women had been admitted the teaching would last five hundred years instead of a thousand. I owe you the scholars on this, and they are firm. The story is told in several versions that disagree with each other; the eight heavy rules look, to a philologist, like a later insertion; and the prophecy is very hard to square with the Buddha's own repeated statement, elsewhere, that his teaching would be complete only when it had monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen who were all accomplished. Analayo, the most careful modern scholar of the question, argues that the misogyny in the passage belongs to the men who transmitted it rather than to the man who founded the order. What is not in doubt is that the order of nuns existed, that it produced the oldest collection of women's poetry in the world -- the verses of the elder nuns, which you met in Kisa Gotami's evening -- and that it died out in the lands of the elders in the eleventh century, and was revived, amid great controversy, only at the end of the twentieth. Where the Buddha stood in all this I cannot tell you for certain. That he said women could attain the goal, and admitted them, against the whole grain of his age, I can.

The second trouble is a man, and his name is Devadatta. He was the Buddha's cousin, and he was, by every account, gifted, and ambitious, and late in the Buddha's life he asked to be given the leadership of the order, on the grounds that the master was old. He was refused, in public, in words that stung. And the tradition then makes him a villain of the deepest dye: he hired archers, who were converted on the spot; he rolled a boulder down a hill, which grazed the Buddha's foot; he loosed a drunken war-elephant in a street, which the Buddha calmed with loving-kindness; and at last he proposed a *reform* -- five rules he wanted made compulsory for all monks: to live only in the forest, to eat only alms, to wear only rag-robes, to sleep only under trees, to eat no fish or meat. The Buddha refused to make them compulsory -- any monk might follow them who wished, he said, but the middle way did not require them -- and Devadatta, calling this laxity, led five hundred monks away to found a stricter order, and was, the legend says, swallowed by the earth. Now the historian's footnote, which I find one of the most striking things in this evening. When Chinese pilgrims walked through India in the fifth and again in the seventh century of the common era -- eight hundred and a thousand years after these events -- they reported communities of monks who still followed *Devadatta's* rule, honouring the Buddhas of the past but not this one, and refusing milk. He had not been swallowed by the earth. He had founded something, and it lasted a millennium, and the tradition that won wrote the story. I tell you this not to rehabilitate a boulder-roller but because it is the first instance of a pattern you will see on every border the teaching crosses: the *rigorists* and the *middle way*, the men who think the founder was too soft and the founder's own insistence that he was not.

Part One in a sentence: the sangha is a community with a discipline, its code litigated into existence one case at a time and recited every fortnight since; it admitted women, against its age and amid a text later hands made ugly; and its first schism was a rigorist's, and lasted a thousand years.

## Part Two -- The Councils, and the Reciting

This part is about how the words survived the man, which they did by being *said*, together, for two hundred years.

When the Buddha died, the tradition says, the Mallas of Kusinara cremated him with the honours of a wheel-turning king, and the ashes and bones were claimed by eight peoples, who came near to fighting over them until a brahmin named Dona divided the relics into eight equal parts, and each built a mound over its share -- the first **stupas**, the ancestors of every pagoda in Asia; and the box that a British estate manager dug out of a mound at Piprahwa, which you heard of in the first evening, is, if the inscription means what it seems to, one of those eight. And the order, which had been held together by a person, had to discover what would hold it together now.

Announce the cast, sir, before anything moves, because this is a story with several actors. **Mahakassapa**, the senior monk, austere, the one who had not been at the deathbed and arrived late; **Ananda**, the attendant, with the memory; **Upali**, a former barber, who had specialised in the rules; and five hundred others, all of them, by the tradition's count, arahants -- finished, awake. Mahakassapa had a reason for haste. On the road to Kusinara, when the news of the death reached his party, an old monk named Subhadda -- a different Subhadda from the last convert -- had said, in effect: *good; now we are rid of the old man telling us what to do and what not to do; now we shall do as we please.* And Mahakassapa, hearing that, resolved that the teaching and the discipline must be *fixed*, at once, before the doing-as-we-pleased could begin.

So, the tradition says, in the rains after the death, five hundred monks assembled at Rajagaha, in a cave in the hills, and *recited*. This is the **First Council**, and the word *council* misleads; nothing was debated. Upali was asked, rule by rule, *where was this rule laid down, on whose account, and what was the offence?* -- and he recited the whole of the discipline. And Ananda was asked, discourse by discourse, *where was this taught, and to whom?* -- and he recited the whole of the teaching, which is why every discourse in the canon begins with the same four words in Ananda's voice: *Thus have I heard.* Each recitation was then repeated by the assembly, in chorus, and thereby became the *agreed* text. There is a small drama attached, which the tradition tells with relish. Ananda was not yet an arahant, and the council was for arahants only; and on the night before it he strove all night and failed, and toward dawn, giving up, lay down to rest -- and, the legend says, attained awakening in the instant between lifting his feet from the floor and laying his head on the pillow, neither sitting nor standing nor lying down, which the commentators enjoy for its neatness. And then, admitted, he was *tried*. The elders charged him with faults, and I shall give them, because they are so wonderfully human: that he had not asked the Buddha which the minor rules were; that he had once stepped on the Buddha's robe while sewing it; that he had let women see the body first, and their tears had stained its feet; that he had not asked the Buddha to remain alive for an age when the hint was given; and that he had pleaded for the admission of women. And Ananda answered each: he did not see a fault in any of it, but out of respect for the elders he would confess to them all. And they let it stand.

I owe you the historian's view, and it is this: the First Council as described almost certainly did not happen as described -- five hundred arahants reciting the whole of a canon that, in its present form, took centuries to assemble, is a foundation story. Something like it, a gathering of the senior disciples to agree what the master had taught, very probably did. And the *method* it describes -- communal recitation, the agreed text carried in chorus -- is exactly how the teaching was in fact preserved, for something like four hundred years, before a word of it was written, and that is the part I want you to understand, because it is a piece of engineering.

Consider the problem. A body of teaching the size of a small library, in a language that was not yet written down for such purposes, to be carried across generations by human memory alone, in a country where the founder's own words were the only authority. How? The order solved it with specialists and with structure. The specialists were the **reciters** -- monks who took responsibility for one collection each: the reciters of the Long Discourses, of the Middle-Length, of the Connected, of the Numerical -- and who chanted their collection together, regularly, as a guild. And the structure is the thing you will have noticed if you have ever read a discourse and wondered why it repeats itself so much. The texts are built of *formulas*: fixed phrases, a few hundred of them, that recur verbatim -- the description of a monk entering the first absorption is the same words in every discourse in which it occurs; the list of the five heaps is the same words; the four-line law of dependent origination is the same words. And the discourses are built of *lists* -- four truths, five heaps, six doors, seven factors, eight factors, twelve links -- and every list is numbered, and the number is a check on the count. Sir, you will recognise the design. Repetition is redundancy; a fixed formula chanted by a hundred monks at once is a text with a hundred copies, checked against each other at every recitation, in which a single monk's error is drowned out by the chorus and corrected on the spot; and the numbered list is a checksum. The teaching survived the way the young master will learn his first songs -- by repetition, with others, the tune carrying the words -- and for the same reason: because that is the most robust storage a human community has. The best evidence that it worked is that when the Pali texts of Sri Lanka are set beside the versions that went north and were translated into Chinese, from a line of transmission that separated from the southern one within a century or two of the Buddha's death, the core of the discourses agrees, formula for formula and list for list, across a thousand miles and a thousand years. Two copies, from independent lines, agreeing: it is the best kind of evidence there is, and two modern monks, Sujato and Brahmali, have written a whole book making exactly that argument, and it is the book of tonight.

One more council, and the first crack. A century after the death, the tradition says -- and the historians, for once, largely agree that this one happened -- monks from the Vajjian country around Vesali were found to be practising **ten points** that the strict regarded as unlawful. Most were trivial: storing salt in a horn; eating a little after noon, while the sun's shadow was still within two fingers' breadth; drinking unfermented palm-toddy; a rug of the wrong size. But the tenth was *accepting gold and silver* -- money -- and that was not trivial; that was the poverty of the order. Seven hundred monks assembled, and the elders, questioned one by one, ruled all ten points unlawful, and the Vajjian monks were censured; and the tradition connects that ruling with the first great division of the order, into the *elders* -- the *Sthaviras*, from whom the tradition of the elders that this evening ends in takes its name -- and the *great assembly*, the *Mahasanghikas*, who were, the historians think, the larger party and probably split a little later over a different question, about how perfect a finished person is. I mark it contested and move on; the point is that within a century of his death the order had split, and within a few more it had split into eighteen schools, and the schools disagreed about the exact words, and the words were still not written down.

Part Two in a sentence: the order kept the teaching for centuries by *reciting* it together, in formulas and numbered lists, which is redundancy and a checksum -- so that the southern and northern copies, separated early, still agree; the First Council is a foundation story of that method, the Second a real quarrel about money, and the first schism followed.

## Part Three -- The Emperor

This part is about the man who turned a sect of the Ganges country into a religion of Asia, and the war that turned him.

His name was Ashoka, and he ruled the Mauryan empire, the first to cover most of India, from about two hundred and sixty-eight before the common era, some hundred and thirty years after the Buddha's death by the scholars' count. In roughly the eighth year of his reign he conquered the kingdom of Kalinga, on the east coast, and what he did afterward is unique in the history of kings: he *published his remorse*, cut into rock, in the language of the people, at sites across his empire, for anyone to read. The inscription -- his Thirteenth Rock Edict -- says that a hundred and fifty thousand people were carried off from Kalinga, that a hundred thousand were killed, and that many times that number died; and that *the Beloved of the Gods*, as he calls himself, *feels remorse*, and that the slaughter and death and carrying-off of the people is *a matter of profound sorrow and regret to him*. And it goes on to say that what he now regards as the only real conquest is *conquest by dharma*.

What Ashoka meant by *dharma* is a question the scholars enjoy, because his edicts are not, for the most part, Buddhist in content: they preach non-injury to living things, respect for parents and elders and for ascetics of *every* sect, tolerance between sects, care of the sick, the planting of shade trees and the digging of wells along the roads, and a general moral seriousness that a Jain or a brahmin could have signed. But some of them are unmistakably Buddhist. One, at a place called Bhabru, addresses the sangha directly and recommends seven passages of scripture by name that monks and laity should study. One, on a pillar at Sanchi, warns that any monk or nun who *divides the sangha* is to be put out in white clothes -- the emperor, that is, taking sides in Vesali's quarrel. And one, on a pillar at Lumbini, you met in the first evening: *here the Buddha, the sage of the Shakyas, was born.* He went there himself. The Buddhist chronicles of Sri Lanka make him more than a patron: they say he convened a **Third Council**, at his capital, to purge the order of impostors who had joined it for the good food; that the council's president compiled a book refuting the errors of the other schools; and that the emperor then sent *missions* to the ends of the known world. Northern Buddhists know nothing of this council, and the scholars suspect the Sri Lankan chroniclers of giving their own school the emperor's blessing; I mark it. But the missions, or something like them, are in Ashoka's own rock. The Thirteenth Edict names the Greek kings to whom he sent envoys of dharma -- Antiochus in Syria, Ptolemy in Egypt, Antigonus in Macedon, Magas in Cyrene, Alexander in Epirus -- which is to say that the first foreign policy of Buddhism was directed at the successors of Alexander the Great, two centuries before the Greek king of the Punjab took a chariot apart with a monk.

And the mission that mattered went south. The chronicles say that Ashoka's own son, a monk named Mahinda, sailed to the island of Sri Lanka and found its king, Devanampiya Tissa, out hunting on a hill called Mihintale, and taught him there, and converted him, and with him the island; and that Ashoka's daughter, the nun Sanghamitta, followed, bringing with her a cutting of the tree from Bodh Gaya, which was planted at the capital, Anuradhapura, and is, as I told you in the first evening, still there. Whatever the truth of the hunting party, Buddhism reached Sri Lanka in Ashoka's reign and never left, and that is the fact on which the rest of this evening turns.

There is an irony in Ashoka's afterlife that I cannot leave out. In India he was *forgotten*. The script of his edicts fell out of use; the inscriptions stood on their pillars for two thousand years with nobody able to read them; the Hindu tradition barely remembered his name; and he was rediscovered only in eighteen thirty-seven, when an English assayer at the Calcutta mint cracked the alphabet and read *Beloved of the Gods* on pillar after pillar and did not know who that was -- a story I am saving for the last evening, because it is where the West's Buddhism begins. But in Sri Lanka he was never forgotten. The chronicles kept him. The island that received his mission remembered the emperor when his own country had lost him, which is the first instance of a second pattern you will see at every border: *the teaching, once it crosses, keeps what the homeland loses.*

Part Three in a sentence: an emperor sickened by his own war published his remorse on rock, preached a tolerant dharma, patronised the order, and sent missions abroad -- the one to Sri Lanka took, and Sri Lanka remembered him when India forgot.

## Part Four -- The Word Written Down

This part is about the first border, and what was kept on the far side of it.

For some two hundred years after Mahinda, the island's monks did what the mainland's did: they recited. And then, in the first century before the common era, in the reign of a king named Vattagamani Abhaya, two things came at once: an invasion from South India that drove the king from his throne for fourteen years, and a famine, which the chronicles say lasted twelve, and which killed monks in numbers. The reciters were dying, and each reciter who died took a collection with him. And so the elders did the thing that, for four hundred years, nobody had done. They met, the chronicle says five hundred of them, at a cave monastery called Aluvihara, in the hills near Matale, and *wrote it down*, on palm leaves, the whole of it, *in order that the doctrine might endure*. Those are the chronicle's words, and I give them to you as the founding sentence of the written Buddhism that every later evening of this course depends on. Everything I have quoted to you from the discourses -- the raft, the arrow, the dipper, the last words -- reached me because a monastery in the hills of Sri Lanka, in a famine, decided that the chorus was no longer safe and the leaf was.

What they wrote down has a shape, and the shape is worth a minute, because you will hear its name in every Buddhist country. It is the **Tipitaka** -- *the three baskets*, so called because the palm-leaf manuscripts were kept in baskets. The first basket is the **Vinaya**, the discipline, with its rules and their case histories. The second is the **Sutta** basket, the discourses, arranged in five collections: the *Long* discourses, the *Middle-Length*, the *Connected*, grouped by topic, the *Numerical*, grouped by the number of items in their lists -- the reciters' filing system made permanent -- and the *Minor* collection, which is not minor at all and contains the Dhammapada and the poems of the elder monks and nuns. And the third basket is the **Abhidhamma** -- the *higher teaching* -- seven books that are not discourses at all but *analysis*: the teaching taken apart and reassembled as a system. The whole runs, in the standard printed edition, to some forty volumes, and it is written in the language the tradition calls **Pali** -- which is not, I should say, the dialect the Buddha spoke; it is a literary cousin of it, the church language of the elders, near enough to his speech that the tradition has always felt entitled to call the texts his words.

Let me give the third basket one paragraph, because it is the part of the canon a man in your trade would find most congenial, and because it is where something you heard in the third evening hardened. The Abhidhamma is the attempt to describe *everything that happens in a mind* as a sequence of discrete events. Its basic unit is the *mind-moment* -- a single flash of consciousness, arising, doing its work, ceasing -- of which it catalogues eighty-nine kinds; and it lays out the *process* by which a sense-impression is received, examined, determined, run through a series of active moments in which the karma is made, and registered, in a fixed order, seventeen mind-moments to one moment of matter. It is, without exaggeration, a state machine of consciousness, drawn in the second century before the common era by people who had spent their lives watching their own minds in the absorptions, and it is the source of the tradition's habit of treating the person as a *stream* of events rather than a thing. It is also -- I promised you this in the evening on the self -- where the founder's strategic silence on the self became a doctrine that there is none: the Abhidhamma's world has events and no owner, and Buddhaghosa's *suffering but no sufferer* is its motto. The system is magnificent and it is the tradition's, not necessarily the man's. Hold both.

Buddhaghosa himself is the last name in this part. He was an Indian monk who came to Sri Lanka in the fifth century of the common era to do a work of *rescue*: the old commentaries on the canon had been composed in the island's own language, Sinhala, and were unreadable to the rest of the Buddhist world, and he translated them back into Pali, and then wrote, as a summary of the whole tradition's practice, a single vast book called the *Path of Purification* -- the *Visuddhimagga* -- which is, to this day, the standard manual of the school. The map of the stages of insight I mentioned last week, with its *fear* and *misery* and *disgust*, is his; so is the three-lives reading of the wheel. If you meet a teacher of the southern school anywhere on earth, sir, you are meeting Buddhaghosa's arrangement of the material, whether the teacher knows it or not.

And so the school. The tradition that kept the Pali canon calls itself **Theravada**, *the doctrine of the elders* -- the elders of the Second Council, whose side it takes -- and it is the oldest continuous Buddhist tradition on earth. From Sri Lanka it spread, over the next millennium, to Burma, whose king took it up in the eleventh century; to Thailand, in the thirteenth; and to Cambodia and Laos after; and those five countries are its home today, with something over a hundred million adherents. If you go to any of them you will find the same things: monks in ochre or saffron, eating once before noon from a bowl; the morning almsround through the streets, with the laity kneeling to give; the fortnightly recitation; the rains retreat, during which the young men of Thailand still, many of them, ordain for three months as a rite of passage; the observance days; and in the forests, a minority of monks who meditate, in the tradition of the forest, and whom the laity regard as the tradition's saints. It is a religion of *merit* for the many and *practice* for the few, exactly as the graduated talk laid it out; and it regards itself, not without reason, as the keeper of the original.

Part Four in a sentence: in a famine, five hundred monks wrote the whole teaching on palm leaves *so that it might endure*; the three baskets are the discipline, the discourses, and the systematic analysis that made the stream of mind-moments a doctrine; and the school that kept them, the elders', is the oldest alive.

## Part Five -- The First Border

The last part is short, and it opens the account on which the remaining four evenings run.

I said that every time the teaching crosses a border it changes, and that the change is the engine of the rest of the course; here is the first crossing, examined. What did Sri Lanka *keep*? It kept the discipline, whole -- the code, the almsround, the rains; it kept the discourses, whole, and wrote them down, and by writing them fixed them, so that a monk in Bangkok chants the same Pali today; it kept the emperor, when India lost him; and it kept the founder's *middle way* against the rigorists, whom it censured at Vesali and remembered as heretics. And what did it *add*? It added a *state*: the king as the sangha's protector and, by the chronicles' reckoning, its purifier, a Buddhism in which throne and order lean on each other -- an arrangement the founder, who had walked out of a chieftain's house, never asked for and which would have surprised him. It added the *chronicle*: history as a religious act, the island's story told as the story of the teaching's keeping. And it added the *merit economy* at national scale, the giving that built Anuradhapura's enormous stupas, which stand, with the tree, still. The plank it kept was the code and the word; the plank it lashed on was the throne. Watch for the same two motions at every border after this: something kept whole, and something the founder never mentioned added beside it.

Because, sir, while the island was writing the old words down, something was happening back in India that no emperor's council had foreseen. In the monasteries of the mainland, in the last century before the common era and the first after, new discourses were beginning to appear -- discourses that claimed to be the Buddha's words too, that nobody at Rajagaha had recited, and that said things the three baskets did not say: that the goal of the finished monk was too small; that the true aim was to become a Buddha oneself, for the sake of all beings; and, in their most famous line, that *form is emptiness, and emptiness is form*. Their authors called their way the *Great Vehicle*. Next week we take it.

## The Raft, So Far

The planks lashed on tonight, sir, said once more.

First: the sentence with which I close every evening is the last thing the man said -- *all put-together things decay; strive on with diligence* -- spoken between two sal trees, after a meal he took care the cook should not be blamed for, to an attendant he had just thanked for twenty-five years of loving-kindness. Second: the order is a community with a discipline, the code litigated into existence one case at a time and recited every fortnight since; it admitted women, against its age, in a text later hands spoiled; and its first schism was a rigorist's, and lasted a thousand years. Third: the words survived the man by being *recited* together in formulas and numbered lists -- redundancy and a checksum -- so that the southern and northern copies, separated early, agree; and a quarrel about money split the order within a century. Fourth: an emperor sickened by his own war published his remorse on rock and sent the teaching abroad, and the island that received it remembered him when his own country forgot. Fifth: in a famine the island wrote the whole teaching down *so that it might endure*, in three baskets -- discipline, discourses, and the analysis that made the mind a stream of moments -- and the school that kept them, the elders', is the oldest alive; and at this first border the teaching kept its code and its word and lashed on a throne.

The one book tonight, and it is free: Bhikkhu Sujato and Bhikkhu Brahmali's *The Authenticity of the Early Buddhist Texts*, a short monograph you may download in an afternoon, which lays out, with a lawyer's patience, why the scholars think the oldest layer of the canon really does go back to the man -- the reciters, the formulas, the northern and southern copies compared -- and which will let you weigh for yourself how much of the last six evenings is testimony and how much is reconstruction.

One account closed tonight, the oldest on the page: the sign-off. One opened: the border, and what each crossing keeps. Next week, then, sir: a cave in the Chinese desert, a scroll dated to the ninth century that is the oldest printed book in the world, and inside it a scripture in which the Buddha says he never taught a thing. The Great Vehicle; the bodhisattva; emptiness; and a monk named Nagarjuna who argued that the wheel of last week and the emptiness of next week are the same thing seen from two sides.

Strive on with diligence, sir.
